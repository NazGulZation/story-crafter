# Chapter 1: The Goddess's Notebook

You die the way you lived: in the middle of a calculation.

The customs dock was wet with a three-day rain, and the crate — the one with the iron straps that the crane foreman swore were "good enough for another season, maybe two" — chose the exact moment you were standing beneath it to remember it had a thousand-year-old fastener and a twenty-year-old memory of being raised. You had time to think *seven thousand two hundred units, insured at eighty percent, hold the line on the claim* before the world ended. Which is why, when you open your eyes on a ceiling that does not belong to any warehouse you know, the first thing you do is not scream.

The first thing you do is count the coffers stacked against the far wall. Forty-three. Each banded in brass. Each with a wax seal the color of old gold.

"Gently," says a woman's voice. "You'll frighten the sums."

She is sitting at the ledger desk at the center of the room — bronze hair in a loose tail, robes the color of honey, a quill in one hand and a stack of life-records in the other. The Goddess of Fortune, Eris, patron of the freshly dead and the unwisely looted. She looks you over with the patient resignation of a woman who has read your entire tax history and does not consider it a compliment.

"You died in service to freight," she says. "It happens to brokers more than the guilds like to admit."

"Am I to be judged?"

"Judged?" She turns a page. "No. The afterlife in this world runs on a different currency. You've been reincarnated, you see — provisionally. The paperwork hasn't been stamped. You get one thing before you go, one carried advantage, and then you will wake up somewhere wet and loud and call it Axel."

You remember the crate. You remember the failed strap, the exact sound of rivets popping, the weight of seven thousand two hundred units descending. You remember — and this is what you tell her — that you died without finishing the sum. The account was left open. Half a decimal unclosed.

"Memories," you say. "I want them back. All of them. Everything with a number in it — faces, prices, promises, interest, the lot."

The Goddess of Fortune looks at you for a long moment, and then she laughs — a small, dry, delighted laugh — and stamps your record. "A broker who remembers everything." She writes something in a margin. "Do you know how dangerous that is? On the other side of that door, the merchants will weep." She hands you a thin purse. "Enough for a registration fee, if you're clever. Don't spend it on ale."

---

Axel smells like wet wool, woodsmoke, and a bakery that knows exactly what it is doing. It is a beginner town, which is to say it is a town of loud, excited, freshly-reincarnated idiots with swords — and you walk through the middle of all of them toward the Adventurer's Guild with a perfect memory and a thin purse, thinking in clauses.

The guild hall is wide and loud: a quest board drowning in paper, a tavern counter with some kind of skewered monster-thing turning on a spit that smells better than it sounds, a card-crystal machine that lights up as adventurers verify the monsters they've killed. And behind the main counter — the counter everyone fails to complain to properly because she is busy being beautiful — Luna.

You clock her the way you clock everything: once, deliberately. Blonde hair in a high bun, held by a burgundy band, with one stubborn tuft fighting its way out of the top. Two thick bangs, orange at the tips, framing a face with gold-colored eyes. A low-cut white top with a red bow at the throat, three orange toggles, a gold star pinned to the right sleeve. Short blue shorts. Brown boots. She has the strained, upright patience of a woman who has filed complaints about adventurers who set their own beds on fire and is eleven working days from her last day off.

You approach the counter. "Registration."

"Do you have a name?" She picks up a card and a quill.

"Arith," you say. "With an inscription."

"I'll believe the inscription when I see it." She passes the card across. "A thousand Eris, coin up front, the guild holds the record."

You look at your thin purse. You look at the interest that will accrue — you can already feel the numbers arranging themselves — and then you look at her, and you give her your best offer. "No coin. But I have a promise, and I know the exact weight of one."

Luna sets down her quill. Gold eyes narrow. "The guild does not normally extend credit to the freshly dead."

"Then I'll be the first debtor it doesn't regret." You write it out on the back of your own card — the note, the principal, the interest, the date, the signature — and hand it back. "One day. I'll clear the mill myself, if I have to."

She reads it twice. Then — slowly, the way a woman who has been burned twelve times by adventurers reads anything with a number on it — she stamps it, and the card-crystal machine makes a soft, satisfied sound.

"Arith. In good standing." She taps her pen against the countertop, once. "Try not to make me regret this. I keep impeccable records."

"I'm counting on it," you say, and mean something she will only understand later.

---

The guild gives you a quest slip the color of a beer label, and the slip gives you a gift. There are farm carts at the east gate selling medicinal herbs at harvest prices — and the guild apothecary, two streets over, buys the same herbs at a markup so large it should be illegal, because adventurers keep setting themselves on fire and eating cursed mushrooms. You buy low at the gate and sell high through the apothecary's back door. The margin, when you tally it, is exactly enough to settle the registration note's principal.

Then you go to the Funnel Mill — Ostrow's mill, at the edge of town, where the grain funnel has hosted a family of goblin scavengers for four days and the miller is losing entire loads of flour to their digging. The quest: clear them, register the kills on your card.

You do not charge in with a sword. A broker doesn't charge anywhere with a sword. You circle the mill, find where the scavengers have tunneled a den under the drain sluice, and — gently, methodically — bait them out with a string of salted fish stolen from your own evening rations. They follow the smell into the funnel chute. You drop the hatch, which is twelve times their strength and the miller's entire reason for posting the quest in the first place. The vermin, caught, are finished quickly and efficiently.

Ostrow the miller pays you in small Eris coins, eyeing you like a man who has just watched a ledger clerk out-clever four goblins without raising his voice.

"Where'd they teach you that?" he asks.

"Warehouse insurance," you say. "You'd be amazed what a broker learns waiting for claims to pay out."

---

That evening you walk back into the guild hall with the smell of toad-skewer in the air and the last of the payment in your pocket, and there is Luna at the counter, closing the books for the night. You set your adventurer card in front of her, fill it with the tally of your kills — the goblin count, the mill, the miller's signature — and then you ask her, because the night is young and you are very new to this world and your perfect memory has already told you she has nowhere to be and nobody waiting.

"Dinner," you say. "At the tavern table. I'm buying."

Luna looks at you — gold eyes, the small professional smile, the exhausted patience of the town's most overworked pretty woman — and something in her posture changes. "You cleared the mill. Today. On a note." She says it almost to herself, counting the hours. "Most adventurers would have burned the note and skipped town before supper."

"Most adventurers don't remember the principal."

She puts her quill down. Takes off her card-holder armband, folds it into her sleeve, and says — "Then yes. One skewer. My feet know how late it is."

The tavern's toad skewers are better than the smell that advertised them: charred, peppered, dripping with whatever the guild cooks do to make monster taste like a reason to live. You split the bill evenly because she insists (she is, you learn, pathologically fair — "a fair split is cheaper than a debt of gratitude"), and she talks, and you remember everything she says, which is something no man in this town has ever done for her.

At the apothecary — the one you've already profited from — you buy three waxed-foil packets, folded, discreet, because even in a beginner town a man with a perfect memory knows the price of respect. You tuck them into the inner pocket of your jacket.

---

Bell Street at the end of the lane, under the bakery sign that no longer lures customers at this hour. Luna's door is heavy, painted the color of a dried rose. You walk her to the step, release her hand, and start to say goodnight — she will not let you finish.

She steps into you. One hand comes up to your collar, pulls you down a half-inch, and kisses you — decisive, dry, then less dry as she decides the ledger is open. It tastes of pepper and the sweet wine she insisted was her share of the bill. When she pulls back, there is a pleased, slightly dangerous look in her gold eyes, the look of a woman who has just read every numeral on your face and found the sum favorable.

"Tomorrow," she says. "After my shift. My flat. I keep impeccable records — the closed kind, and the other kind."

It is only later, walking away, that you reach into your jacket and find the pocket lighter than it should be. Three packets, bought and folded. A coin's worth of foil, gone.

Somewhere behind you, Luna's fingers — the ones that catalogue quest slips, card-crystal chimes, and the exact interest rate on a broker named Arith — are closing around the packet she lifted from your pocket, quick and secret and pleased.

She keeps impeccable records, all right.

You're going to make her keep you.
