# The Vermillion Gate

The cinnabar paint on the timber gates smelled of ground cedar dust, linseed oil, and old iron rust baked under the afternoon sun. Over two thousand vermillion arches climbed the steep slopes of Inari Mountain, their upright pillars jammed so closely together that the July daylight filtered through the gaps in thin orange ribs, striping the damp flagstones like the ribcage of an immense sleeping serpent.

Chinatsu Ibuki walked three paces ahead of the tour group, her low-heeled leather flats clicking a steady, dry rhythm against the granite risers. Her navy linen blouse was buttoned straight to the throat, crisp and immaculate despite the ninety percent humidity rolling off the swampy bamboo ravines below. A single strand of raven hair had slipped from her grandmother’s brass hairpin, its amber undertone catching the slant of cinnabar light each time she turned to check our pace.

"Most guidebooks tell you these arches were raised by pious emperors seeking divine favor for their harvests," she said, projecting her voice easily over the drone of cicadas sawing in the cryptomeria trees. Her voice was cool, melodious, accented with the round vowels of Edinburgh where she had spent her third university year studying historical topography. "That is the romantic version invented for postcards. In truth, every timber you see here was paid for by commercial guilds—rice syndicates from Osaka, sake distillers from Nada, timber factors trying to secure their river barges against summer floods. If you turn your eyes to the left upright of any pillar, you will see the merchant’s commercial house name and the date carved into the wood. Religion in Kyoto has always balanced on the sharp edge of an invoice."

The Australian couple behind us nodded vaguely, fanning their red faces with plastic convenience-store fans, while the German student beside them aimed a telephoto lens at a bronze lantern.

I stayed beside her, close enough that the scent of roasted hojicha and citrus sunscreen drifted off her collar. We had been climbing for forty minutes since the main lower courtyard, and with every hundred stone steps, the physical space between us had steadily shrunk.

"A pragmatist's shrine, then," I remarked as we reached a brief plateau where the path widened.

Her dark eyes cut toward me, sharp and amused. "A survivor's shrine," she corrected, tapping the pine pillar with the knuckle of her forefinger. The brass of her hairpin caught the orange reflection of the pillars. "Kyoto burned to ash four times between the fourteenth and eighteenth centuries. The shrines endured because the merchants kept rebuilding the roof timbers over the gods' heads. Ideals crumble when winter damp sets in. Grain credit endures."

We had met at nine that morning beneath the massive steel canopy of Kyoto Station. While the other tour guides gathered their groups with brightly colored flags and plastic megaphones, Chinatsu had simply stood beside the northern information pillar holding a narrow wooden clip-board, her posture straight, her presence commanding attention without raising her voice. When I approached her, she had inspected my walking shoes with a brief, approving glance.

*“At least you wore real leather instead of rubber beach sandals,”* she had murmured then, her eyes sparkling with dry, self-contained wit. *“Most tourists dress as if they are visiting a suburban swimming pool rather than three miles of mountain stairs.”*

Now, an hour past noon, the morning crowds had thinned as we climbed beyond the lower loop. The air grew heavier, cooler, laden with the pungent scent of decomposing oak leaf mold, damp basalt, and the sweet resin of mountain cedar.

A Sudden crush of descending school children in navy sailor uniforms forced us toward the right balustrade. The passage between the vermillion pillars was barely wide enough for two people to pass shoulder-to-shoulder. To let the chattering teenagers through, I stepped back against the timber gate, and Chinatsu was pushed flush against my chest.

Her linen blouse pressed flat against my shirt. Through the thin layers of fabric, I felt the rapid, fluttering rhythm of her heart and the heat radiating from her skin. Her hand came up to steady herself against my forearm, her slender fingers tightening around my bicep with surprising firmness.

For three heartbeats, neither of us moved.

Her breath brushed the side of my throat—warm, sweet, carrying the trace of cold green tea. Her brown eyes lifted to mine, wide and dark, stripped for an instant of her professional guide's composure. She didn't step back even after the last student had scampered past. Her fingers remained curled around my bicep, her thumb pressing lightly into the muscle.

"You have very steady footing," she murmured, her voice dropping half an octave, the international cadence melting into a soft, private murmur.

"Comes from watching where I step," I replied, holding her gaze.

A tiny, knowing smile touched the corner of her lips. She slowly lowered her hand, but as her fingers slid down my forearm, her nails traced a slow line of electric heat along the sensitive skin of my wrist before she turned back to the steps.

We reached the junction of the Yotsutsuji ridge ten minutes later. Here the twin tunnels of orange gates opened onto a wide granite landing carved from the mountain shoulder. A stone trough caught spring water trickling from a hollow bamboo culm, spilling over the green algae rim with a hollow, liquid chuckle. A weathered granite fox statue stood guardian beside the basin, its jaws clamping a stylized iron key, its neck wrapped in a faded red cotton bib stained brown by decades of mountain rain.

"Fifteen minutes for water and rest," Chinatsu announced to the group, bowing with practiced elegance. "The descent route splits to the west. If you wish to reach the Keihan Line before the evening commuter rush, we begin our descent at four."

The other three travelers scattered toward the coin-operated vending machines humming against the stone retaining wall. I walked to the cistern, scooped cold spring water into the wooden ladle, and rinsed the dust from my hands and wrists. The mountain runoff was icy, sending a shock of clarity through my overheated skin.

Chinatsu walked over to the stone balustrade, unbuttoned the top button of her navy blouse, and took a small linen handkerchief from her canvas satchel to blot her throat. Her neck was long, pale, glistening with fine beads of sweat that caught the golden afternoon sun. When she noticed me watching, she didn't avert her eyes. Instead, she took her time blotting her collarbones, her lips curving with that dangerous, teasing edge that made my pulse beat against my ribs.

"You look at the joinery rather than the fox tails," she said, resting her hips against the granite balustrade. The vast bowl of Kyoto opened behind her, sprawling across the valley floor in a grey haze of slate-blue tile roofs, railway wires, and distant office towers. "Most guests want to hear about fairy foxes and spirit possession. You were checking the tenon joints on the third gate."

"Wood holds weight better than folklore," I said, leaning beside her. My shoulder brushed hers—linen against linen, warm and solid. She didn't pull away.

"My grandfather ran a tea terrace in Uji," she said softly, her Kansai dialect bleeding through her words, softening her consonants into something rich and intimate. "He taught me that every wooden hall in this valley stands because someone understood how to divert ditch water. If you do not respect the timber rot, the roof collapses while you are praying."

"Did he teach you to despise the tour route?"

A laugh broke in her chest—low, throaty, slightly rough around the edges. She reached up with both hands, pulled the antique brass pin from her hair, and shook her head once. Her heavy raven hair tumbled across her shoulders in a glossy, fragrant wave that smelled of yuzu soap and hot sun. She re-fastened it with a swift, fluid twist of her slender wrists. The movement pulled the damp linen of her blouse tight across her breasts, outlining the curve of dark lace beneath the fabric.

"I despise reciting the same seven dates to people who only want a photograph for their telephone," she murmured, stepping an inch closer. Her shoulder pressed firmly into my bicep, radiating a heat that had nothing to do with the summer sun. "I have given this speech three hundred and forty times this year. Sometimes I want to scream into the bamboo."

"Then don't recite it."

"And lose my municipal license?" She arched an eyebrow, but the playful spark in her gaze was laced with a deeper, reckless hunger. "Kyoto has thousands of hidden corners that the tourism board pretends do not exist because they lack safety rails and English placards."

"Show me one."

She tilted her head, studying my face with an intensity that made the air between us grow thin and taut. Down at the landing, the Australian couple was tossing empty green tea bottles into a wire recycling basket, preparing to head down.

"Ten minutes up the western spur," she whispered, her voice dropping into a conspiratorial register that set my pulse drumming against my ribs. "Past the tea hut where the path turns into unpaved dirt. There is an old auxiliary shrine swallowed by camphor roots. A timber merchant built it in 1690 for an apprentice courtesan he could not legally bring into his family compound. Nobody climbs up there. The steps are broken, the moss is slippery, and there are no signs."

She looked at her brass watch, its leather strap darkened by sweat against her slender wrist. Then she looked back up at me through dark, thick lashes, her tongue darting out to moisten her bottom lip.

"We can walk down to the station together," she said, the words measured, giving me the choice. "The tour ends at the gate, we bow, I hand you a promotional postcard, and you take the train to your hotel. Or you let the group go down alone, and you follow me into the mountain."

The choice sat between us, heavy and electric as the summer storm brewing over the western hills.
