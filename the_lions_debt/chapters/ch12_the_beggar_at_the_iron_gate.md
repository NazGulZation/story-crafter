# The Beggar at the Iron Gate

When my father died of a sudden seizure in his solar, the low-born girl who had shared his bed thought her tears and bare skin would buy her mercy. For five years, she had ruled the household stewards, demanding deep bows from western knights and decking her throat in the emeralds of my dead mother. She had made a mockery of Casterly Rock, turning the seat of kings into a brothel where every parasite in Lannisport could feast on Lannister gold. When I walked into my father's bedchamber, she fell to her knees on the sheepskin rugs, weeping loud, tearing open her silk bodice to offer her body to the new Lord of the West.

I looked down at her with cold disgust. A weak man sees tears and takes frantic squirming for love; a lord sees a blood-fat leech hunting for a fresh vein. I ordered my household guards to strip her of every stitch of silk, every gold ring, and every pearl. I made her walk naked through the streets of Lannisport for two weeks, confessing to every shopkeeper, docker, and tavern wench that she was a common thief, before running her out of the West with empty hands. She wept and begged at every street corner, but stone does not weep.

Those who trade in betrayal always turn to tears and bare skin when the bill comes due. They must learn that justice is stone, deaf to weeping and blind to flesh.

***

The command tent of the Lord Protector smelled of burning anthracite coal, mulled cider steeped with cloves, and wet wool drying near the stove.

An iron stove, hauled from the city upon a dedicated ox cart and vented through a double copper chimney, threw fierce, dry heat into the double-walled canvas pavilion. Outside, the blizzard howled across the frozen basin of Skelter-Ridge, burying the four thousand dead frost-orcs beneath three feet of drifting snow. Inside, the ground was covered with thick straw mats and cured cowhides to keep the mountain frost from biting through the leather of our boots.

I sat behind a folding camp desk of dark walnut, reviewing the casualty returns, ammunition tallies, and lists of seized baggage.

A tin cup of hot, watered wine sat untouched near my right elbow. At my back stood two veteran syndicate sergeants in crimson wool surcoats, their gloved hands resting on the pommels of their broadswords. Beside my desk stood Master Garrow, dipping his goose quill into an ink-pot, noting down the serial numbers of forty wagons recovered from the snowdrifts.

The heavy canvas flap parted, letting in a swirl of snow and a blast of freezing mountain air that set the oil lamps swinging from the ridge pole.

Captain Korgan walked in, accompanied by four armored guardsmen carrying loaded crossbows.

Between them stumbled the three surviving women of the *Dawn-Seekers*.

They looked like drowned rats dragged from a frozen sewer.

Lady Vivienne Montclair hung limp between two soldiers, her boots dragging through the straw mats. The high-born sorceress had lost her fur mantle in the rout; her indigo silk robe was torn at the shoulder, exposing skin the grey color of curdled milk. Her fingers were wrapped in filthy, blood-stained linen rags to hide the frostbite eating her knuckles, and dried black crusts ringed her mouth and nostrils from her burst mana-veins. Her breath came in short, rattling wheezes, her head lolling on her chest.

Beside her walked Kallista Kroll. The marcher skirmisher held her chin high, though her wrists were tied tightly behind her back with tarred hemp rope. Her scarred face was caked with dried mud and black blood, her breath whistling through a broken, swollen nose, her dark eyes glaring around the tent like a trapped badger.

And behind them came Rosamund Cleary.

She had lost both of her leather shoes during the wild run across the shale. Her feet were wrapped in bloody rags cut from an orc tunic, soaked through with icy red slush. Her torn green velvet dress clung damply to her shivering frame, the hem caked with horse dung and frozen mud. Burrs and twigs tangled her matted hair.

When she stepped into the warm air of the tent, she did not look at the iron stove. She did not look at the steaming pot of cider.

She looked at me.

I did not lift my eyes from the parchment until I finished checking the final row of confiscated wagon manifests.

Then I set down my iron pen, blew dry sand across the ink, laced my fingers together at my chest, and met the eyes of the prisoners.

"Lady Vivienne Montclair," I said, my baritone low, flat, and dry as ash.

Vivienne lifted her chin with an agonizing effort. Her dark eyes, once full of courtly pride and academic disdain, were clouded with fever and the dull, vacant terror of an arcane addict whose drug had run dry.

"You are charged with high treason against the Grand Duchy of Dun-Carrow," I stated, reading from the legal roll drawn up by Morcar's clerks. "Specifically: the buying, hauling, and consumption of raw, unregistered northern frost-crystals, smuggled through the coal pits of the lower wharves in breach of the Sovereign Contraband Edict."

Vivienne's cracked, blackened lips twitched. "I... I needed them for the spells... to hold the shields... to protect the vanguard... to serve the light..."

"You took them to hide your own failing gift," I told her without pity. "And when the cold hit the basin, your poison stones failed you. You collapsed in the snow, leaving two hundred men without a shield against the blizzard. Under ducal statute, your academic titles are forfeit. Your lands in the East Reach are seized to pay your company's unpaid debts to the city treasury. You will return to Dun-Carrow in an iron cage to face formal judgment before the High Assize."

Vivienne said nothing. A dry, broken sob rattled in her throat as her knees gave way. She sagged between the two guards, who hauled her out through the canvas flap into the snow.

I turned my gaze to Kallista Kroll.

The marcher woman looked at me with cold, bitter hatred. "Save your speech, clerk. I swung steel till my blade broke. If you mean to hang me, find a pine tree and get it done. I won't beg to an ink-pot."

"I have no intention of hanging you, Kallista," I said.

She blinked, her brow furrowing, her jaw shifting in surprise. "You won't?"

"A dead cutthroat is worth zero copper coins," I told her. "A live one who knows how to handle a pickaxe has economic value. You hired sixty sellswords and failed to pay their wages, starting a riot that wrecked municipal property and burned two warehouses. Your personal debt to the city stands at twelve hundred silver crowns."

I pointed toward Captain Korgan.

"You are remanded to the Pioneer Sapper Corps. You will receive an iron shovel, a sixteen-pound sledgehammer, thick wool mittens, and hard tack to eat. You will spend the next three years breaking granite, grading timber roads, and hauling gravel across the Skelter Pass until your labor pays the bill in full. If you run, Korgan's men will put a bolt through your knees and leave you for the mountain wolves."

Kallista stared at me. Her jaw tightened into a hard, white knot. She saw the hard reality before her: three long years of breaking stone in the sleet.

"Take her out," I commanded.

The guards led Kallista away through the flap.

Then only one was left.

Rosamund Cleary stood alone in the center of the straw mats.

The dry heat from the cast-iron stove was beginning to thaw the frost in her hair, sending dirty drops of water trickling down her bruised cheeks. Her teeth clicked together in the quiet room—a rhythmic, shivering rattle that broke the silence of the tent.

She took one shaking step toward the desk. Then another.

Her knees buckled.

She hit the straw mats on her hands and knees, scrambling across the floorboards until she reached my walnut desk. Her raw, red hands reached out, grabbing desperately at the leather of my riding boots.

"Lucan..." she wept, her voice high, broken, and soaked in tears. "Lucan, please... please, look at me..."

I did not move my boot. I looked down at the crown of her tangled head the way a stonecutter looks at a cracked paver before tossing it into the rubble heap.

"My name is Lord Protector Holt," I said. "You will use the title."

Rosamund looked up, tears washing clean tracks through the grease and grime on her cheeks. She forced a shaking, desperate smile to her mouth—the same sweet, doe-eyed expression she had used as a girl whenever she wanted the boy Lucan to carry her water bucket or hand over his portion of honeycake.

"Lucan, it’s Rosamund," she sobbed, pressing her dirty cheek against the toe of my boot. "Your Rosamund. We grew up together in the Weald. Remember the willow creek? Remember the hay-loft behind your father’s barn? You said you would build a stone house for us... you swore you would always keep me safe..."

"The boy who swore that died on the floor of the Sun-Lance Pavilion," I said, my voice quiet as falling rock. "You killed him with your own tongue."

Her smile froze. Terror flashed in her wide hazel eyes.

"I was under a spell, Lucan!" she cried, her hands moving up to grip my calf through the boiled wool. "Lysander... he had that golden charm from the Goddess! He charmed my wits, he made me say those awful things! I never wanted him, Lucan! I swear it by the light! I was terrified! When he took me into his tent, I wept every night... I thought of you out in the dark... I prayed you would come and save me!"

A cheap, grotesque play. A lie so clumsy a village fool would have seen through it in an instant.

I leaned forward, resting my forearms upon the walnut desk.

"You were on your knees," I reminded her softly. "Those were your words, Rosamund. In the Sun-Lance Pavilion. You told me you were on your knees upon his wolf furs, taking him into your mouth until you choked on his seed. You laughed while your friends mocked my lack of steel. You told me I was a small, dry, boring little man, and that you had had your fill of small men."

Rosamund flinched as if struck across the face with an iron gauntlet.

All the blood drained from her lips. She shrank back against the floorboards, shaking violently as she realized that every cruel word she had thrown at the discarded boy had been etched into granite inside my memory.

"I... I was angry..." she stammered, dropping into a desperate, pleading whisper. "I was a fool, Lucan. A silly country girl who lost her head over silk ribbons and shiny plate. But I see the truth now! Look at what he did! He ran away! He left us all to die in the snow like dogs!"

Her fingers fumbled frantically with the dirty lace of her torn green bodice.

With shameless, terrified haste, she yanked the linen aside, baring her pale chest and shivering breasts in the warm lantern light.

"Take me back, Lucan," she begged, leaning in until her bare skin touched the wood of the desk. "You wanted me for years. I know you did. I can see it in your eyes. Take me tonight. In this tent. On these furs. I will do whatever you want. I will crawl for you, Lucan. I will wash your feet, warm your bed, be your girl, your whore, anything... just don't send me back into the cold. Don't leave me with nothing."

I looked at her skin.

It was pale, dirty, and smelled of sour sweat, cheap lard, and animal fear.

In my past life, I had ruled queens and high ladies of the realm. I had watched my daughter Cersei weave schemes with golden venom; I had dismissed the courtesans of King's Landing with a flick of my fingers; and I had watched my father drag our name through the gutter by letting a low tavern girl walk Casterly Rock in my mother's emeralds.

To a lion, the squirming of a common slut is only an insult.

I reached out, took the torn collar of her dress, and pulled the cloth shut over her chest with cold, impersonal efficiency.

"Cover yourself," I said.

Rosamund froze, her eyes wide with blank, uncomprehending shock. In her narrow, rural mind, a woman's body was a coin that could buy bread, pardon, and safety from any man with blood in his veins. She had never met a man who looked on her flesh with the cold disgust he might give to sour milk.

"You speak of debt, Rosamund," I said, rising from my seat.

I stood over her, a tall, dark shadow against the yellow lantern light.

"A Lannister always pays his debts," I told her, my voice ringing off the canvas like a bronze bell. "I pay my friends with silver, safety, and honor. And I pay my foes with iron and ruin."

"Lucan... please..." she whimpered, curling into a ball on the straw. "What will you do to me?"

"You wanted to be a great lady," I said, walking around the desk to the tent flap. "You wanted to live on the wealth of the towns without paying for the bread. You have your wish."

I looked at Captain Korgan.

"The salt mines of House Belmonte at the river mouth belong to The Golden Lion Factor now. They need hands to shovel the salt pans and sort the brine blocks. Take this woman to the coast under armed guard tomorrow morning."

Rosamund gasped, terror twisting her mouth into an ugly knot. "The salt mines? No! The brine burns your skin! The women in the pans don't last three years! Lucan, no! Mercy! By the gods, mercy!"

"Mercy is for those who surrender with honor," I said, not looking back. "Traitors receive only their wages. You chose the golden peacock. Now you shovel salt to pay for his grave."

"Lucan! Lucan!" she shrieked, clawing at the straw mats as two guards caught her arms and dragged her backward out the tent door into the storm.

Her screams died in the cold dark, swallowed by the roaring wind.

I walked back to my desk, sat down in the high-backed camp chair, and dipped my iron pen into the horn of black ink.

Captain Korgan returned to the tent, unbuckling his wet iron gauntlets, and set a fresh sheaf of baggage manifests upon the cedar wood. 

I turned the page, sprinkled dry sand across the signatures, and began auditing the requisition rolls for the march back to Dun-Carrow.
