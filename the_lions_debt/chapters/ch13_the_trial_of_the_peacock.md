# The Trial of the Peacock

*And who are you, the proud lord said,*
*that I must bow so low?*
*Only a cat of a different coat,*
*that's all the truth I know.*

A proud boy with a gilded title always asks the same question when the bill comes due. He believes his noble blood, his handsome face, and the songs sung in his honor place him above the arithmetic of debt. He thinks a man in a black wool coat has no right to demand his accounting. He forgets that gold, silver, and iron care nothing for coats or pedigree. When the iron dock closes, the proudest lord learns that the law is an executioner's scaffold built of calfskin and ink, and the ledger always collects its fee.

***

The Grand Hall of Justice in the Citadel of Dun-Carrow was built to cow lesser men.

Soaring rib-vaults of black Old Imperial basalt rose sixty feet above a floor of polished green porphyry tiles, reflecting the pale, icy winter light streaming through high lancet windows. At the southern end of the hall stood the raised dais of the High Assize, flanked by twelve high-backed seats of carved oak where sat the Grand Magistrates of the realm, wrapped in heavy sable mantles and white ermine collars. 

The air in the cavernous room was bitter cold, smelling of beeswax tapers, damp wool, cold stone flags, and the sour sweat of three hundred packed bodies. A bailiff in black livery struck the flags three times with a brass-tipped staff, the sharp thuds echoing through the stone arches.

Beneath the dais gathered the grandees of the realm: marching barons in wolfskins, wealthy guild masters with heavy gold chains around their velvet doublets, cathedral prelates in saffron robes, and city wardens with their hands resting on iron pommels. Along the northern gallery sat thirty women in black mourning shawls—the mothers and widows of the squires and levies whom Lysander had led to slaughter at Skelter-Ridge. They sat like stone statues, their red-rimmed eyes fixed upon the center of the hall with cold, unblinking hatred.

In the center of the floor, enclosed within the heavy iron railing of the prisoner's dock, stood Lysander Morvan.

He had spent three weeks hiding in a goat shack in the high mountain crags before creeping back into the city under cover of darkness, only to be taken at the river gate by Captain Korgan's night watch.

Yet pride dies hard in an empty vessel.

He had demanded his plate armor from the palace armory, and though the gilded breastplate was dented from orc clubs and the sunburst across his ribs was scored by shale, he wore it with the haughty defiance of a tragic hero. His spun-gold hair had been combed back over his broad shoulders; his blue eyes burned with indignation as he rested his right hand upon the pommel of his Sun-Sword.

"This court is an outrage!" Lysander’s voice boomed through the vaulted hall, rich, melodious, and practiced in its theatrical fury. "I am a Knight of the Radiant Dawn! The chosen champion of the solar heavens! I rode into the teeth of the northern frost to defend this ungrateful city! If the vanguard broke, it was because of treachery! The commissary starved my horses! The quartermasters withheld our grain! I stand here the savior of Dun-Carrow, betrayed by a scheming clerk who bought his title with stolen silver!"

A murmur ran through the noble gallery. Several marcher lords who remembered Lysander's victories in the spring tourneys shifted in their seats, whispering behind their gloved hands.

I sat at the High Justiciar’s table at the foot of the dais, clad in a mantle of black velvet trimmed with sheared Norrmark lynx. 

Before me lay no weapons, no holy relics, no radiant sunburst medals. On my table sat three heavy pigskin ledgers, an iron sand-pot, and forty signed, notarized deposition rolls.

I stood up from my chair.

The movement was slow, deliberate, and unhurried. The murmur in the hall vanished instantly, swallowed by the heavy silence that followed my presence wherever I walked.

I picked up a single sheet of vellum and stepped up to the iron bar of the dock.

"You speak of starvation, Sir Lysander," I said, my baritone low, gravelly, and carrying to the furthest corners of the basalt vaults. "Let us look at the books of the High Commissary."

Lysander curled his lip in contempt. "I care nothing for clerk's scrawl! Good blood was spilled on that ridge while you counted coppers in a warm room!"

"On the twelfth day of the ninth moon," I continued, speaking over him without raising my voice, "the *Dawn-Seeker* company received an advance requisition of four thousand silver crowns from the Sovereign Treasury. That coin was allocated by the Archon for the purchase of three hundred cold-weather wool coats, twelve hundred pairs of felt boots, and six wagons of salted grain for your retainers."

I turned the parchment toward the twelve Grand Magistrates on the dais, then signaled to the clerk’s bench.

"Call Master Harlen of the Merchant Factor," I said.

An elderly clerk with ink-stained fingers and iron spectacles stepped forward, carrying a leather dispatch case. He took the stand, bowed to the magistrates, and placed three wax-sealed receipts upon the table.

"Read the itemized delivery receipts from Madame Clarisse of the Velvet Gallery," I directed.

Master Harlen cleared his throat, his dry voice cutting through the cold hall. "Item: four sets of barded horse cloths dyed imperial sky-blue and embroidered with silver thread, one thousand two hundred silver crowns. Item: eight crates of southern almond pomade, rose-water, and scented soap for Sir Lysander's personal bath tent, eight hundred crowns. Item: one electrum parade scabbard inlaid with river pearls and gold wire, six hundred crowns. Item: forty casks of spiced sun-wine for the officers' mess, four hundred crowns."

A sharp hiss of disgust broke from the merchant gallery.

"The boots were never ordered," I stated flatly, looking up from the paper to the prisoner. "The wool coats were never woven. When your vanguard reached the foothills of Skelter-Ridge, seventy-two of your men suffered severe frostbite before the first orc horn blew, because their commander preferred scented bath water to woolen socks."

Lysander’s face flushed dark crimson. His fingers gripped the iron railing until the knuckles turned white. "The glory of the Goddess demands splendor! A champion cannot face heathen beasts dressed like a ditch-digger! Splendor inspires the men! It gives them heart!"

"Splendor does not stop frostbite," I replied. "Call the next witness."

The side doors of the hall creaked open. A young man hobbled into the chamber on two wooden crutches, supported by a city warden. He was seventeen years old, dressed in a clean but worn linen tunic. His left trouser leg was pinned up at the knee, and his right foot was bound in thick wool bandages.

"State your name for the court," I said.

"Tomas, my lord," the boy whispered, his voice trembling in the vast hall. "Squire to Sir Lysander's second company."

"Step forward, Tomas. Show the magistrates your foot."

The boy leaned heavily on his crutches, sat upon a low wooden bench placed by the bailiff, and untied the wool wrapping. 

A collective shudder ran through the spectators. The front half of the boy's right foot was gone—cut away at the arch, leaving a jagged, purple stump sealed with black tar and thread.

"Tell the court how you lost your foot, Tomas," I said quietly.

"We walked through the Skelter drifts in thin summer shoes, my lord," Tomas said, tears cutting through the grime on his pale cheeks. "We had no tallow for our leather, no grease, and no wool socks. On the third night, my toes turned black as coal. The surgeon took them off with a carpenter's saw behind Sir Lysander's silk tent while the captain was drinking hot spiced wine with his squires."

From the gallery of widows, a woman let out a strangled, weeping sob. Several marcher barons turned their heads away, unable to look at the boy's ruined limb.

Lysander shifted his boots on the green tiles, his eyes darting toward the exits. "A soldier accepts hardship! The boy knew the risks of war!"

"The boy was ordered into the snow without boots while you spent his equipment money on almond soap," I said. "Dismissed, Tomas. The city will provide you with an artisan's pension."

The boy was helped from the hall. I laid the commissary roll upon the table and picked up a second vellum dispatch.

"Let us examine the muster rolls of the fourteenth day," I said, addressing the magistrates. "Captain Korgan’s sappers built an oak timber bridge across the Skelter Gorge at the cost of eighty seasoned balks and four days of hard labor. You had a written order, bearing my personal seal as Lord Protector, to hold the bridgehead on the southern bank and await the arrival of the main army."

I stepped one pace closer to the iron dock, my golden-green eyes locking onto his.

"You burned the dispatch in your camp brazier. You took three hundred mounted men and crossed into the northern basin twelve hours ahead of schedule, with only three days of dry bread in your saddlebags. Why?"

"To strike first!" Lysander shouted, puffing out his chest against his gilded breastplate. "To seize the initiative! A true knight attacks; he does not sit on his rear in the mud waiting for baggage carts and fat clerks!"

"You crossed because Margrave Leopold’s heralds were spotted approaching the camp," I told him, laying bare the truth with ruthless precision. "You knew that if the main host arrived under my command, the glory of breaking the frost-orcs would belong to the Sovereign Army. You wished to claim the sole honor of slaying Warlord Mor-Goth so you could force the Margrave to grant you the hand of Lady Alicia and the hereditary lordship of the South Reach."

In the front row of the noble benches, Margrave Leopold sat bolt upright, his liver-spotted face flushing with sudden, venomous fury. Beside him, Lady Alicia buried her face in her ermine muff, weeping with bitter shame at having been courted by such a transparent knave.

Lysander reached for his sword hilt, his voice rising in panic. "Lies! Spiteful slander made up by a bitter clerk whose village milkmaid threw him over for a real warrior!"

I did not flinch. I did not raise my voice.

"Let us speak of the battle itself," I said, lifting the third document. "The sworn words of fourteen surviving knights of your vanguard, taken under holy oath before the Archon’s bailiffs."

The hall fell dead silent, three hundred people leaning forward on their benches.

"When the frost-orcs encircled the knoll," I read, "Lady Vivienne collapsed from arcane burn. Captain Kallista’s bow snapped in the cold. Rosamund Cleary hid beneath your baggage cart. Two hundred squires and levies formed a ring of steel around your sunburst banner. What did their glorious captain do?"

I lowered the parchment and fixed Lysander with an icy stare.

"You mounted your fresh reserve courser. You turned your horse's head away from your dying men. You trampled two wounded squires beneath your hooves as you broke through your own lines, and you fled alone into the western pass, leaving three hundred men and women who trusted your divine mission to be butchered like cattle in a ditch."

A roar of pure fury erupted from the benches of the City Wardens.

"Coward! Oath-breaker! Traitor!"

Men who had lost sons, brothers, and comrades at Skelter-Ridge surged toward the iron bar, their hands clawing at their dagger hilts, restrained only by the locked pikes of Korgan’s red-cloaked guards.

Lysander backed away until his armor clattered against the rear railing of the dock. Sweat poured down his pale face, pasting his golden curls to his cheeks. He looked at the magistrates; he saw twelve masks of carved granite. He looked at the barons who had cheered him at the tourneys; they looked upon him with pure contempt.

His world had crumbled. The theater was dismantled; the audience had turned into a mob thirsting for his ruin.

In his cornered, desperate madness, he reached for his final, hollow illusion.

He threw both hands toward the black basalt rib-vaults.

"Hear me, Mother of the Dawn!" Lysander shrieked, his voice cracking into wild fanaticism. "Look down upon this nest of vipers! Look upon this faithless, cold usurer who dares drag your chosen champion into the dirt! I call upon your holy fire! Cleanse this hall! Strike down this insolent clerk! Consume Lucan Holt in the righteous wrath of the solar skies!"

He closed his eyes tight, his body shaking violently, waiting for the miracle.

The assembled nobles shrank back against their benches, several crossing their breasts in dread, half-expecting the vaulted ceiling to split open and rain celestial fire upon the dais.

Ten heartbeats passed.

Twenty heartbeats passed.

Thirty heartbeats passed.

The high lancet windows remained grey, frosted with winter rime. The black basalt arches echoed only with the mournful moan of the wind against the Citadel walls.

No fire fell. No thunder shook the stone. No divine voice spoke from the clouds.

The hall remained freezing, dark, and utterly indifferent.

In the front row of the clerical benches, High Inquisitor Malachai stood up, smoothing his heavy saffron robes. The fat prelate did not look at Lysander. He turned his back upon the dock, raised his gold pectoral cross toward the magistrates, and spoke in a clear, resonant tone.

"The Church of the Radiant Dawn has examined the conduct of Sir Lysander Morvan," Malachai announced, his voice devoid of pity. "A knight who steals church-blessed tithes, starves his men, and abandons his holy banner in flight has severed his bond with the heavens. The Mother of the Dawn knows no cowards. The Church formally revokes his knighthood and yields him wholly to the temporal justice of the Crown."

The church had received six thousand silver crowns from The Golden Lion Factor two weeks ago to pay off its cathedral debts. The gods of Dun-Carrow, like their priests, had no ears for broke fools.

Lysander opened his eyes. He stared at his trembling, empty hands. He looked up at the cold stone roof.

"No..." he whimpered, his voice shrinking to the thin, cracked squeak of a beaten child. "Mother... why have you left me?"

"The gods do not answer debtor's prayers, Morvan," I said quietly.

I turned away from the dock, laid the notarized deposition rolls upon the clerk's table, and addressed the twelve Grand Magistrates on the high dais.

"The depositions are sworn," I announced. "The evidence is sealed. The Crown rests its case."
