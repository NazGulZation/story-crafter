# The Winter Advance

Kallista Kroll knew cold. A border skirmisher did not live past thirty winters along the northern marches without learning what freezing air did to steel, horseflesh, and bone.

She knelt in two feet of crusted snow on the bare basalt shelf, her bare knuckles bleeding as she chipped packed ice from the frog of her dun mare’s left hoof. The little mountain horse shuddered violently, its shaggy coat matted with frozen sweat, its breath rattling in its chest like dry leaves. There were no oats left in the feedbags. There was no meadow hay. The scrub grass across the high basin lay buried under three feet of hard winter crust, and horses could not graze on frozen granite.

"Easy, girl," Kallista muttered through her wool scarf. Her breath rose in thick grey plumes that froze instantly to her eyebrows and the rough fur collar of her coat. Her jaw still ached with a dull, throbbing pain where the mutinous sellsword sergeant had smashed her with his dagger hilt back in Dun-Carrow. 

She stood, stamping her iron-shod boots into the snow to drive the numbness from her toes, and looked across the camp of the Sun-Lance Vanguard.

It was a butcher's yard waiting for the cleaver.

Three hundred horsemen, forty knights in gilded plate, and seventy noble retainers huddled together on the crown of an exposed rocky knoll in the dead center of the Skelter Basin. The position was madness. To the north, east, and west, the black basalt crags of the ridge rose like the teeth of a bear trap, sheer and wind-scoured. Any sergeant with half an eye for ground would have seen the danger in ten seconds: whoever held the high crags commanded the basin, and whoever sat on the knoll below was an open target.

Worse, the men were freezing in their saddles.

Lysander’s knights wore heavy plate armor over fine wool and silk. In an autumn tournament, the silvered steel and gilded lions looked glorious beneath the sun. In a mountain blizzard at ten degrees below freezing, plate armor acted like an iron ice-box. It sucked the heat directly out of a man’s core, stiffening his joints and turning the sweat on his linen arming jacket into sheets of frost. Knights sat on their mounts like frozen statues, their teeth chattering so loud the sound carried over the wind, their fingers too numb to grip their lances.

Near the center of the knoll, three noble squires were trying to start a cook-fire with wet dwarf-pine branches. Thick, bitter white smoke billowed across the snow, stinging Kallista’s eyes and bringing tears that froze on her cheeks. There was no dry timber. Lysander had refused to wait for the pioneer wagons carrying seasoned cordwood, declaring that the Sun Goddess would keep her faithful warm.

Inside the large command pavilion of crimson velvet, Lady Vivienne Montclair sat on a cedar chest, wrapped in four heavy wool blankets. The highborn sorceress was shivering with violent, racking tremors. Her face had the color of curdled tallow, her lips were grey-blue, and dark purple circles bruised the skin beneath her hollow eyes. Earlier that morning, Vivienne had attempted to channel her solar mana into a warming sphere to heat the command pavilion. The brutal mountain chill had sapped her magical reserves within minutes, leaving her with severe mana chills. Every ten minutes she dry-heaved into an iron bowl, clutching her ebony staff with trembling, claw-like fingers.

Beside her, Rosamund Cleary sat on a heap of damp fur cloaks, weeping with soft, frantic misery.

The childhood sweetheart of Lucan Holt looked like a drowned mouse. The ermine-trimmed green velvet gown that Lucan had bought for her on merchant credit three months ago was soaked with black slush around the hem, dragging heavily in the mud. Her delicate archer’s hands, once kept smooth with rosewater ointment, were red, raw, and cracked with chilblains.

"My feet hurt, Lysander," Rosamund whined, her voice thin and shrill in the freezing tent. "The ice is coming through my slippers. When will the servants bring hot spiced cider? Why haven't the scouts found a warm hall?"

Lysander Morvan stood at the open flap of the pavilion, gazing out over the frozen wasteland.

He looked magnificent. His sunburst breastplate caught the faint grey glare filtering through the snow clouds, the golden filigree shining bright against the dark wool of his heavy mantle. His long golden hair whipped in the wind like a battle banner, and his handsome face bore the serene, untroubled smile of a divine champion.

"Patience, my sweet rose," Lysander said, his rich tenor voice ringing with absolute confidence. He reached out with an armored hand, patting Rosamund's tear-stained cheek. "The road to triumph is paved with small hardships. The Goddess tests our faith before she grants the crown. When we break Mor-Goth’s vanguard, his halls will provide us with roast venison, southern wine, and hearths piled high with cedar logs."

Kallista marched up to the tent flap, ducking under the canvas with a snort of pure disgust.

"Mor-Goth doesn't have a warm hall, Lysander," Kallista snapped, dropping her voice to a harsh, flat growl so the shivering squires outside wouldn't hear. "He’s a frost-orc warlord living in turf huts and leather yurts. And we aren't breaking anyone. Look outside! We have three hundred horses with their ribs showing, forty knights who can't unbuckle their own codpieces because their fingers are dead, and exactly three barrels of hardtack left in the baggage carts!"

Lysander turned, his blue eyes radiating calm amusement. "You worry like an old fishwife, Kallista. We hold the center of the valley. When the heathen horde descends, our knights will charge downhill and shatter their foot soldiers with holy lances."

"Charge downhill through four feet of snow?" Kallista grabbed his gilded vambrace, pointing a calloused finger through the tent flap at the steep, boulder-strewn slopes below the knoll. "The horses will founder up to their chests in thirty yards! They’ll break their forelegs in the scree! The orcs won't even need to fight us—they'll stand on the rim and shoot our mounts full of iron-tipped hunting arrows until we're all walking on foot!"

"The light will shield us," Lysander said smoothly, pulling his arm free with easy arrogance. "Faith pierces the storm."

"Faith won't feed three hundred horses!" Kallista’s voice rose, raw with fury. "The bridge over the Skelter gorge is gone, Lysander! One of the carters shouted it across the canyon before the last foot-rope snapped. An ice dam broke upriver. The entire wooden trestle went into the river at dawn! We are completely cut off on the north bank. Lucan’s supply train is trapped on the other side with forty wagons of flour, eight barrels of cured beef, and all our spare horse fodder!"

At the mention of Lucan's name, Rosamund sat up from her furs, her chapped mouth curling into a spiteful sneer.

"Stop saying his name!" Rosamund spat, wiping snot from her upper lip with the back of her sleeve. "You sound like that useless coward! Lucan was always crying about flour sacks and wagon wheels! He wanted us to live like common shopkeepers! Lysander is a hero blessed by the Goddess herself! He doesn't need a grubby clerk to win a war!"

Kallista stared at the girl, then at Lysander's smiling, empty face.

A cold stone dropped into the pit of her stomach.

For three long years, Kallista had laughed at Lucan Holt. When the party made camp, Lucan was the one who sat by the grease-lamp until midnight, checking every horseshoe nail, verifying grain sacks, and making sure the wagon axles had fresh tallow. When they crossed the salt bogs, Lucan was the one who brought sulfur ointment to stop foot-rot. When they hired mercenaries, Lucan was the one who counted out the silver crowns and made every captain sign a binding receipt.

Kallista had called him a useless pen-pusher. She had laughed when Lysander kicked him into the tavern mud without boots. She had thought that steel and swagger were all an army needed to take the world.

Now, standing in the teeth of a howling northern blizzard, the brutal truth struck her like an iron hammer:

Lucan had kept them alive.

Every meal they had eaten, every dry pair of socks they had pulled on, every rested horse they had ridden into battle had come from the cold, ruthless calculations of the clerk they had despised. Lysander had never planned a campaign in his life. The golden hero simply rode forward, smiled for the bards, and left Lucan to clean up the blood, pay the bills, and haul the bread.

Without Lucan, Lysander Morvan was only a pretty boy in gilded iron, leading three hundred starving fools into an open grave.

Before Kallista could speak, an iron war-horn of the northern crags brayed through the sleet—deep, vibrating, humming through the frozen stone beneath their boots like the growl of a mountain lion.

Another horn answered from the eastern ridgeline.

Then a third, from the high western pass behind them.

Kallista tore out of the pavilion and ran to the edge of the knoll.

Through the whirling sheets of white snow, dark shapes were emerging along the black basalt rims of the canyon. Hundreds of them. Frost-orcs riding shaggy, heavy-chested winter wargs, their pine-plank shields painted with white runes, their heavy iron cleavers resting against their shoulders. They did not rush down the slope. They did not scream war-cries or break into a wild charge.

They simply spread out along the high ridge, sealing every defile and pass, blocking the only paths out of the valley. They sat on the high stone like vultures on a slaughter rail, watching the knights freeze.

Lysander strode out of the tent, drawing his magnificent enchanted sword. The gold blade glowed with a faint, flickering amber light that cast weak shadows on the snow.

"To me!" Lysander shouted, raising the sword into the gale, his voice booming across the knoll. "Knights of the Sun! Draw steel! Let the heathens witness the wrath of the righteous!"

Nobody moved.

A dozen squires looked at the glowing sword, then up at the high black crags lined with five thousand orcs. One of the knights dropped his lance into the snow; his fingers were so stiff with frostbite that he could not pick it up.

Kallista reached down to test her horn-and-sinew recurve bow. The extreme mountain freeze had hardened the animal glue inside the limbs until it was brittle as glass. The string gave an ominous, dry groan under her thumb.

She closed her eyes, her breath catching in her throat.

They were trapped. The trap was sprung, the meat was on the table, and the clerk who knew how to save them was ten leagues away on the other side of a broken gorge.

During the winter campaign across the high passes of the Golden Tooth, when snow lay three feet deep on the tracks and the mountain creeks froze solid into blue ice, two of my supply captains started stealing grain from the army wagons. They sold salt pork to local peddlers at four times the market rate, watering the men's oats to hide the stolen weight. A soldier marching ten leagues through sleet can bear hunger for an afternoon. By the third morning of thin gruel, his fingers go numb on his spear-shaft, his knees give out in the slush, and he starts eyeing his officer's throat with a skinning knife in his fist.

My brother Kevan wanted to fine the men their back pay and transfer them to the baggage train. I had them stripped to the skin and hanged from the gateposts of the nearest keep, with their false ledgers nailed directly into their ribs with iron studs. In winter, an army lives on the cold iron of its supply line. A lord who lets his officers steal bread kills his own men faster than an enemy cavalry charge.

Winter strips away all fine talk. It leaves only frozen mud, the squeal of wagon axles, and the hard will of command.

Fools talk of glory; men of sense talk of wagons and bread.

By the second week of the northern march, the military road from Dun-Carrow was a frozen ditch of black mud, rough ice, and dead draft mules that had lacked iron winter shoes. The cold bit so deep that the axle grease in our wagon hubs turned to stiff grey lard. The wind blew straight off the northern ice caps, a screaming gale that drove hard snow into our faces like crushed glass.

I rode in the center of the column on a stout grey gelding dressed in boiled-wool trappings.

Around me marched four thousand provincial soldiers: two thousand pikemen in boiled leather and iron caps, eight hundred heavy crossbowmen, six hundred City Wardens, and six hundred drivers handling two hundred supply wagons.

Every man had a thick wool coat lined with fleece, two pairs of dry socks inside his pack, and grease for his boots. Each squad of ten men had an iron fire-pot and a sack of hard anthracite coal. At the third watch of the day, the column halted, the iron kettles boiled over coal embers, and each man received a hot bowl of thick barley porridge cooked with salt pork, dried peas, and lard.

Discipline in winter starts with dry feet and hot broth.

A horseman in a snow-crusted cloak came galloping down the track from the advance screen, his mount's breath rattling like a forge bellows.

It was Captain Korgan. He reined in beside my gelding, his thick beard white with frost, his face set in grim lines.

"The bridge over the Skelter gorge is gone, Lord Protector," Korgan yelled over the scream of the wind. "An ice dam broke three miles upriver. A wall of drift-ice and dead pine hit the stone piers at dawn. The center posts snapped like dry twigs. The whole ninety-foot span dropped into the canyon."

I drew rein. Behind us, two hundred heavy wagons groaned to a halt, their iron-rimmed wheels grinding hard into the ruts of ice.

"Where is the vanguard?" I asked.

Korgan spat a mouthful of dark phlegm into the slush. "Lysander crossed last night before the ice hit. He had three hundred horse, forty knights, Alicia's guards, and his personal baggage. When the bridge started creaking, his rear carters panicked. Lysander wouldn't stop to secure the approaches. He sent a runner back on the foot-rope before the timber gave: he claimed the Goddess had opened the road, and he was marching straight into the basin to engage Mor-Goth's screen."

"He left his reserve rations on this side of the gorge," I said.

"Aye," Korgan growled. "Twenty wagons of hardtack, six barrels of salt beef, and all the spare bowstrings. The boy took three days of food in his saddlebags. He told his knights they would eat roast pig in the orc camp by nightfall."

"A fool who eats tomorrow's dinner in his dreams starves by morning," I said. "Where is the nearest standing timber?"

"No pine within four leagues that hasn't been splintered by the ice storm," Korgan replied, shaking his head. "Felling trees and squaring forty-foot balks with hand broadaxes will take six days. The men will freeze solid in this gorge."

"We do not cut green trees," I said, pointing down the river track toward the eastern spur. "A mile south sits Margrave Leopold's summer hunting lodge. It was built forty years ago out of seasoned heart-oak beams six inches thick. Take two companies of sappers with heavy felling axes and iron wedges. Tear the lodge down to its foundation. Take the roof beams, the floor joists, and the great barn timbers."

Korgan stared, his jaw dropping open. "Tear down the Margrave's lodge? Lord Protector, Leopold sits on the Sovereign Council! He spent ten thousand gold ducats hauling that oak from the southern forests!"

"The Margrave can sleep in a snowdrift if he objects," I said. "Seasoned oak carries weight; green pine snaps under a wagon wheel. Have eighty beams delivered to the chasm edge within three hours. If the carters drag their feet, hang the lead teamster and find a man who knows how to crack a bullwhip."

"At once, Lord Protector!" Korgan turned his horse and galloped down the trail, his cloak snapping in the gale.

I swung down from the saddle and walked back toward the fourth wagon division, where the grain wagons were parked beneath the shelter of a basalt bluff.

A violent dispute had broken out near the rear of the train.

Two supply lieutenants in ducal blue wool were shouting at six ragged peasants who had fled the burning farms along the northern bank. One of the officers, a rat-faced fellow named Eldred, stood over an open supply cart, hugging a heavy sack of smoked bacon to his ribs and shouting at a shivering woman with a blue-lipped infant wrapped in rags.

"Three silver crowns for the bacon, woman!" Eldred barked, his face flushed red with greed. "No silver, no food! Clear the road before I have the guards run you into the ditch!"

I stepped behind the man, my iron-rimmed boots crunching on the frozen crust.

"What is in that wagon, Lieutenant?" I asked.

Eldred jumped, spinning around as if a dagger had pricked his kidneys. When he saw my face, the blood drained out of his cheeks, leaving his skin the color of dirty tallow.

"Lord... Lord Protector!" Eldred stammered, offering a crooked, trembling salute. "These... these peasants were trying to raid the commissary bins! I was merely securing the supplies for the army!"

I looked past his shoulder into the wagon bed.

Beneath a tarred canvas sheet lay eight barrels of prime salt pork. The bung-holes had been knocked open and the brine drained into the snow to reduce the weight, making room for four wooden casks of southern vintage wine and six bolts of expensive patterned velvet looted from a burned roadside manor.

I turned my gaze to the peasant woman. Her hands were purple with frostbite, her lips cracked and bleeding, her infant shivering under thin scraps of sheepskin.

I drew a heavy silver crown from my belt purse and pressed it into her stiff fingers.

"Take your child to the third division wagon," I said quietly. "Tell Master Garrow I ordered him to give you both a bowl of hot pea broth and two wool blankets."

The woman dropped into the slush on her knees, weeping words of blessing, but I was already looking back at Eldred.

"You dumped the brine from eight barrels of meat to haul stolen wine and velvet," I said, my voice dropping to that calm, level register that preceded the rope. "The meat will spoil within three days of exposure. And you attempted to sell provincial army rations to starving subjects for personal coin."

"My lord, mercy!" Eldred shrieked, dropping to his knees in the mud. "It was surplus! The military regulations allow five percent for road waste—"

"A soldier who wastes food in winter kills his comrades," I said. "An officer who sells army rations during an invasion hangs for high treason."

I signaled to the six City Wardens standing ten paces back with cocked arbalests.

"Hang them both," I said.

"My lord! Please!" the second lieutenant screamed, lunging forward to grasp my stirrups.

A warden stepped between us, driving the heavy iron butt of his crossbow into the man’s sternum with a sickening crunch, dropping him gasping into the snow.

"Hang them from the dead pine boughs overhanging the road," I told the warden sergeant. "Strip their boots and wool coats for the teamsters. Pin their commission papers to their chests so every carter in this host understands the cost of stealing bread from my wagons."

Ten minutes later, two bodies swung from the bare branches of the dead pine, twisting slowly in the freezing wind like pendulums.

The three hundred carters and laborers who witnessed the execution returned to their teams in total silence. No man muttered; no man complained of the biting frost. Wagons were greased, harness buckles tightened, and draft oxen hitched with mechanical precision.

By the fourth hour of the afternoon, Margrave Leopold's grand hunting lodge was a pile of splintered stone and empty earth.

Eighty seasoned heart-oak beams were hauled to the chasm by teams of heavy oxen. Pioneer crews drilled into the cliff stone with iron augers, securing the bridge timbers with heavy iron pins and forged chains produced in my city blast furnaces. By dusk, a solid timber trestle sixty feet long spanned the chasm, broad enough to carry forty heavy wagons an hour without a tremble.

I stood on the fresh oak planking, watching the first ranks of pikes march across the gorge into the northern defile.

The men moved in iron silence. No songs, no silk banners flapping in the dark, no drunken boasts of glory. Only the rhythmic, heavy crunch of hobnailed boots on oak, the clinking of iron mess kits, and the deep, grinding rumble of heavy supply wagons rolling steadily north. On the far side of the chasm, pioneers had planted iron tripods bearing cauldrons of burning pine-tar to illuminate the landing, throwing a fierce amber glare across the black canyon water churning eighty feet below.

Two scouts in white wolf-skin capes rode down the mountain trail, leading their frost-rimed mounts by the bridles. They halted before me, their breath steaming in the torchlight.

"Report," I said.

"The vanguard is trapped on the high knoll in the center basin, Lord Protector," the lead scout said, brushing ice from his lashes. "Morvan rode straight into the center flats without securing the high ridges or digging defensive ditches. The frost-orcs let him enter, then sealed the three mountain passes behind him. They've ringed his company on the rocky shelf. The boy has no firewood, no grain for his mounts, and the wind is blowing at gale force."

"Has he engaged?"

"They exchanged arrow fire at sunset, my lord. But the freeze is killing his horses. The knights are huddled under their shields, shivering in their armor. If the storm holds through the night, half his company will freeze to death before dawn."

Across the chasm, the narrow defile climbed steeply into the teeth of the blizzard, winding upward toward the high granite flats of Skelter-Ridge. The snow came down thick and blinding, muffling all sound save for the shrieking gale.

Far up the valley, muffled by the distance and the storm, the deep thud of orc war-horns vibrated through the stone—a steady, patient drumming that sounded like a butcher tapping his cleaver against a wooden chopping block.

I turned to the scout sergeant. "Send these men to the cooks' wagons for hot lard and barley porridge. Bring fresh mounts from the picket line."

I pulled my heavy leather gauntlets tight against the frost, mounted my grey gelding, and raised my hand to Captain Korgan.

"Advance the column across the bridge," I ordered. "Pikes in the center, heavy crossbows on the flanks, supply wagons locked tight in the rear. Maintain twenty paces between companies and keep the pitch torches burning."

Korgan raised his arm, the horn sounded two short, sharp blasts through the storm, and the army stepped across the river into the frozen dark.
