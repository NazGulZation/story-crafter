# The Splendor of the Gilded

During the reign of King Robert Baratheon, King’s Landing threw feasts for every royal name-day that cost forty thousand gold crowns a tilt. Robert would sit under gold cloth, roaring drunk, throwing handfuls of fresh silver to the cheering mob while knights smashed ash lances in the dirt. The crowd screamed his name until their throats bled; the hedge knights hailed him as the greatest warrior since the heroes of the Dawn Age; and the singers made rhymes about his bottomless purse.

I sat in the royal box beside Lord Arryn, auditing the debt books in cold silence. While Robert threw gold into the mud to buy the cheers of sheep, the crown borrowed millions from the Iron Bank, the Faith, and the vaults of Casterly Rock. A tourney produces nothing. It grows no wheat, smelts no iron, paves no road, and guards no gate. It is a bonfire where a foolish king burns his children's silver to buy an afternoon of noise from beggars who would spit on his bones tomorrow.

When an incompetent ruler faces a disaster he cannot fix, he throws a tournament.

The tourney field of Dun-Carrow smelled like a ditch dressed in silk.

A dry autumn wind blew off the plains, carrying the greasy reek of roasted oxen, horse dung, sour wine, and the sweat of twenty thousand commoners packed tight along the wooden fence. Bright pennants snapped from fifty pine poles against a pale sky. Horns blew from the Archon’s box at every tilt, drowning out the quiet panic of bread riots brewing down on the docks.

I stood in the shadow of the stone gallery, leaning against a grey pillar.

I wore a high-collared coat of black wool lined with wolf fur, cut clean and severe. My boots were greased black calfskin, with iron buckles. Beside me stood Captain Korgan of the City Wardens, his leather vest plated with forty iron scales forged in my cellars.

"The crowd loves him, Lord Factor," Korgan spat, his dark eyes watching the sand. "Listen to them howl. You’d think the boy pulled the sun out of his own pocket."

Below us, Lysander Morvan turned his white stallion in a tight circle.

The horse wore cloth-of-gold down to its hooves, its bridle jingling with silver bells. Lysander himself was a shining tower of pride. His plate armor had been dipped in gold, every shoulder-guard embossed with suns and cherubs. Over his back hung a white fur cape, flowing in the wind as he lifted his painted lance to salute the Archon.

The crowd roared.

*"The Sun-Gilded! The Son of the Dawn! Hail Lysander!"*

Lysander took off his helm, letting his gold hair fall loose over his neck. He flashed his bright, boyish grin, reached into a velvet saddle-bag, and hurled a double handful of silver coins into the front rows. The mob surged like starving pigs at a trough, gouging each other's eyes in the dirt for a scrap of stamped metal.

Beside the Archon’s throne sat Rosamund Cleary.

She had managed to get a seat among the ladies of the Margrave’s daughter, wearing a low-cut dress of blue silk. Around her throat hung a collar of pearls and red stones—rented from a harbor lender on sixty-day notes. She sat back on her cushions with a silver fan, laughing with shrill delight whenever Lysander looked her way.

She looked like a tavern maid playing dress-up in her mistress’s bedchamber.

"Bread and games," I said, my voice quiet over the horns. "King Robert Baratheon spent millions on tourneys while his realm bled into the hands of foreign banks. And when the feast ended, the wolves ate his house."

Korgan frowned, his hand on his sword hilt. "Who was that, Lord Factor?"

"A dead king who thought cheers were power," I said, smoothing my sleeve. "Tell me of Archon Althaus."

Korgan turned his head toward the middle box. 

There sat the master of Dun-Carrow: a fat, red-faced man of fifty years, slumped under purple velvet. His swollen right foot rested on a red pillow, his fingers dripping with soft gold rings. He chewed roasted birds while a dwarf fool tumbled across a southern pile rug before his throne.

"The Archon owes three hundred thousand gold ducats to the north," Korgan whispered, leaning his ear toward me. "The bailiffs have already pledged the wine taxes of the valleys for four years. Last week, the treasurer sent word to the guard: our pay was cut by a third, and we were told to steal oats from the farm carts."

"And the border?" I asked.

Korgan’s jaw locked tight. "The forts along the gorge are empty ruins. The garrison at High Crag ran off two weeks ago because their bread was full of worms. Warlord Mor-Goth has four thousand frost-orcs in the hills. They crossed the Frozen Tarn. When the hard frost hits the river next month, the ice will hold their wagons. They will march straight down the valley."

"And the Archon spends twelve hundred gold ducats on flags and roasted birds," I said.

"He thinks Lysander will kill them all," Korgan said with bitter disgust. "The priests tell him the boy is the living sun. They preach that when Mor-Goth comes, Lysander will ride out, draw his bright sword, and melt the horde into warm mud."

Below us, the horns blew again. 

A wooden gate swung wide. Lysander’s foe—a marcher knight in dented grey iron—walked his horse into the lane. 

The flag dropped.

The two horses surged down the sand, hooves throwing dirt. The marcher knight lowered his heavy ash lance with steady, veteran skill, his shield tucked tight to his ribs. Lysander did not lower his lance until the last three strides. Then, with a loud shout, his chest badge flared with blinding gold light.

The flash took the marcher knight square in the eye-slits. The older man flinched, blinded for half a second. 

Lysander’s lance took him right beneath the throat, lifting him straight out of his saddle. The iron-clad knight hit the wooden rail with a sickening crunch of timber and bone, dropping limp into the sand.

The crowd went wild. 

Rosamund stood up, clapping with shrill joy, her face red with excitement. Archon Althaus clapped his soft hands, tossing a gold purse down into the sand at Lysander's boots.

Lysander did not check to see if the knight was alive. He raised his lance, soaking in the cheers, turning his face to the sky as if he truly were a god on earth.

"A cheap trick," I murmured. "A blinding flash to confuse a man in a clean lane. Put that boy in a muddy ditch against twenty pikemen with ash shafts and no sun, and he will die squealing in the briars."

A soft rustle of silk sounded behind me.

I turned. 

Lady Vivienne Montclair stood in the arch of the gallery, leaning against the stone with a wine cup in her pale hand. 

She wore a blue riding dress, her black hair pinned with silver needles. Her grey eyes were cold, looking at me with the high pride of an academic. Yet under her white powder, I saw the twitch in her left lid and the grey hue around her mouth—the unmistakable rot of a caster starving for fresh catalyst stones.

"You look grim for a guest, Lucan," she said, her voice smooth as cream. "Or do they call you 'Lord Factor Holt' now? Word travels fast. They say you took that damp stone pile by the coal sumps."

I did not move. I kept my hands folded.

"The tower is dry, Lady Vivienne," I said. "And the stone is sound. Unlike your party's purse."

Her smile faded. "Do not flatter yourself with petty court tricks. A temporary hold on a wagon of copper is a brief delay, nothing more. Once the Archon ratifies Lysander’s win today, Lord Belmonte will speak to the council. Your writ will die with a stroke of the pen."

"Lord Belmonte will not speak to the council," I said.

Vivienne let out a dry sniff, sipping her wine. "And why not? He is Lysander’s greatest patron."

"Because Lord Belmonte has spent the morning burning his letters," I told her, my voice level and cold. "Three hours ago, *The Golden Lion Factor* bought forty thousand silver crowns of Belmonte’s bad paper from the northern exchange. At noon tomorrow, my bailiffs will seize his salt mines on the river for debt. If he speaks to the Archon for Lysander, I will take his house before sunset."

Vivienne’s wine cup stopped at her lips. 

Her grey eyes searched my face. She looked for the nervous farm boy who used to stutter when she demanded hot tea. She looked for the timid clerk who shrank from her gaze.

She found only cold stone.

"Who are you?" she whispered, her smooth mask slipping. "Lucan Holt could not count sacks without sweating."

"I manage the contracts of Dun-Carrow," I told her, stepping past her toward the gallery stairs. "And your company owes fourteen hundred silver crowns to the wharf factors. Advise your captain to settle the interest before the week ends."

I walked down the stone steps into the crowded avenue, turned my collar against the damp river wind, and headed back toward the counting house to review the evening manifests.
