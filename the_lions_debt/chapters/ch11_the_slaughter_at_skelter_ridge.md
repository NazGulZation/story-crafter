# The Slaughter at Skelter-Ridge

At the Battle of the Blackwater, Stannis Baratheon thought his vanguard had taken the gates of King’s Landing. His men swarmed over the burning hulks of royal galleys, howling hymns to their red god, sure that the capital and the Iron Throne lay open to their blades. They had thrown every company, every pikeman, and every mercenary into a headlong rush toward the Mud Gate, forgetting that an army committed entirely to a frontal charge possesses neither flanks nor rear guard. Inside the Red Keep, Queen Cersei sat in Maegor’s Holdfast drinking sour Arbor gold, clutching a vial of nightshade, ready to poison young Tommen rather than face the rabble. Cowards surrender to despair before the steel meets; fools throw away their lives for noise.

I brought the combined host of Casterly Rock and the Reach down upon their unarmored flank at dusk. We struck their exposed lines with twenty thousand heavy horsemen in one crushing hammer blow. Stannis's line dissolved into the smoking mud of the riverbanks. We offered no parleys, stopped for no speeches, and fought no duels between rival lords. We executed a methodical, mechanical butchery with cold iron and armored horse, driving five thousand drowning men into the black waters of the bay. When the night ended, the boy Joffrey sat his throne, the Tyrells were wed to our cause, and the realm learned that salvation belongs to the man who brings the reserves.

War is a butcher's yard. The man who keeps his head takes the skins of the reckless.

***

The basin of Skelter-Ridge was a vast, shallow saucer of grey glacial shale, ringed by jagged walls of black basalt that trapped the storm like wine in a cracked cup.

When our main host broke through the frozen defile at noon, the valley before us was an open slaughterhouse.

Two feet of powdery snow covered the ground, churned into thick red slush by the boots and claws of eight thousand fighting men and beasts. Broken supply wagons lay overturned on their sides, canvas ripped to shreds by the wind, spilling velvet doublets, silver mirrors, and hair grease into the frozen mud. Dead chargers lay stiff in their embroidered caparisons, bellies torn open by orc axes, steam rising from their guts into the freezing sub-zero air. Wounded horses shrieked in the drifts, their legs snapped by jagged rocks, kicking blindly at the grey sky.

The vanguard of the *Dawn-Seekers* was finished as a fighting force.

Three hundred knights and palace retainers huddled in a tight, desperate ring around a rocky knoll in the middle of the basin. Four thousand frost-orcs circled them, huge brute warriors clad in cured mammoth pelts and plates of hammered black iron, swinging jagged bone poleaxes and barbed harpoons. The orcs moved with the patient malice of wolves around a trapped herd, prodding the defenders with javelins, waiting for the cold to do the heavy work.

The cold was the deadliest enemy on the field. At thirty degrees below freezing, men without hot rations, thick socks, or dry boots lost the feeling in their fingers within an hour. The knights' fine gilded plate armor had become an iron freezer, leaching warmth from their ribs, freezing their sweat-soaked undershirts into stiff sheets of abrasive ice. Their fingers were swollen and blue; their breath rattled in their throats; half of them could barely lift their swords above their waists.

On the rocky knoll, the famous Sun-Sword of the Goddess sputtered and died.

Lysander Morvan stood atop a granite boulder, his golden curls matted with black blood and frozen slush, his fine sky-blue mantle shredded by crossbow quarrels. He swung his six-foot blade in wild, exhausted arcs, but the bright solar light was gone. In the freezing mist of the glacier, the weapon cast only a sickly yellow glow that hissed and sputtered whenever an orc axe struck its flat. His face was pale as suet, his eyes darting across the circle of dead squires, searching frantically for a miracle that would not come.

Beside him, Vivienne Montclair was on her knees in the snow, coughing hard, her breath wheezing through bloody lips. Her dark hair hung in matted knots over her face. Her hands clawed weakly at an empty velvet pouch at her belt. She had burned through her last smuggled frost-crystal an hour ago; now her body was paying the alchemical bill. Blood leaked continuously from her nose and ears, staining the snow scarlet as arcane tremors racked her bones. She could not spark a candle.

Ten yards down the slope, Kallista Kroll cursed as she tried to string her recurve horn bow. The extreme cold had crystallized the animal glue inside the wood. With a sharp crack like a pistol shot, the limbs snapped, the hemp cord whipping across her face and reopening her old scar into a sheet of red. She threw the broken wood into the slush and drew a notched broad dagger, her boots slipping in the blood of her fallen archers.

Under an overturned baggage cart crouched Rosamund Cleary.

She wore a tattered green wool dress, clutching dirty linen rags to her chest. Her hazel eyes were round with animal terror, watching orcs smash squires with bone clubs ten paces away. She had pulled a greasy horse blanket over her head, shivering so hard her teeth clicked like dice in an iron cup.

Then Lysander looked south toward the ridge.

He saw my main host deploying in the pass: four thousand men in dark wool and iron brigandine, advancing with the mechanical precision of an iron wall. Behind the lines moved two hundred supply wains, their canvas covers intact, their teams of heavy oxen steaming in the cold.

Lysander did not counter-attack. He did not attempt to rally his surviving knights. He did not reach down to pull Vivienne or Rosamund from the blood-slicked mud.

He vaulted from the boulder, landed in the saddle of his unarmored reserve charger—a swift, pure-blooded courser he had kept tethered behind the rock—and dug his spurs deep into the beast's ribs.

"Make way! For the glory of the Dawn!" Lysander screamed, his voice cracking into high, hysterical panic.

He abandoned his men. He abandoned his women. He trampled two dying squires beneath his horse's hooves, broke through a momentary gap in the orc ring, and bolted at full gallop into the blizzard toward the western hills like a whipped cur running from the whip.

A collective groan of despair broke from the trapped knights on the knoll.

"The coward..." Captain Korgan spat into the shale beside my horse. "Left his own people to die like sheep in a ditch. And after all his hymns to the sun."

"He acted like himself," I said, keeping my gelding still upon the crest. "A peacock runs when the fox enters the yard. Sound the horn."

I raised my right hand.

Beside me, Master Garrow blew three deep, heavy blasts on the great bronze horn of the syndicate. The notes rolled across the basin, cutting through the screaming blizzard like iron bells.

"Pikes in the center," I commanded, my voice flat as cold slate. "Four ranks deep. Shields locked. Scorpions to the flanks. Forward at a walk."

Two thousand heavy pikemen of Dun-Carrow stepped down into the basin in tight, steady lines.

Their boots came down together—*thud, thud, thud*—in the marching step drilled into them over three weeks of winter road. Every man had a hot meal of bacon, peas, and barley in his belly; every pike was forged from tough Norrmark bog-iron that would not snap in the frost; every file was locked shoulder to shoulder behind four-foot oak shields braced with iron bands. Behind them marched six hundred City Wardens, holding the reserve with short spears and heavy bucklers.

Warlord Mor-Goth turned his frost-orcs from the slaughter on the knoll to face the new line.

The orc chief stood nine feet tall atop a massive white winter warg, wearing iron plates and a cuirass strung with the skulls of imperial officers. He bellowed a deafening challenge, pointing his jagged iron poleaxe at our center.

Three thousand orcs turned and charged the ridge, howling like wolves tearing at an elk's carcass.

They came on like a tidal wave of fur, bone, and iron, their heavy boots drumming on the shale, expecting the human line to break and run as the peasant levies had done at the river forts.

I did not move. I did not draw steel.

"Hold the line," I said.

Two hundred yards. A hundred and fifty yards. A hundred yards.

"Scorpions," I ordered.

On both flanks of the host, forty repeating scorpions spanned by heavy iron levers let fly simultaneously.

Forty heavy steel quarrels, two feet long and tipped with incendiary sulfur pitch, hissed through the storm like diving hawks.

The iron points drove clean through bone breastplates, pinning warriors together like dried beetles on pins. The pitch flared on impact, catching the greasy mammoth furs and bear fat, turning forty charging beasts into thrashing, screaming torches. The smell of burning grease and scorched hide rolled across the snow in a black, choking cloud.

"Wind the gears," Garrow shouted from the wing. "Loose!"

Another forty bolts slammed into the charging pack. Then another. The repeating winches turned without pause, laying down a sheet of iron and fire that broke the charge fifty paces from our front rank. The screams of burning wargs echoed off the black cliffs.

"Pikes, plant!" Korgan bellowed.

A thousand men dropped to their right knee in the red slush, the butts of their twelve-foot ash poles braced against iron footing pegs driven into the shale. The ranks behind leveled their steel points over their shoulders, creating an impenetrable hedgehog bristling with four tiers of cold Norrmark iron.

The orc vanguard slammed into the pikes.

The shock sounded like a ship breaking upon a reef.

Dozens of charging orcs ran straight onto three pike points at once, their own charging weight driving the iron clean through their ribs and out their backs. The front rank held behind their locked shields, taking the bone-jarring blow without giving an inch of ground. The iron bands on the shields groaned under the impact of mammoth-bone clubs, but the line did not yield.

"Thrust!" Korgan shouted.

"Hah!" two thousand men roared together, pulling back their pikes and driving them forward in rhythm.

*Thrust. Pull. Thrust. Pull.*

Behind the pikes, eight hundred syndicate crossbowmen stepped up, shooting into the dense, milling press over our men's heads. At twenty paces, every four-ounce quarrel found an eye, a throat, or a belly. Orcs fell in heaps, their corpses forming a low rampart of dead meat that tangled the feet of the warriors behind them.

Warlord Mor-Goth saw his horde melting into the snow before an iron machine. In a blind rage, he drove his giant warg over the pile of corpses, leaping straight at our center.

The beast landed on two shields, claws splintering the oak, crushing two pikemen beneath its bulk. Mor-Goth swung his heavy poleaxe to cleave through the gap.

He never finished the swing.

Captain Korgan and six veteran arbalestiers stepped forward from the second rank. At point-blank range, six heavy bolts hit the warlord at once: two in the chest, one through the throat, and three into the skull of his white mount.

The giant warg hit the red slush like a falling pine. Mor-Goth crashed down, choking on his own black blood, his poleaxe slipping from his dead fingers.

The remaining orcs saw their chief go down.

Their courage broke like dry twigs. The great northern horde scattered, turning in blind animal terror to flee back toward the northern glaciers.

"Hold the line," I commanded, raising my hand to halt the horns. "Chasing them into a blizzard will only kill our horses and freeze our men. Secure the knoll. Finish the wounded beasts on the field. Bring in the survivors."

The battle of Skelter-Ridge was done.

Four thousand frost-orcs lay dead in the red snow. My host had forty-one hurt, mostly broken bones and split shields from the first crash.

It was cold, clean butcher's work, bought with wagons, bread, and steady ranks of iron.

I rode my grey gelding down the slope toward the center of the knoll.

The snow fell softly now, covering the dead like white cloth. Around us, pioneer crews were already stripping the dead orcs of iron plates, piling their mammoth tusks onto sledges, and stacking the bodies for burning with wood-pitch. Quartermasters moved between our ranks with wooden buckets of hot broth, handing tin cups to the shivering pikemen.

At the base of the rock, my guards had gathered what was left of the vanguard.

Vivienne Montclair sat in the slush against a broken cart wheel, staring with blank, unseeing eyes at the frostbite eating her fingers, her silk dress stained with vomit and blood. Kallista Kroll stood nearby with her wrists tied behind her back with tarred rope, her jaw set in hard, bitter defeat, blood dripping from her split nose.

And kneeling in the slush on her hands and knees was Rosamund Cleary.

She looked up as my horse's hooves crunched on the crust of snow. Her hazel eyes found my face, wide with terror, her lips parting as if to beg for mercy, but only a dry rasp came from her throat.

I gave her no reply.

I signaled the provost sergeant to place the survivors under guard, turned my horse back toward the ridge crest, and rode over to inspect the captured baggage carts before night fell.
