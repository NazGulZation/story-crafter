# The Rains of the Red Kilns

When Lord Roger Reyne of Castamere took the field against my house, he believed that centuries of Lannister gold had softened our blood into water. My father Tytos had let Lord Roger laugh in his face, had forgiven his unpaid loans, and had allowed the red lion of Castamere to strut through the West as if Casterly Rock were an almshouse for insolent vassals. When Tytos sent riders asking for repayment, Lord Roger sent back letters dripping with polite mockery, promising silver in the spring while using Lannister coin to hire hedge knights and fortify his stone halls. Lord Roger assumed that a lord who smiles at an insult will smile at outright rebellion.

I rode to Tarbeck Hall with two thousand heavy horse and twenty siege engines. I threw down its stone walls in an afternoon, crushed Lady Ellyn beneath the collapse of her own roof-tree, and put her brothers to the sword. At Castamere, when the Reynes retreated into their deep subterranean gold mines, barred the iron oak gates, and offered terms of conditional surrender, I did not bother to unroll their parchment. I put five hundred miners to work diverting the stream. The water rushed down the mine shafts, drowning three hundred men, women, and children in the dark. Their bones are still down there, locked behind fifty feet of solid rock.

When men see that a lord forgives a debt, they laugh. When they laugh, they take his dignity; and when they take his dignity, they take his head. A debtor must learn that paying in full is the kindest mercy he will ever get from House Lannister.

***

The dining hall of House Belmonte smelled of beeswax tapers, roasted suckling pig glazed with spiced plums, and lavender water sprinkled over damp silk.

A hearth of carved green marble blazed at the end of the long chamber, throwing warm orange light across tapestries depicting ancient sea fights that the current lords of the house had never seen. At a table of polished rosewood sat Lord Raymond Belmonte, a fat, perfumed lord of forty-five winters whose soft, ringed fingers tapped nervously against a fluted silver cup filled with sweet southern vintage. Three liveried footmen stood behind his chair, carving roasted capons and pouring wine into fluted crystal.

Across from him sat Lysander Morvan. The Sun-Gilded Champion wore a velvet cloak dyed imperial violet, his golden curls freshly washed and oiled by palace barbers, trying to mask the bitter sting of his late defeat at the North Palisade. Beside Lysander sat Rosamund Cleary in a low-cut gown of crimson satin that clung tightly to her waist. Her hazel eyes darted over the silver platters with the anxious greed of a village root-girl desperate to look noble, her neck adorned with a triple strand of freshwater pearls purchased from a street jeweler on credit.

I walked in without knocking.

The heavy walnut doors swung wide, letting in a gust of freezing night wind that set the tapers dancing and made the hearth flare with spitting sparks. Four of my syndicate guards in dark Norrmark wool stood in the antechamber, their heavy arbalests resting unstrung across their arms, their eyes fixed on Belmonte's domestic retainers.

I walked down the long length of the woven southern carpet, the heels of my greased leather boots thudding softly on the oak floor joists.

I wore no velvet, no gold embroidery, no crests. My doublet was plain Norrmark wool, dark and stiff-collared, buttoned straight to the throat with unpolished horn.

Lord Belmonte froze, his silver fork hovering above his glazed pork. "What is this? Who admitted this... this fellow into my private hall?"

Lysander stood so fast his heavy oak chair scraped loud against the floorboards. His hand went straight to his belt, though he had been forced to leave his great Sun-Sword in the entry hall per domestic house rules.

"Lucan," Lysander sneered, puffing out his armored chest and forcing a loud, theatrical laugh. "You have grown remarkably bold for a cast-off clerk. You dare barge into a noble hall during a private supper?"

Rosamund laughed, a sharp, nervous screech that echoed off the high plaster ceiling. "Look at him, Lord Belmonte! The peasant thinks a few sacks of bruised grain give him the right to walk into a grand salon! Call the guards! Drag him to the stable yard and whip him until his back bleeds!"

I did not look at Rosamund. I did not look at Lysander.

I walked to the foot of the rosewood table, pulled out a high-backed walnut chair, turned it deliberately so it faced Lord Belmonte, and sat down.

I folded my hands over my knee and met the fat lord's watery brown eyes.

"Lord Raymond Belmonte," I said, my baritone low, gravelly, and flat as slate. "You sent a ducal bailiff to the Skelter wharves three hours ago. The man carried a warrant bearing your personal seal, ordering the seizure of sixteen grain barges belonging to *The Golden Lion Factor*."

Belmonte swallowed hard, setting his silver cup down with a sharp clatter. He puffed up his double chin, trying to muster the haughty disdain of an imperial magistrate.

"I did," Belmonte declared, waving a soft, ring-laden hand. "The Grand Duchy is at war. Warlord Mor-Goth threatens our northern borders. As a Member of the Archon's Sovereign Council and Chief Patron of the *Dawn-Seeker* Company, I exercised the Crown’s right of eminent domain. Your grain is requisitioned for the war effort. You will receive promissory scrip redeemable at the end of the winter campaign."

"Promissory scrip," I repeated softly. "Drawn upon which treasury?"

"The Sovereign Treasury of Dun-Carrow, of course!" Belmonte snapped, his jowls quivering.

"The Sovereign Treasury possesses ninety-four silver crowns and forty bushels of weevil-eaten oats," I told him, leaning back slightly in the walnut chair. "Its debts exceed four hundred thousand gold ducats. Your promissory scrip is toilet paper, Lord Belmonte. And you know it."

Belmonte’s face reddened to the hue of his glazed plum sauce. "How dare you speak to an imperial lord in such terms! You are an upstart merchant factor! A common adventurer who washed up from the Weald with mud on his boots!"

"You borrowed twelve thousand gold ducats from the Norrmark Banking house four years ago to finance your salt-mining concessions at the Skelter Estuary," I said, ignoring his shout completely. "You pledged the title deeds to your ancestral quarries, your fleet of six coastal caravels, and this very townhouse as collateral."

Belmonte went rigid. The color began draining from his forehead, leaving his skin looking like cold dough.

"The Norrmark notes were renewed annually at eight percent interest," I continued, tracing a dark vein in the rosewood table with my index finger. "However, your payments lapsed four moons ago. The Norrmark consortium grew weary of your excuses, your tourney sponsorships, and your unpaid promises."

I reached inside my dark wool doublet, withdrew a folded parchment bound with black silk ribbon, and dropped it onto Belmonte’s silver plate, directly atop his half-eaten pork.

The warm grease soaked instantly through the calfskin, staining the red wax seal pressed into its corner.

Belmonte stared at the seal. His jaw fell open, his jowls trembling like blancmange.

"The Great Lion of Norrmark..." he whispered, his voice cracking into a dry squeak.

"Three days ago, *The Golden Lion Factor* purchased every defaulted debt marker issued by House Belmonte across the northern exchanges," I said. "I bought them at a thirty percent discount. I now hold forty-six thousand silver crowns of your personal obligations, matured, past due, and subject to statutory collection under the *Imperial Maritime Code*."

Lysander slammed his heavy fist onto the table, making the wine goblets jump and slosh red onto the white linen. "This is trickery! A scrap of paper means nothing! Lord Belmonte is an imperial peer! The Archon will never allow a common clerk to seize a noble house!"

I finally turned my head and looked at Lysander.

"A lord who cannot pay his debts is only a beggar in velvet, Morvan," I said with freezing finality.

Rosamund stood up, her face white with wild, sudden panic. She grabbed Lysander's arm, her fingers digging through his violet velvet sleeve. "Lysander, kill him! Draw your dagger and kill him! He is alone! He has no right to touch our patron! If Belmonte falls, who pays for my gowns? Who pays for our horses?"

"If anyone draws steel in this room," I said without shifting an inch, "my crossbowmen will execute every living soul here before your next breath."

Silence fell like a slab of granite.

The hearth embers popped, sending a shower of sparks up the green chimney. Outside, the rain beat hard against the leaded glass windows, cold, heavy, and relentless.

Belmonte picked up the greasy parchment with shaking hands. His eyes darted across the rows of precise arithmetic: the principal sums, the compounded interest, the legal penalties, the total demand for forty-six thousand crowns.

"What... what do you want?" Belmonte stammered, sweat beading thick across his brow, running down into his eyebrows. "I can find coin. I can sell the spice ships arriving from the south in the spring—"

"There will be no spice ships," I said. "Your credit across the Skelter is dead. Tomorrow morning at dawn, my bailiffs will take possession of the Skelter Estuary salt mines. The miners will answer to my overseers. The revenues will flow into my counting house. Your six caravels are now registered under the flag of *The Golden Lion Factor*."

"My salt mines..." Belmonte choked, clutching his chest with both hands as if his heart were giving out. "That is the lifeblood of my house! If you take the salt, House Belmonte is ruined! We will have nothing! We will be beggars!"

"You will have your life," I replied. "Provided you sign the dissolution of your sponsorship of the *Dawn-Seekers* tonight."

Lysander took a step forward, his jaw tight with fury. "You cannot do this! The party needs Belmonte's name to pass our supply wagons through the city gates!"

"You have no wagons, Morvan," I said, rising slowly from the walnut chair. "You have no iron. You have no grain. You have only that glowing toy at your hip, and the vanity that blinds you to your own folly."

I walked around the table until I stood beside Belmonte’s chair. The fat lord cowered back into his embroidered cushions as if an executioner had laid a cold iron glove upon his neck.

"In my past life," I said softly, my voice carrying the ancient, bloody memory of the Westerlands, "there was a proud lord who ruled a great red fortress called Castamere. He wore red velvet, borrowed gold he could not repay, and laughed at my father when my father asked for his due."

Belmonte looked up at me with wide, terrified eyes. "What... what happened to him?"

"He laughed until the water reached his chin," I whispered, leaning low until my breath stirred the grey hairs around his ear. "I turned the river into his mines. I drowned three hundred of his kin beneath the earth. Their bones are still there, rotting in the dark. No one sings for House Reyne today, Lord Belmonte. They sing only of the rains that washed them away."

I tapped the greasy parchment on his plate with my iron stylus.

"Sign the transfer of the salt mines. Or I will buy this palace by noon tomorrow, pull down its stone walls, and plant turnips where you eat."

Belmonte did not hesitate. He scrambled for his silver quill, his hands shaking so violently that black ink splashed across the white tablecloth and spattered his cuff. He pressed his family gold signet ring into the hot red wax with a dry, broken sob.

I picked up the signed deed, blew dry river sand across the wet ink, and tucked the parchment inside my wool doublet.

Then I turned and looked at Rosamund Cleary.

She stood frozen beside Lysander, her fingernails digging deep into the crimson satin of her skirts. The haughty, mocking sneer had completely vanished from her features, replaced by the sheer, sickening horror of a girl watching the ground open beneath her feet. The silk, the pearls, the fine rooms, the easy life of a hero's kept woman—all of it had been bought with borrowed silver. And the bill had just been paid in full.

She opened her mouth to speak—to plead, to beg, to offer some desperate excuse.

I did not give her the chance.

I turned my back on them, stepped through the double walnut doors, and walked down the marble portico into the freezing rain of Dun-Carrow. 

A covered carriage drawn by two stout black geldings waited by the curb, steam rising from the horses' wet coats. Captain Korgan stood by the carriage door, his hand resting on the hilt of his sword, water dripping from the brim of his iron helmet. Inside the carriage sat High Factor Morcar, wrapped in a sheepskin mantle, an open strongbox resting across his knees.

"Did the fat lord sign?" Morcar asked, his voice shaking with nervous tension as I stepped inside and pulled the door shut against the gale.

I reached inside my coat and placed the signed calfskin deed into the strongbox.

Morcar leaned close to the oil lamp, adjusting his wire spectacles. His eyes traced the signature, the lead-stamped clauses, and the crimson seal of House Belmonte. A slow, incredulous whistle broke from his lips.

"The Skelter Estuary salt mines," Morcar whispered, touching the wax with a trembling finger. "And six sea-going caravels. Holt... you have broken the chief patron of the *Dawn-Seekers* in a single evening. What happens to the hero now?"

"Without Belmonte's purse, Morvan cannot pay his stable fees or buy oats for his horses," I replied, pulling my woolen cloak tight as the carriage lurched forward over the wet cobbles. "His mercenary archers will desert within a month. His sorceress will run out of crystals. And the girl will learn the true value of a champion who fights on credit."

"And the salt mines, Lord Factor?" Korgan asked from his horse beside the window.

"Take fifty syndicate guards to the estuary at first light," I commanded. "Take possession of the brine pans, the drying kilns, and the storehouses. Post my muster rolls at the gates. If Belmonte's stewards resist, put them in irons. The salt belongs to the Golden Lion now."

The carriage rolled down the steep hill, the iron-shod wheels rattling against the wet cobblestones of the Lower Ward. 

I leaned back against the leather bench, watching the rain wash across the glass panes. Tomorrow morning, fifty miners and six carters would be waiting at the factory gates to take over the estuary workings.

I pulled my wool mantle tight across my knees and let the horses carry us through the fog toward the warmth of the counting house.
