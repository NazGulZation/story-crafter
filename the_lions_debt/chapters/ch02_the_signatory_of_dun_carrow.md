# The Signatory of Dun-Carrow

In the second year of my rule as Hand of the King to Aerys Targaryen, the harbor masters of Oldtown tried to dodge the crown dues at King's Landing. They came to the Red Keep with chests of silver and slick smiles, bragging of old deals struck with my predecessors over cups of sweet wine. My father Tytos had spent his life nodding to such men, trading Lannister honor for a merchant's grin and a handful of empty promises. 

I met them in the council chamber without offering a drop of wine. I had Grand Maester Pycelle unroll the royal charters signed by King Jaehaerys, line by line, holding the Great Seal before their noses. A spoken promise dies the moment the breath leaves a man’s mouth; a pact pressed into calfskin outlives fire, plague, and war. When the factors saw the black ink and the unbroken lead seal, their knees hit the stone. They paid forty thousand gold sovereigns in back taxes before the tide turned.

Power sits where men think it sits, and in any land built on trade, men fear the weight of an inked signature.

Rain fell over the Lower Wharves in grey sheets, turning the stones into slick tracks of mud and coal dust. 

The Skelter River met the salt tide with a low suck, bumping fifty heavy timber barges against the slimy posts. Dockers in wool caps dragged barrels of salt fish and marsh-hay up the mud, their breath smoking in the cold. The wind smelled of pitch, river slime, and wet horse dung steaming on the stones.

I walked down the steep steps of King’s Stairs, my boots steady on the wet rock. 

A weak boy would have run to an alehouse. The boy who owned this skin had spent his nights in low cellars, nursing sour cider while listening to teamsters tell dirty jokes, dreaming of the day Rosamund Cleary might touch his arm without asking for a favor. 

I did not seek an alehouse. Power in this town, as in the one I left behind, lived in ink, wax seals, and cold arithmetic.

I reached the Grand Assize of Dun-Carrow three bells past dark.

The hall was a huge stone barn of black rock, squatting by the docks like a turtle in armor. Iron pots flared outside its heavy oak doors, spitting black pine smoke into the rain. Inside, forty goose quills scratched across damp parchment. Abacus beads clicked like dry teeth, and clerks cursed under their breath as they weighed cargo sheets.

I walked up to the High Clerk’s counter.

Behind a slab of dark walnut sat an old factor named Morcar. His skin looked like salt pork that had spent six weeks in a cellar. He wore the black wool robe and lead collar of the Inland Trust, his watery eyes peering through round glass lenses framed in brass wire.

"Registry shuts at four bells, lad," Morcar said without looking up, his quill scratching down a line of figures. "If you’re logging a rowboat or five tubs of river carp, dump the paper in the wicker basket and bring two coppers tomorrow."

I laid my left hand flat on the wood.

The boy Lucan had carried a leather pouch inside his shirt for three years, guarding it with his life while wolves scratched at the camp tents. Inside lay the parchment that kept the *Dawn-Seekers* from falling apart.

I pulled out the scroll and rolled it across Morcar’s open book, pressing the corners flat with my fingers.

"Read," I said.

My voice hit the stone counter like an iron hammer on an empty vault.

Morcar froze. His quill stopped above the page, a black drop of ink trembling on the nib. He pushed his glass frames up his crooked nose and tilted his head, squinting at the red wax seal pressed into the tail of the skin.

"The Charter of Free Venture," he muttered, his voice dropping as he read the old script. "Signed in the third year of Archon Althaus... The *Dawn-Seeker* Warband. Company of the fourth rank."

"Read the names," I said.

Morcar leaned in, his dirty thumb tracing the lines. "Lysander Morvan, Knight of the Sun-Lance, War Leader. Vivienne Montclair, Mistress of the First Circle. Kallista Kroll, Scout Captain. Lucan Holt... Legal Factor, Quartermaster, and Sole Signatory of Record."

He blinked, looking from the seal to my face. "You’re Holt?"

"I am."

"Lysander Morvan logged a claim two hours ago," Morcar said, his eyes narrowing with sharp suspicion. "His boy ran down from the Sun-Lance Pavilion. The company took the Red Kilns. Twelve thousand gold ducats in copper bars, red ore, and four magic stones pulled from the old tombs. The boy said the whole lot was cleared to sell through House Belmonte’s counting house."

"Morvan lied," I said.

Morcar leaned back in his oak chair, folding his hands over his belly. A thin, hungry grin touched his lips. On these docks, an old clerk fed on fights the way gulls fed on fish guts.

"The claim has Morvan’s gold ring stamped in the wax," Morcar said.

"A ring is a toy," I said, my voice dropping low and cold. "Read Section Fourteen of the City War Code. Seventh line. *'No loot, salvaged bullion, or raw ore taken under a ducal war-charter may be sold, pledged, or moved without the seal and signature of the named Company Factor.'*"

Morcar’s grin stiffened. He reached for a fat, pigskin book on the side shelf—the *Law of the High Port*. He licked his thumb, flipped sixty pages of cramped script, and found the line.

His eyes scanned the words. The color drained out of his cheeks.

"A full hold," Morcar whispered.

"A total lock," I said. "Without my mark, the Red Kiln haul is stolen goods in the eyes of the court. Any bank or merchant who touches that metal commits theft against the Admiralty Bench. The penalty is the loss of the whole cargo, plus a forty percent fine paid in silver to the city vaults."

Morcar shoved his glasses up his nose, staring at me as if a wolf had walked into his office dressed in a carter's coat. "Morvan has the temple at his back. He has Lord Belmonte. Belmonte sits on the Archon’s council."

"Belmonte is broke," I said. "He pays his whores with paper drawn on next year's salt pan. If he touches that copper without my stamp, your Inland Trust can seize his docks at the North Quay for debt. You know the law, Morcar. And you know what your trust gains if Belmonte trips."

The old man went stone still. The scratch of quills around the room seemed to fade into the rain drumming on the slate roof.

Morcar was no fool. For twenty years, the Inland Trust had fought House Belmonte for the river tolls. Lysander was Belmonte's pet dog—a pretty boy whose fame let Belmonte borrow more silver than he could ever repay.

"What do you want, Holt?" Morcar asked, his voice low. "You didn't come here to rot in an assize pen while lawyers chew the fat."

"Three things," I said, leaning in until my face was a foot from his spectacles. "First, a court writ locking the Red Kiln haul in the city vaults tonight. Not a single bar of copper leaves the carts tomorrow."

"Done," Morcar said, his hand already grabbing a blank writ. "That’s standard law. What else?"

"A new trade house. *The Golden Lion Factor*. Registered in my name, backed by the seal of your Inland Trust."

Morcar frowned, his brows pulling tight. "A factor needs coin, boy. You're a kicked-out clerk with mud on your boots."

"I hold Lysander Morvan by the throat," I said without blinking. "The Red Kiln haul is worth twelve thousand ducats. Under the law, my share is thirty-five percent—four thousand two hundred gold ducats. I will sell seventy percent of my claim to your trust tonight, for fifteen hundred silver crowns in hard coin, pushed across this counter within the hour."

Morcar sucked in his breath. His tongue wet his cracked lip. 

The math was ruthless. For fifteen hundred silver crowns, the Inland Trust bought a prime claim on three thousand gold ducats, while snapping the spine of their biggest rival.

"And your third demand?" Morcar asked, his pen shaking as he dipped it in red ink.

"An office," I said. "The Old Imperial Toll-House by the north wall. The three-story stone tower over the river gate. It has stood empty since the flood. Your trust holds the deed for back taxes."

"The roof leaks," Morcar muttered, trying to fight for an inch of ground. "The cellar has two feet of river mud."

"Stone does not rot," I told him. "Sign the deed and bring up the silver from the iron room. Clean coin. Norrmark silver, unclipped and heavy. I will take no clipped local trash."

The old factor stared into my eyes. 

He looked for the trembling boy who had hauled bags behind Lysander Morvan for three years. He found nothing. He saw only the cold resolve of a man who had built kingdoms, moved armies, and buried great houses without blinking.

"Bring the silver," Morcar barked to a clerk behind the screen. "And heat the red wax."

An hour later, I walked out of the Grand Assize into the dark.

In my coat, I carried the deed to the Old Imperial Toll-House, stamped with the lead seal of the port. Behind me, two heavy porters in oiled capes carried an iron-bound chest holding fifteen hundred silver crowns.

Up on the hill, the lights of the Sun-Lance Pavilion blazed through the rain. 

Lysander Morvan was drinking sweet wine, bragging of his kill in the Red Kilns, surrounded by fawning sycophants and the milkmaid who thought she had traded up in the world. 

They thought they had tossed out an errand boy. 

Tomorrow morning, the city bailiffs would arrive at their compound gate with iron padlocks and a court order of seizure.

The porters hauled the heavy coin chest up the granite steps of the Old Imperial Toll-House, their boots splashing through the puddles on the quay. I unlocked the heavy oak door, pushed it open against the damp smell of stone and river mud, and told the watchman to light the hearth brazier. We had work to do before the morning bells.
