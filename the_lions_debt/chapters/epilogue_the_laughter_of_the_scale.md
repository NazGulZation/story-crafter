# Epilogue: The Laughter of the Scale

Seven high-backed thrones stood upon the crystal rim of the Empyrean Dome, arranged in a wide semicircle above the well of mortal mirrors. Each chair had been cut from a different foundation of the cosmos. 

To my left sat the Lord of the Deeps, carved from greenish sea-basalt that dripped perpetual brine upon the floor flags. Beside him, the Lady of the Sown Furrows rested in her seat of petrified oak, smelling of wet loam, crushed barley, and summer threshing floors. Farther down the arc sat the God of the Red Forge, leaning against blackened meteorite iron that radiated the heat of an open smelting hearth, while the Mistress of the Shrouded Loom smoothed her robes of starless void, her spider-bone shuttles clicking in her lap. Next to her was the Warden of the Gray Gate, perched upon pale chalk limestone, his cowl shadowed, holding his leaden key across his knees.

Then there was the Lady of the Dawn.

Her chair was wrought from electrum and raw solar crystal, glowing with such ferocious brilliance that the air around her shivered with heat.

And then there was mine.

My seat was cut from dark slate and banded with cold Norrmark iron. Across my lap rested the great Bronze Balance, its twin pans hanging in exact equilibrium from chains of woven electrum. In my right hand rested the heavy iron stylus; upon the cedar pedestal at my elbow lay the Great Ledger of Covenants, bound in oiled pigskin and closed with a silver hasp.

The chamber smelled of scorched myrrh, sharp ozone, hot sulfur, and the sour tang of celestial temper.

"An outrage!" the Lady of the Dawn shrieked.

Her voice hit the vaulted quartz ceiling like a brass horn blown directly into an ear canal. She had been on her feet for the better part of an hour, pacing the platform until the crystal under her golden sandals was scorched black with soot. The radiant corona around her brow flared in jagged, uneven spikes of orange and white flame, throwing angry sparks into the hair of the Forge God, who merely grunted and brushed them away with a calloused thumb.

"A humiliation! A mockery of divine selection!" She spun around, pointing a trembling, incandescent finger down into the steaming basin of the scrying well. "Look at him! Look at my boy! Look at what that... that thieving, gray-faced parasite has done to the Sun-Gilded Lance of the Western Marches!"

I did not have to look into the water. I had kept the page open in my ledger since the winter solstice.

Down in the mortal realm, twenty feet beneath the low-tide swell of the Grey Gulf, Lysander Morvan stood upon the slick stone bench of his cell in the Black Bastion. Freezing ocean water surged through the iron drainage gratings, foaming around his armpits, coating his blond beard in gray scum and dead eel-grass. His shattered kneecaps, fused into lumpy knots of scarred gristle, screamed with agony every time a wave broke against his chest. When the tide receded, he would drop onto his belly in the mud, weeping into the foul straw, waiting for the jailer to throw down a crust of moldy barley biscuit.

My heart gave a delightful, violent flutter. 

Deep inside the marrow of my divine ribs, a quiet, bubbling tickle began to swell. I tightened my lips, smoothed the gray wool folds of my robe over my knees, and kept my face as placid as a frozen pond.

"Sister," I said, pitching my voice into that soft, melodic cadence that mortals use when calming a frightened mule. "We hear your distress. Truly, the sight stirs the spirit to pity."

*Pity!* In the silent chambers of my thoughts, I threw my head back and roared with hysterical, belly-aching laughter. 

*Pity for that strutting, brainless doll!* For four centuries, the Lady of the Dawn had paraded her golden-haired boys across the mortal plains. She picked them solely for their jawlines, the width of their shoulders, and their ability to shout righteous poetry while waving three feet of burning steel. She gave them miraculous blades and shiny armor, sent them out to chop down hill-clans, and then demanded eighty percent of the realm’s tithes to build three-story marble shrines in her honor. Her priests took out mortgages, ran up bills with every silversmith in Dun-Carrow, and sneered at the tax collectors because 'the radiant light pays all debts in glory.'

And now her glorious champion was chewing rat droppings in the dark.

The laughter boiled in my chest like new cider in a sealed cask. I had to press my knuckles firmly against my kneecap to stop my shoulders from shaking.

"Pity?" she screamed, her golden eyes blazing until the veins on her porcelain neck bulged like thick cords. "I do not want your pity, brother! I want thunder! I want solar fire! I want a pillar of white flame dropped squarely upon the Sea Citadel of Dun-Carrow! I want that miserable, cold-blooded usurer roasted until his skin peels like dry birch bark! I want his bones crushed into dust and scattered across the gulf!"

"Now, now," I murmured, offering her an open, sympathetic palm. "Let us examine the grievance with judicial care. Was there a violation of the Higher Compact? Did an outsider invoke dark sorcery from the forbidden pits? Did an elder demon breach the threshold of the world?"

"He used bog-iron!" she wailed, throwing her hands to the heavens in theatrical despair. "Forty tons of raw, unrefined river mud! He buried it under the courtroom floor like a common sewer grate! My sacred fire... my divine solar flame, blessed by my own lips during the feast of the summer solstice... it touched his floor and it went *pfft*! It hissed like a greasy sausage dropped into a wash-tub! In front of three hundred lords! In front of the bishops! In front of the guild-masters!"

She stomped her foot. The shockwave rattled the crystal rim of the well, sending a splash of fragrant, steaming water across the hem of the Harvest Goddess’s apron.

The Lady of the Sown Furrows did not flinch. She took a linen cloth from her pocket, dried the water from her fingers, and looked up with tired, earthy eyes.

"The granaries in Dun-Carrow are full, sister," the Harvest Goddess said, her voice heavy with the slow patience of ploughed soil. "Ninety thousand bushels of dry winter wheat. Forty thousand casks of salt herring. The bakers in the lower town are selling two-pound loaves for a single copper farthing. My people are not eating grass this winter. For the first time in sixty years, mothers are not burying their babes in the snow along the Skelter road. The boy you call a usurer fed them."

"He fed them with ledgers!" the Dawn Goddess spat, curling her lip in disgust. "With promissory notes and warehouse manifests! Where is the faith in that? Where is the awe? A mortal should look to the sky and weep with gratitude when the morning sun breaks through the clouds! They should fall on their faces and sing hymns of thanksgiving! Instead, what do they do now? They wake at dawn, check their wax tallies, take their wagons to the public weigh-station, and tip their caps to the Lord Protector’s bailiffs! It is vulgar! It is soulless!"

"It is four silver crowns a month, paid on the first morning of every moon without fail," the God of the Forge rumbled from across the circle. He spat a dark wad of burnt cinder into the crystal trench at his feet. "My smiths in the Foundry Quarter are burning hard coal instead of wet peat. They have twenty tons of high-grade Norrmark iron delivered to their doors every fortnight. When Lysander was prancing around the tourney fields, he owed the Guild of Armorers fourteen thousand crowns. The boy stole three suits of chased plate and forgot to pay for the rivets. A man who does not pay his smith is a thief in gold leaf. I have no quarrel with the new master."

"He broke the Sun-Sword!" the Dawn Goddess shrieked, her voice cracking into a ragged soprano sob. "On an anvil! In the middle of the hall! That was a holy relic! A sacred gift from the upper heavens!"

"He broke it with a sixteen-pound sledgehammer," the Forge God replied, a slow, appreciative grin splitting his soot-stained beard. "Master Beric struck the sweet spot right above the fuller. Good strike. Clean fracture. The iron core was too brittle anyway. I told you three hundred years ago that electrum plating ruins the temper of high-carbon steel, but you wanted it shiny."

A sound leaked out of me. 

It was half a snort, half a cough, muffled hastily behind my clenched fist.

The Lady of the Dawn whipped her head toward me, her golden brows knitting together with furious suspicion. "What was that?"

"A tickle in the throat, radiant sister," I replied instantly, widening my eyes with innocent concern. "The smoke from your righteous fury carries quite a draft. Please, continue."

Behind my solemn, attentive mask, my soul was writhing in pure ecstasy.

*He broke it with a hammer!* 

I remembered that moment with exquisite clarity. I had watched through the waters as that iron-willed spirit, commanding the slender vessel of Lucan Holt, stood five paces from the foot of the dais. He held no sorcerous charms. He chanted no prayers. His hands rested calmly behind his back under his lynx-lined mantle, looking upon the charging champion with the cold, bored irritation of an auditor discovering an uncrossed 't' in a laundry receipt.

And then: *clack*. 

The circuit closed. Forty tons of cold bog-iron connected by copper grounding cables to the river estuary.

The great and terrible wrath of the solar heavens, snuffed out like a tallow candle pinched between two wet fingers.

*Oh, it was magnificent.*

For three thousand years, these flashy, loud-mouthed divines had treated my domain like a despised scullery. They sneered at the scales. They mocked the ledgers. They treated contracts as paper trifles to be burned whenever a hero had a dramatic revelation or felt the urge to bed an innkeeper's daughter. *'The gods provide!'* their priests would shout. *'Faith moves mountains!'*

Draft mules hauled sixty wagons of winter grain through the frozen ruts of the Skelter Gorge. Shovels cleared the drifts. Seasoned timber braces held the collapsing road bridges. And disciplined wardens with hard silver in their purses guarded the baggage train while her golden boy chased songs across the snow.

The Lady of the Dawn took two furious strides toward my chair, her skirts of woven light trailing smoke across the floor. 

"You!" she hissed, pointing her slender finger at my nose. "You are behind this! I know your quiet, creeping ways, brother! You always favored those dry, ink-stained wretches who count beans in dark basements! You did something to the boy! Lucan Holt was a spineless little dog! He wept when my Lysander took that farm girl into his tent! He was supposed to curl up in a ditch and die of shame, like all rejected weaklings do! Who put that cold, venomous adder into his chest?"

I blinked, keeping my eyes wide, mild, and completely guileless.

"Sister, you wound me," I said, placing my right hand over my heart with gentle, pained humility. "Lucan Holt suffered a grave rupture of the spirit. When a mortal soul breaks from grief, the empty vessel drifts upon the currents of the void. If an iron-minded soul from across the cosmic curtain happened to be passing through the dark at that precise second—having departed his own body under somewhat... indignantly fragrant circumstances—can I be blamed if like drew unto like?"

"What circumstances?" she demanded, narrowing her eyes.

"An arrow through the bowels while seated upon a private garderobe," the Warden of the Gray Gate muttered from beneath his cowl. The Death God turned a leaden key in his pale hands, his dry voice rustling like dead leaves in autumn. "A most shameful exit for a king's hand. The old man arrived in the gray mists spitting bile, cursing his sons, and demanding an itemized accounting of every drop of blood he had spent for his house. He refused to step onto the ferry until he saw the charter of the river. When Holt's body opened up, the old lion simply walked back into the living world to collect what was owed to him. The laws of transit were respected. I stamped his exit pass myself."

The Dawn Goddess stared at the Death God, then rounded on me again.

"You brought a monster into my world!"

"I brought a man who understands arithmetic," I corrected her gently, my voice remaining level, smooth, and polite as polished glass. "And more importantly, sister, he has broken no law. Not one. I have audited every transaction he has undertaken since the morning he walked out of your pavilion."

I opened the Great Ledger upon my pedestal. The heavy vellum pages turned with a crisp, dry rustle that seemed to grate on her nerves like a file across a tooth.

"Let us see," I said, tracing the lines of High Imperial script with the tip of my stylus. "Item: The liquidation of the Red Kilns salvage claim. Properly registered under the *Charter of Maritime and Expeditionary Liberties*, Article Four. Lysander Morvan failed to produce a counter-signed factor receipt within the statutory sixty-day window. The foreclosure was entirely lawful."

"He stole the bounty!" she cried.

"He purchased the underlying lien from the Inland Trust for fifteen hundred silver crowns," I replied, looking up with a mild, sympathetic smile. "Item: The grain requisitions. The High Patriarch attempted to seize seventy river barges without ducal warrant. The Lord Protector cited the Municipal Assize of Dun-Carrow, passed under Grand Duke Stephen three hundred years ago. The church was required to pay fair market value. Item: The trial. Lysander Morvan was charged with desertion, reckless endangerment of sovereign levies, and grand embezzlement. Forty-two witnesses testified under oath. His own company scout confirmed he fled the ridge on a fresh horse while his men were being butchered in the ravine."

"He was seeking reinforcements!"

"He was sixty leagues to the rear before the sun went down," the Death God whispered. "Five hundred of my new guests confirmed it. They were very loud about the matter when they crossed my bridge."

The Dawn Goddess clenched her fists until her knuckles flared with blue sparks. "He is my champion! The divine will outranks mortal ink!"

"Not within the boundaries of the Grand Duchy," I replied with the softest, most deferential tilt of my head. "The Grand Duchy was founded under the Sovereign Covenant of Salt and Iron, ratified by the Seven in the First Age. You signed that charter yourself, radiant one. Your seal—the seven-rayed sunburst—is pressed into the wax on page twelve. It explicitly states: *'No celestial power shall directly annul the commercial codes or judicial verdicts of the mortal assize, lest the balance of the elements be dissolved.'*"

She opened her mouth, her face turning crimson, but no sound came out. 

She looked at the Forge God, who was cleaning his fingernails with an iron file. 

She looked at the Harvest Goddess, who was calmly braiding stalks of winter wheat. 

She looked at the Lord of the Deeps, who had closed his eyes and was snoring with the rhythmic, hollow rumble of an ocean cavern.

Not a single god moved to support her.

"This is not finished," she hissed, her voice shaking with suppressed fury. She gathered her blinding skirts around her, stepping back toward her burning throne. "The Southern Sun-Empire has thirty thousand men and three hundred catapults. Their Emperor traces his bloodline to my own altar. When the spring thaws come, his legions will cross the Great River. They will burn Dun-Carrow to the ground, hang that insolent clerk from his own battlements, and restore my holy altars!"

"Perhaps they will," I conceded smoothly, dipping my head in polite acknowledgment. "War is an uncertain enterprise. Though, if I may offer a quiet observation from the ledger... the Emperor's legions must march through twenty leagues of black willow swamp during the spring run-off. A single legionary consumes two pounds of hard flour a day. Thirty thousand men require sixty tons of provisions every twenty-four hours, carried by four thousand draft oxen across roads where mud reaches a wagon's hubs. The Lord Protector has already bought every sack of grain within forty leagues of the river, burned the timber bridges, and mounted twenty torsion scorpions on every river bluff."

I looked up at her, my eyes gleaming with quiet, serene amusement.

"Your Emperor will be eating his own saddle leather before his vanguard sees the sea citadel."

The Lady of the Dawn let out a strangled shriek of absolute, impotent rage. She threw herself back into her electrum chair, crossed her glowing arms over her chest, and turned her face away from the council, her corona sulking into a dull, smoky red.

Silence settled over the Empyrean Dome.

The dry, comforting smell of old ink rose from the pages of my ledger. Outside the crystal dome, the infinite wheel of stars turned in their silent, mathematical orbits, keeping time to the eternal laws of balance, weight, and measure.

I looked down into the water of the scrying well one last time.

Down in Dun-Carrow, night had fallen. 

Torches flickered along the high granite battlements of the Sea Citadel. Along the parapet, the City Wardens marched their posts in pairs, their boarspears resting steady on their shoulders, their quilted red livery keeping out the winter wind. In the lower town, the bakers were lighting their morning fires, mixing flour and water in great wooden troughs, whistling tunes while the city slept in quiet, well-fed peace.

And in the high tower, a lean young man with cold hazel eyes sat behind an oak desk, dipping an iron stylus into an inkwell, reviewing the tax receipts of the western turnpike with patient, unyielding focus.

A soft snicker escaped my lips.

I reached out with my left hand, took the bronze weight from the tray, and placed it upon the right pan of the balance. The chains chimed softly as the scale settled into perfect, immovable alignment.

I closed the pigskin ledger, fastened the silver hasp with a firm click, and settled comfortably into the cold slate of my throne to wait for the business of the dawn.
