# The Cracks in the Mirror

When my father Tytos died of a sudden seizure, I rode home from King's Landing to take Casterly Rock. I found the great stone seat of my house infested like a granary full of rats. Hedge knights who had never seen a battlefield were drinking our cellars dry in the Great Hall, dice rattling across tables of carved wood, while my father's low-born mistress walked the halls wearing the emeralds of my dead mother.

Debts had gone unpaid for ten years. The armories were missing half their pikes, sold to border cutthroats by crooked stewards to pay for singers, jugglers, and dancing boys. Tytos had believed that softness bound men to a house. In truth, it bred only contempt. When a lord lets go of the reins, men take his kindness for weakness. They gorge on his meat until the bone is bare, then turn on each other like starving dogs. I cleared Casterly Rock with cold iron. I threw out the hedge knights, called in every unpaid loan within thirty days, and showed the West that the Laughing Lion was dead, and a real beast had taken his seat.

A house built on pride rots from within long before an enemy strikes the gate. The rot starts the day a man calls the ledger a nuisance.

***

Folly costs dear. It lasts only as long as another fool pays the tab.

By the tenth moon, the leased mansion of the *Dawn-Seekers* in the High Ward reeked of stale mutton grease, sour wine, and wet wool. The great residence, once the pride of a wealthy silk merchant, was now an armed den of broken grifters and frightened retainers. Outside the ornate iron gates, thirty creditors banged on the wood wickets from dawn till dusk, waving unpaid notes for oats, saddles, wine, and hair oil. The baker had cut off their bread a fortnight ago; the hay-ward had seized their three spare baggage mules; and the palace tailors were threatening to haul Lysander before the municipal magistrate for unpaid velvet doublets.

I sat in my solar atop the Old Imperial Toll-House, reading three scouting reports brought by Korgan's riders.

A coal fire burned red and hot in the iron brazier, keeping the bitter autumn chill from my knuckles. The room smelled of hot ink, drying sand, and the distant, sulfurous tang of coal smoke rising from the legionary furnaces below the cellar vaults. On the table lay an itemized tally of the *Dawn-Seekers'* remaining assets, calculated down to the last copper coin.

"The party is eating its own tail, Lord Factor," Morcar said, placing a freshly blotted sheet before me. The old clerk’s spectacles sat crooked on his nose, his eyes bright with professional satisfaction. "Just as you said it would."

"Seeing the fall of proud men takes only plain counting, Morcar," I said, running my iron stylus down the column of red figures. "Five heroes can kill a beast in a hole. Feeding an army takes iron will, grain stores, and hard ledgers. When they kicked the boy Lucan out the door, the fire lost its wood. Without an engine, the cart rolls downhill until it smashes against the rocks."

The reports laid bare the rot.

First came Lady Vivienne Montclair. 

The academy sorceress was coming apart at the seams. Without the clean, refined spell-salts the boy Lucan used to buy from licensed alchemists across the river, she had turned to smugglers operating along the coal pits of the lower wharves. She had bought raw frost-stone dug from northern ice-beds. The raw stone was foul, spiked with poison, sulfur, and lead salts.

Two days ago, while showing her magical craft before the Archon's court in the High Palace, her hands shook so hard her silver needles dropped and rolled across the marble floor. Her evocation broke in a cloud of burning sulfur and bitter green steam that singed the beard of the High Judge. Blood seeped from her gums, turning her teeth black. She lay in her bed now in the mansion's east wing, shivering under three feather quilts, screaming for spiced wine and cursing every name in the city while her mana-veins burned like dry straw.

Then came Kallista Kroll. 

The marcher woman had recruited sixty border sellswords from the river taverns, promising them forty silver crowns a month to ride in Lysander's vanguard. She had bragged that steel and arrows would win the war while clerks starved behind stone walls.

Those sixty sellswords had not seen a single silver copper in three months.

Yesterday morning, the sellswords mutinied in the courtyard. They broke into the cellars, drank two casks of vintage wine, butchered Lysander's prize hunting hounds for spit-meat, and stole thirty fine steel pikes from the racks before marching north to join the road brigands. Kallista tried to draw steel on their sergeant. The man smashed her cheek with his heavy dagger hilt, kicked her into the horse trough, and spat in her face before riding out through the gates.

And then came Lysander Morvan.

Drowning in debt, mocked by the guild masters, and avoided by every sensible factor on the river, the Sun-Gilded Champion looked for a richer purse to save his skin. He went after Lady Alicia of High-Rock, the seventeen-year-old sole daughter of Margrave Leopold. Alicia was plain, foolish, and thoroughly taken with Lysander's spun-gold curls, his flashing smile, and his shiny blade.

Three nights ago, he had brought her into his private chambers at the mansion, promising a grand wedding, a ducal coronet, and eternal glory under the light of the Goddess.

And Rosamund Cleary?

I picked up the third sheet, written in the cramped, spidery hand of a bailiff watching the mansion from the attic across the street.

The milkmaid in velvet had fallen fast and hard.

Once Lady Alicia agreed to visit Lysander's bed, Rosamund became an active danger. Lysander could not show a common village herb-girl to a Margrave's daughter. He had stripped Rosamund of her fine emerald gowns and pearl collar, handing the jewelry to Alicia's maid as a courtship present. He threw Rosamund into the cold scullery behind the stables.

She wore greasy wool now, boiling wash water in iron pots, scraping horse dung from Lysander's riding boots, and scrubbing bloody rags left behind by the mutinous sellswords. When she tried to reach his bedchamber door to beg him on her knees, Lysander's squires barred the way with oak staves, warning her that one more shout would put her in the town pillory.

Morcar shook his head, a dry chuckle in his throat. "The bailiff reports the girl wept at the water pump this morning. Her hands are raw and cracked from lye soap, her face bruised from a brawl with the palazzo cook over a loaf of stale bread. She tried to pawn an old brass locket at the goldsmith's shop to buy soup, but the shopkeeper recognized the piece as stolen goods and took it without paying her a copper."

I set the report down upon the oak desk.

The brass locket had belonged to Lucan's mother. The dead boy had given it to Rosamund by the willow stream when they were fifteen, promising that one day he would fill it with a pearl. She had tossed it into her apron like a pebble, and now she tried to sell it for cabbage water.

I felt no joy. Spite is for weak boys—a hot, petty itch that clouds the mind and wastes coin. What I felt was the cold truth of the world: a sheep that runs with wolves gets eaten.

"She chose her lord," I said, my voice dry as parchment. "Now she scrubs his floor."

"Lysander is playing his last card," Morcar said, his voice dropping low as he leaned across the table. "Archon Althaus called the Sovereign Council to an extraordinary session this morning."

"Why?"

"Warlord Mor-Goth crossed the Skelter Gorge," Morcar said, his hands tight on the edge of the table. "The border forts fell three days ago. The garrisons were butchered to the last man. The northern horde is eight thousand strong—four thousand orc heavy foot, two thousand winter warg riders, and an iron vanguard of mountain trolls. They are burning every farmstead between the river and the hills."

I rose from the chair and walked to the high casement window overlooking the river basin.

The cold salt wind rattled the leaded panes. Below in the harbor, fifty of my grain barges lay moored under the heavy protection of Korgan's crossbowmen. The municipal granaries held eighty thousand bushels of winter rye, bought across the scale before the freeze. The four blast furnaces under my toll-house were turning out three tons of cold-iron pike heads and scorpion bolts every twenty-four hours. My mercenaries were paid in clean Norrmark silver every Friday at noon. My cellars held ninety kegs of Norrmark powder and five hundred steel-spanned crossbows.

I held the grain. I held the iron. I held the river.

The Archon of Dun-Carrow had only debt, terror, and a golden boy who thought his smile could turn back an army of eight thousand beasts.

A horn blew down in the street.

A ducal carriage, painted scarlet and drawn by four sweating bay mares, clattered over the cobblestones and halted before the gates of the toll-house. A royal herald in gold-laced livery stepped down into the slush, holding a heavy parchment roll bound with imperial purple ribbon.

"The Archon calls for you, Holt," Morcar breathed, his hands trembling slightly.

"The council has been sitting since sunrise," Morcar whispered, peering through the window. "The Archon wants the factor in the palace."

"Let him wait another quarter of an hour," I said, picking up the audited ledger and slipping my iron signet onto my thumb. 

I turned to the door and gave my orders to the sergeant on the landing. "Have Captain Korgan ready forty mounted guards in full mail. We ride to the High Council before the sleet worsens."
