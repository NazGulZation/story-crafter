# The Weight of Iron

Before the trouble at Duskendale, the merchants of Lannisport tried to lock up the trade in wood and coal for the armories of Casterly Rock. The guild heads met in secret rooms behind the fish market, hiking the price of charcoal threefold. They thought that because House Lannister sat on mountains of gold, we would pay any price rather than let our fires go out. My father would have called a feast, laughed at their tricks, and offered to split the cost.

I did not bargain with them. I sent three fast cogs to the Crakehall shore, buying raw timber and coal straight from the woodsmen, landing it at our own docks. At the same time, I stripped the tax breaks from the twelve richest merchants in the ring until their books were cleared. In twenty days, the price of wood fell by half, and three guild masters were begging for bread outside the Rock.

A ring of merchants holds together only until someone opens a new road. Once the wagons roll around them, their greed chokes them.

The Foundry Quarter reeked of brimstone, burnt horn, and wet coal dust.

Day and night, thirty heavy water-hammers along the Black Ditch smashed down on iron anvils, shaking the stone under Dun-Carrow. The sky hung grey and thick with greasy soot that stung the throat and fouled a man's collar.

I sat in the high room of the Old Imperial Toll-House, checking my cargo sheets.

The room was bare, like a war tent. A heavy oak table sat in the middle of the floor, cleared of all junk save for an iron ink-pot, brass scales, and two tallow candles burning inside horn lanterns. Two guards in boiled leather stood at the door with steel crossbows—border veterans hired at four silver crowns a week, paid in hand, on time, with no cuts.

A hard knock shook the oak door.

"Enter," I said.

Morcar came in, his black wool coat wet with rain and soot. His eyes were wide behind his glass frames, wheezing as he pulled a damp folder from his ribs.

"The bailiffs seized the wagons at noon, Holt," the old clerk said, grabbing a three-legged stool. "I watched from the gallery. It was quite a sight."

"Did Morvan draw his sword?" I asked, keeping my eyes on my numbers.

"He tried," Morcar chuckled, rubbing his hands. "He pulled that glowing blade of his, yelling about gods and holy duty. But Bailiff Korgan brought twenty archers with cocked winches. A man can spit at a god, but he can't argue with twenty steel bolts aimed at his teeth. The copper ingots from the Red Kilns are locked in the city cellars under three heavy padlocks. Not a pound moves until court sits in winter."

"And the warband?"

"Broke," Morcar said, leaning across the table. "Lord Belmonte wouldn't cover their debts. The money-lenders in the High Ward shut their doors in their faces. The dressmaker on Silk Street took back four of Rosamund Cleary’s fancy gowns because her bill was unpaid. The girl was hauled out of the shop bawling like a kitchen maid caught with stolen ham."

I turned a page in the book. "That is what happens to fools who spend gold before the wagon is weighed."

"There is worse news," Morcar warned, leaning close. "Lysander went to Master Hallor of the Iron Guild. He promised Hallor half the loot from three barrows in the northern crags for fresh mail, horses, and swords. And Hallor took the deal."

I dipped my quill. "Hallor is an idiot. Old barrows hold only bones, mold, and rot."

"Idiot or not, Hallor owns the forges," Morcar said grimly. "The guild controls every hearth between the river mouth and the hills. Hallor found out you bought this tower and wanted fifty tons of iron to arm the City Wardens. An hour ago, Hallor shut you out. No smith in Dun-Carrow will sell you an iron bar, a keg of nails, or a rivet. He plans to break you before you forge a single spear-head."

I laid the quill in the brass tray and folded my hands.

"He shuts the gate," I said softly.

"A total lock," Morcar said. "The guild has the monopoly under the old charter. If you don't deliver iron to the city guard by the next moon, you break your bond. You lose your deposit of eight hundred silver crowns, and the court takes this tower."

Friction. The rot that breaks every pretty plan.

Whenever men drew lines on clean calfskin in a warm room, they thought the road would stay dry and the horses fat. In truth, mud deepened, mules died, and greedy fools tried to bleed you for a copper. My father Tytos would have wept, called a guild meeting, and offered them soft words and higher pay. 

I did not plead with cartels. I broke them.

"Who owns the timber boats down at the West Sump?" I asked.

Morcar blinked, thrown off. "The West Sump? Those belong to the hill-clans from the high woods. Rough men, Holt. They bring down cedar logs and fat. They carry heavy axes, speak broken words, and sleep with their hounds."

"Bring their headman here," I said.

"Holt, those men are savages—"

"They are traders choked out of the market by Hallor’s fees," I cut in, my voice cold and flat. "They hold raw rock they can't sell, and their bellies are empty. Bring him here. Now."

Half an hour later, the door opened to a mountain man who filled the room.

He called himself Jarek. He wore greasy wolf pelts over hard leather, his grey beard tied with bone rings, smelling of pitch, wet fur, and smoke. He leaned on an iron-shod staff, eyeing my guards and the scales like a wolf watching a trap.

"You have silver, city boy?" Jarek growled, his voice like stones grinding under an iron shoe. "Or you waste my night with soft words?"

I did not offer him a stool. I walked to the iron-bound chest behind my table and turned the brass key.

I threw back the heavy lid.

Fifteen hundred clean Norrmark silver crowns gleamed in the tallow light, cold and heavy.

Jarek’s eyes went wide. His knuckles turned white on his staff.

"The Iron Guild charges you a heavy cut at the river boom," I said, looking him in the eye. "Hallor pays you four coppers for every stone of raw bog-iron your clan digs from the hill-muck. Then he smelts it and sells it to the city guard for six silver crowns."

"Hallor is a pig," Jarek spat on the floor. "He sits behind stone walls and starves my kin."

"I will buy every pound of bog-iron on your boats," I said. "Not for four coppers. I will pay eight silver crowns for every hundredweight of raw ore landed at my slipways by tomorrow night."

Jarek took a heavy step toward the desk, his brow dark with doubt. "Bog-iron is raw. Full of dirt, peat, and sulfur. The city smiths can't work it in their soft charcoal fires. It takes stone kilns with hot blast to burn out the slag."

"This old tower has four legion furnaces in the deep cellars," I told him. "Built by soldiers three hundred years ago to burn hard coal, not soft pine wood. I have five boatloads of coal tied to my pier right now."

Morcar sucked in his breath. The old clerk had forgotten the ancient stone ovens under our boots.

Jarek stared at me for three long heartbeats. A broad grin broke through his beard, showing yellow fangs.

"You break Hallor’s back," Jarek said.

"I break his grip," I said. "And I take his place. If your ore is good, I will sign a three-year pact with your clan. We pay in clean Norrmark silver, over the scale, with no city cuts."

The hill man spat in his rough palm and stuck it out.

I did not take his hand. A lord does not spit in a hand to seal a trade; he seals it with coin and ink.

I reached into the chest, scooped up two handfuls of heavy silver crowns, and let them slam onto the sheepskin manifest with a loud ring.

"That is your earnest money, Jarek. Three hundred crowns. If your boats are not tied to my posts before second watch tomorrow, my bowmen will run you out of the reeds, and you will never sell a log in Dun-Carrow again."

Jarek swept the coins into his pelt bag with greedy speed, ducking his head with rough respect. "The ore will be on your stones, master."

He turned and strode out, his heavy boots shaking the stairs.

Morcar leaned on the wall, his spectacles trembling in his grip. "Gods above, Holt... if this works, you will make twenty tons of legion steel at half the price Hallor charges the city."

"It will work because men understand silver and food," I said, sitting back at my desk. "Now write to Captain Korgan of the City Wardens. Tell him *The Golden Lion Factor* will deliver three hundred iron spear-heads and two hundred crossbow cranks to the East Gate on the first of the month. At a ten percent cut under the Iron Guild’s price."

"Hallor will raise hell with the guild masters," Morcar whispered, dipping his quill into the black ink. "He'll claim we are undermining the city rates."

"Let him lodge his petition with the magistrates," I told him, reviewing the signed manifest. "Make sure our foundrymen have their hearth coal weighed before dawn. We have three hundred spear-heads to cast before the first river ice."

I sprinkled grey sand across the wet ink, blew the excess from the vellum, and handed the dispatch across the desk.
