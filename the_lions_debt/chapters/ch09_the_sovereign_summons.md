# The Sovereign Summons

When King Aerys called me to King’s Landing to take the Handship, he was a young monarch full of wild, childish dreams. He spoke of building a second Wall of white marble in the far north to keep out the wildlings for all time, of digging great canals to turn the red sands of Dorne into flowering gardens, and of conquering the Free Cities of Essos with fire and sword. While the boy babbed of wonders, the royal vaults were bone dry, cutpurses ruled the high roads, and the lords of the realm spat on decrees issued from the Iron Throne. 

I took the heavy gold chain of the Hand on my own terms. I did not flatter his fantasies. I reduced port dues to bring trade back to the harbor, mended the King’s Road from the Wall to the Red Mountains, paid off the crown's arrears to the Iron Bank in solid Lannister gold, and stationed garrison companies with spears at every bridge and crossroad. For twenty years, Aerys sat on the iron seat picking his scabs and raving in his cups, while I governed the Seven Kingdoms. The land enjoyed peace and full bellies because a hard man held the reins. A crown is only a hoop of gold; the Hand is the fist that swings the hammer.

A weak king in a storm looks for a savior. A wise man makes sure that when the king calls, the terms of rescue reduce the monarch to a pensioner.

***

The ride from the wharves to the High Palace was a slow, deliberate march through the grey sleet.

I rode at the head of forty mounted syndicate guards clad in full mail coats beneath heavy cloaks of crimson wool. Behind our horses rumbled two iron-bound supply wains, escorted by Captain Korgan and Master Garrow. Along the steep, cobblestone streets of the Middle Ward, shopkeepers stood in their doorways in wool aprons, watching our column pass in absolute silence. They saw the discipline of our files; they saw the Norrmark iron pikes held upright without wavering; they saw the gold lion banners snapping in the wind. The common people possess a sharp instinct for the movement of power: they knew the palace had run out of bread, and they knew whose carts held the grain.

We reached the High Palace at the fourth bell of the morning.

The Pillar Room smelled of wet lime plaster, burning tallow, and the cold, sour sweat of frightened lords.

Charcoal fires burning in iron braziers in the alcoves failed to warm the immense, high-vaulted chamber. Cold drafts whistled through the high lancet windows, ruffling the frayed gold fringes of ancient ancestral banners depicting long-dead grand dukes who had once owned armies, silver, and spine. 

Around the horseshoe council table sat twelve noble grandees of Dun-Carrow. Their faces were grey as cold lard. Margrave Leopold of High-Rock clutched a parchment dispatch with liver-spotted, shaking hands; the Chief Assessor wept silently into his velvet sleeves over an empty ledger; and upon the raised throne of purple velvet sat Archon Althaus, pale as curdled milk, his swollen, gouty foot propped on a stained cushion.

Lysander Morvan stood in the center of the hall.

He had donned his newly gilded plate armor, polished to a bright shine, complete with a sky-blue mantle lined with white fur, though his scabbard was visibly dented from his drunken brawl in the High Ward and his squires looked half-starved.

Beside him stood Lady Alicia of High-Rock, clinging to his armored forearm with adoring, naive fervor, looking up at him as if he were an archangel stepped down from the clouds. Behind them stood Kallista Kroll, her swollen, purple cheekbone standing out stark against her pale skin, and Vivienne Montclair, white as a sheet, her dark eyes hollow, shaking with mana-chills as she leaned heavily upon a polished ebony cane.

"I swear before this council!" Lysander was shouting as I reached the heavy bronze doors. "The Goddess of the Dawn came to me in a holy dream! I shall lead the vanguard to Skelter-Ridge! My blade shall ignite the snows! The frost-beasts will shatter like brittle glass before the wrath of the sun!"

"And who feeds your vanguard, Sir Lysander?"

The words fell into the hall like a heavy slab of river slate.

Lysander choked on his next breath. The twelve councilors turned their heads simultaneously, their wide, terrified eyes locking onto the bronze portal.

I walked down the central aisle, unhurried, the heels of my greased boots loud on the old mosaic tiles. Behind me walked Captain Korgan in heavy iron plate, followed by four syndicate guards carrying two heavy, iron-bound chests containing the grain manifests and sovereign debt deeds of Dun-Carrow.

I halted ten paces from the dais. I did not bow. I did not drop to a knee. A creditor does not kneel before a debtor whose palace he already owns.

"Holt," Lysander snarled, his face twisting as his hand dropped to his hilt. "You dare break in on a sovereign council of war? Guards! Throw this merchant out into the yard!"

Not a guard moved.

The twenty guardsmen lining the stone walls were City Wardens. I had paid their four-month back wages in clean, unclipped Norrmark silver three days ago. They stood like iron posts, their eyes fixed firmly on the oak rafters above.

Archon Althaus leaned forward on his throne, wheezing with desperate, wheezing relief. "Lord Factor Holt... by the mercy of the gods, you answered our summons."

"I am here, Your Grace," I replied, my gravelly baritone echoing off the limestone vaulting. "Though I see you have been listening to play-actors while your northern borders burn."

Lysander took two furious steps toward me, his scabbard clattering. "Watch your mouth, clerk! You speak to a champion of light! I am the sword of Dun-Carrow!"

"A sword without an arm to swing it is merely three feet of sharpened scrap," I told him, not bothering to turn my head to look at his face. "And a sword without bread in the belly of the swordsman is dead weight in a snowdrift."

I looked directly at Archon Althaus.

"Warlord Mor-Goth crossed the Skelter Gorge with eight thousand frost-orcs," I stated with cold, unyielding clarity. "He burns the farmsteads of the Upper Weald. In four days, his vanguard reaches the Skelter Defile. If he takes the pass, the road to Dun-Carrow is wide open. The city has barely three days of grain in the municipal silos. The city mills have ceased grinding because the carters have not been paid in four months."

Althaus wiped his wet jowls with a silk handkerchief. "We know, Lord Factor... we know. The treasury is empty. The Norrmark banks cut off our credit after Lord Belmonte’s collapse. We need grain. We need iron. We need forty thousand bushels of rye to feed the army through the winter campaign."

"You need an army," I corrected him. "You currently possess only two thousand terrified peasant boys with sticks, eighty palace guards who have never seen a spear thrust in anger, and twenty pretty knights who spend their mornings curling their hair."

Lysander roared in fury. "I will not stand here and be mocked by a pack-mule! Your Grace, give me the army! Give me the sovereign banner! I will march to Skelter-Ridge and bring you Mor-Goth's head on a pike!"

"You will bring him your head on a platter," I said quietly. "And lose the army before you cross the first mountain pass."

"Silence, both of you!" Margrave Leopold yelled, slamming his hand onto the council table. "The orcs are four days away! Lord Factor Holt, you hold the granaries of the South Reach. You hold the blast furnaces of the Black Ditch. The Crown demands your aid under the Sovereign Defense Covenant!"

"The Crown has defaulted on five consecutive covenants with the Inland Trust," I replied, stepping forward and nodding to Korgan.

The captain set the heavy iron chest on the table and threw back the lid.

Inside lay stacks of vellum debt rolls, sealed in red wax with the marks of the Archon's kin down to the present day.

"I hold four hundred and twenty thousand gold ducats in sovereign notes issued by Dun-Carrow," I said, resting my hands on the table edge. "Every toll gate, every maritime dock, every salt mine, and every tax-farming concession from the Skelter River to the Grey Gulf belongs to my syndicate. If I close my granaries tonight, Dun-Carrow starves before the orcs arrive. If I shut down my furnaces, your smiths will not produce a single arrowhead."

Althaus began to tremble, his soft fingers digging deep into the velvet of his sleeves. "What... what do you want, Holt? Gold? We have no gold!"

"I know you have no gold," I said. "I demand the rule."

A sharp gasp ran down the length of the horseshoe table.

"Treason!" Vivienne Montclair wheezed from behind Lysander, her dark eyes wild with fever, her staff rattling on the floor. "He is an extortionist! Seize him!"

Still, no guard moved.

"These are my terms for the preservation of this realm," I continued, unrolling a clean calfskin sheet across the oak table. 

"First: I am named High Justiciar and Lord Protector of the Grand Duchy, holding absolute civil, judicial, and military authority until the northern invasion is crushed."

"Lord Protector?" Leopold stammered, his eyes bulging. "That is sovereign regency! You strip the Crown of its power!"

"The Crown has no power," I said, holding his eyes until the old man looked down. "It has only debt. Second: the complete subordination of all military forces—the City Wardens, the peasant levies, and all private expeditionary companies—under my direct, unified command. No knight or captain may move a company or engage the enemy without my countersigned written order."

Lysander stepped before the throne, pulling his blade three inches from the sheath, bright white fire spilling across the carpet. "Never! I am Chosen of the Dawn! I take orders from the heavens, not from an ink-stained clerk!"

"Third," I said, ignoring the boy and looking straight at Althaus. "The mortgage on the eastern maritime customs is transferred permanently to *The Golden Lion Factor*. In exchange, I will open my granaries. I will feed four thousand soldiers three hot meals a day. I will deliver two thousand iron pikes, five hundred arbalests, and forty heavy siege engines to the Skelter Defile before the first blizzard hits."

The Pillar Room fell dead silent.

The councilors looked at Lysander Morvan, shining in his pretty silver armor, promising miracles on an empty belly with no supply wagons.

Then they looked at me. Standing in plain black wool, holding the deeds to their food, their weapons, and their lives in my two hands.

Archon Althaus slumped into his cushions, a broken, defeated husk of a ruler.

"Bring the Great Seal," Althaus whispered, his voice cracking into a dry sob.

"Your Grace, no!" Lysander yelled, his face turning purple with humiliation. "You surrender the realm to a usurer!"

"Bring the seal," the Archon wheezed, not looking at him. "Unless you have forty thousand sacks of flour hidden under your cloak, Sir Lysander."

Ten minutes later, the imperial calfskin was signed and pressed with the Great Seal of Dun-Carrow.

I blew dry sand across the red wax, folded the skin, and slipped it inside my wool doublet.

I was now the Lord Protector of the Grand Duchy. The Hand of the realm in all but name.

I turned slowly and faced Lysander Morvan.

"You wished to lead the vanguard to Skelter-Ridge, Morvan?" I asked, my voice cutting through the silence like an executioner's blade.

Lysander stood rigid, his blue eyes burning with murderous hatred. "I will slay Mor-Goth with or without your permission, clerk."

"You have your wish," I said. "Take your three hundred retainers, your mounted knights, and your holy banner. March out at first light tomorrow. Take the forward redoubts at Skelter-Ridge."

Lysander blinked. He had expected to be stripped of command, imprisoned, or dismissed. 

"You... you give me the vanguard?" he stammered, suspicion flickering across his handsome face.

"I give you the vanguard," I replied, stepping past him toward the great bronze portal. "Go and win your glory, Morvan. Prove to the realm that your Goddess can feed an army with prayers. The main host will march three days behind you."

Lysander’s pride flared back to life, blinding him to the yawning pit beneath his feet. He threw his head back and laughed—a booming, arrogant sound that echoed off the damp limestone pillars.

"You hear that, councilors?" Lysander declared, turning to the trembling lords. "The clerk admits his fear! He cowers behind the stone walls while true men ride to battle! Come, Vivienne! Gather the knights! Tomorrow we ride for immortality!"

He strode out of the hall, dragging Lady Alicia behind him, followed by Kallista and his squires.

Captain Korgan fell in beside me as we descended the grand palace staircase into the freezing dusk.

"Lord Protector," Korgan said, his scarred brow furrowed with deep unease. "The boy has three hundred riders in his vanguard. If he marches without the main host, he will run straight into Mor-Goth's heavy screen."

"That is his responsibility," I told him, stepping out into the slush of the lower courtyard. "Our duty is the logistics of the main body. Send word to the baggage train to prepare forty wagons of dry oats, eighty tierces of salt pork, and two thousand heavy quarrels. We march at daybreak on the fourth day."

I gathered my mantle against the mountain wind, climbed into the waiting carriage, and told the driver to head for the arsenal.
