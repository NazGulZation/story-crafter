# The Privy and the Peacock

The stench left me first.

Latrine muck, stale piss, and the hot, sour tear in my gut where the crossbow bolt punched through leather and flesh. I remembered the heavy walnut stock in my dwarf son’s hands. I remembered the yellow, hateful squint in Tyrion’s eyes, and the thunk of the iron release lever. I remembered dying with my breeches down around my knees on an oak privy bench, my bowels spilling filth into the pit beneath the Tower of the Hand. 

Lord Tywin Lannister, Shield of Lannisport, Warden of the West, Hand of the King, slaughtered like an old hog in a ditch by his own stunted get. 

Then came the void. A cold, black gulf without sound or breath, where the memory of thirty years of rule seemed to spin away like grease on water. I had held the Seven Kingdoms in my closed fist. I had built a house that made kings tremble, only to die in my own waste in the dark.

A sudden gasp dragged me back.

A rush of sharp salt air hit my throat, pulled into lungs twenty years younger than the ones I died with.

My breath rattled in my teeth. I blinked against the glare of yellow wax tapers and bronze sconces. My ribs were whole. My belly held no iron bolt, no leaking muck, no burning agony. I stood straight on two sound legs, dressed in a salt-stained wool tunic and a worn leather vest smelling of tallow, ink, and dried herbs. My hands—lean, free of gout, callused from quill-stems, ink-stones, and inventory slates—hung steady at my sides.

Memories that did not belong to the Lord of Casterly Rock flooded my skull like cold river water through a burst sluice.

The boy was nineteen. His name was Lucan Holt. He was the son of a widowed tenant farmer from the willow-wealds south of Dun-Carrow. Three years ago, he had gathered five runaway youngsters around an alehouse table, drafting a hunting covenant on greasy butcher's paper to form the *Dawn-Seekers*. He had spent three years doing the butcher's work, the mule work, the ledger work. He had scraped the copper together to buy their first spears; he had bargained with carters for cheap oat sacks; he had patched their boots by tallow candle while they slept.

And now he was standing in the high hall of the Sun-Lance Pavilion.

"Are you deaf, Lucan, or just stupid?"

The voice rang across the hall with the brassy noise of a cheap street horn.

I lifted my chin.

The hall was broad and high, lined with stone pillars and hung with red banners bearing a rising sun. A long table of dark oak stood buried under greasy trenchers of cold roast fowl, spilled cups of sweet wine, melon rinds, and cracked sugar crusts. Three house servants in white linen stood near the side doors, carving fat capons and pouring southern vintage into silver goblets.

In the middle of the dais stood a tall youth whose vanity filled the room like horse sweat.

His hair fell over his plate-armored shoulders in spun-gold ringlets. His cheekbones were smooth and sharp; his skin had the clear, unblemished glow of a boy who had never spent a wet night in a siege trench or slept on frozen shale. At his throat hung a sun-disk of pale electrum that pulsed with a faint, irritating shine—the holy favor of the sun-god this realm’s simple folk worshipped.

Lysander Morvan. The name drifted up from the boy’s brain like scum on boiled mutton broth.

He was the golden boy of Dun-Carrow. Born with a pretty face, blessed by the Church of the Radiant Dawn, handed a six-foot sword forged of bright sun-steel, and hailed as a champion by priests who cared more for gold lace than blood and bone.

Beside him sat two women in silk.

To his left, Lady Vivienne Montclair spun a silver fork between pale, slender fingers, her eyes hooded with courtly boredom. She wore the blue silk robe of a trained spell-caster from the High Academy, three sapphire pins holding her black hair tight against her skull. She smelled of bitter almond oil and burnt alum—the sharp, poisonous stink of those who burned their brains on smuggled frost-crystals and raw mana formulas. Her knuckles were white, her fingers twitching with a faint, hidden tremor that told me all I needed to know about her failing art.

To his right slumped Kallista Kroll, a marcher skirmisher with cropped red hair and a scarred jaw. She had a face like a weathered fence post. She scraped pork grease from her thumbnail with a bone-handled skinning knife, her thick forearms resting on the stained linen cloth, an open tankard of dark ale at her elbow.

"We march for the Red Kilns at dawn," Lysander said, resting his gold-plated gloves on the pommel of his great sword. "The High Archon gave us our writ. We face frost-trolls, stone-beasts, and the old imperial vaults. We need steel. We need god-given strength. We need men who can cleave an orc to the brisket without wetting their pants."

He stepped off the dais, his gilded spurs clinking loudly against the green stone flags. He stopped two paces from me, puffing out his chest, his blue eyes bright with theatrical scorn.

"You hauled our pack-bags well enough, Lucan," Lysander said, looking down his straight nose. "You weighed our grain, greased our wagon wheels, and beat down the potters for cheap lard. The *Dawn-Seekers* thank you for that. But your fighting rank is fourth-tier trash. Your blood holds no magic. You swing a sword like an old washerwoman beating rugs on a rock. If I drag you into the kilns, you will die, and your carcass will only slow me down."

He reached into his belt purse, pulled out a small leather pouch, and untied the string. With a lazy flick of his wrist, he threw it at my chest.

The leather hit my ribs and fell to the floor, spilling ten dark copper farthings across the flags.

They rolled in the dust, coming to rest beside my boots.

"Ten coppers," Lysander said with a stage-actor's grin. "More than enough for three months of rye bread and small beer. Go back to your father’s farm. Mend stone walls. Breed sheep. Leave the war to men born for the blade."

Vivienne Montclair let out a dry, airy laugh, lifting her silver cup. "Good riddance, Lysander. The campsite smelled of greasy slates and lamp-oil whenever he sat by the fire. A warband needs clean fire and high magic, not a plow-boy scratching totals in the dirt with an ink-stained thumb."

Kallista Kroll grunted, wiping her grease-stained blade on her leather breeches. "Good riddance. Tired of looking back when we run to see if our clerk dropped his ink-pot in the dirt. Steel wins wars, boy. Not numbers."

Inside my borrowed ribs, a sudden wave of boyish grief surged up—a desperate, weeping panic that tasted of crushed summer grass, cheap cider, and adolescent yearning.

*Rosamund,* the dead boy's memory cried inside my mind. *Look at Rosamund. She will speak for you. She promised. You shared black bread by the mill. You swore to build a house of fieldstone and oak.*

I crushed the feeling down with cold, deliberate weight.

It was the same sniveling rot that had ruined my house forty years ago. The weak, soft-bellied rot that had made my father Tytos the laughingstock of the Westerlands. Tytos had spent his life smiling at insults, forgiving debts, and begging for love from men who only laughed in his face. He had let low hedge knights mock his name in his own hall, trading Lannister honor for a merchant's smile and a whore's embrace.

I had spent my entire life stamping out that weakness with fire and blood. I would not let it rise in this new skin.

I buried the boy’s tears under forty years of Casterly iron.

Then I turned my head and looked at the third woman.

Rosamund Cleary sat near the end of the long table, wearing a dress of green velvet with cuffs of white hare fur. The cloth was far too fine for a village girl who had spent her childhood digging mandrake roots in the marsh; the silver hooks down her bodice were brand new, bought with the silver from the company's last bounty.

Her hazel eyes—wide, soft, and moist—looked straight into mine.

The boy Lucan remembered her as an angel from the hedgerows. He had carried her heavy herb packs through briars, sleet, and mud for three years. He had stood guard in the freezing rain until his boots turned to ice so she could sleep dry and warm in the center tent. He had kept his mouth shut, terrified that a clumsy word of love might spoil the sacred bond between them.

Now, Rosamund’s lips pulled tight into a sharp, mocking line. The sweet, innocent country look fell away like a discarded rag, replaced by the hungry, venomous smirk of a tavern wench who had wriggled her way into a lord's bed.

"Look at him," she said, her voice dripping with amusement. "Still staring at me like a kicked hound. Did you really think I’d speak for you, Lucan?"

She stood up from her chair, walking over to Lysander. She laid her soft, white hand upon his gilded breastplate, her fingers stroking the electrum sunburst with the easy, shameless habit of a kept woman.

"You were fine when we had nothing," she said, resting her chin upon Lysander's armored shoulder, her eyes mocking me. "You carried my root baskets, washed my dirty rags in the creek, and argued with the carters over three copper coins. But look at Lysander. He is the sun's own champion. He smells of sweet almond oil, victory, and power. When he takes me, the earth moves under my back."

She leaned forward over the table, making sure every single word struck like a thorn.

"Did you think I spent those cold nights praying for your skin, Lucan? While you were out in the weeds freezing on the perimeter watch, counting sacks of bruised turnips and writing numbers in your greasy book, I was in Lysander’s tent. I was on my knees on his wolf furs, taking him in my mouth until I choked on his seed. He takes me three times a night while you shiver over your ledgers. You are a clerk, Lucan. A dry, boring, pathetic little runt. And I am done with runts."

Lysander laughed, pulling her tight against his hip with one armored arm, his fingers digging into the velvet of her waist. Vivienne smiled into her wine cup; Kallista gave a rough chuckle and went back to picking her teeth.

They stood there in the candlelight, waiting for the collapse.

They expected the boy to break. They waited for him to fall to his knees on the stone flags, sobbing and begging for pity. They waited for frantic excuses, tears in the dust, or a wild, clumsy swing of a fist that Lysander could bat aside with the back of his steel gauntlet to give his women a good laugh.

I looked at them.

I looked at Lysander Morvan. A brainless bull in gilded plate, an empty shell of a man whose whole craft consisted of waving three feet of glowing steel and hoping a god remembered his face when the blood started flowing.

I looked at Vivienne Montclair. An arrogant academy dabbler whose grey skin and black-rimmed gums reeked of arsenic and bad crystal, so weak she could not cast a ward without burning out her veins.

I looked at Kallista Kroll. A border dog who thought cruelty was courage, too blind to realize that sellswords turn and run the moment the grain wagons stop rolling.

And I looked at Rosamund Cleary. A common village girl in borrowed velvet who thought that spreading her thighs for a golden-haired fool made her a lady of the realm.

My father had done the exact same thing. After my mother died, Tytos had taken a common girl into his bed—a candle-maker's daughter who strutted through Casterly Rock wearing my mother's emeralds, giving orders to knights and councilors, believing that an old fool's lust gave her sovereign power.

I had made that woman walk naked through the streets of Lannisport for a fortnight until every docker and fishmonger knew the price of Lannister blood.

A cold, hard quiet settled into my chest. The shame of the privy—the dwarf’s crossbow bolt, the stink of my own loosened bowels—burned away, replaced by the iron will that had governed seven kingdoms for thirty years.

I did not weep. I did not raise my voice. I did not look down at the ten copper coins lying in the dirt.

I smoothed the rough wool of my cuffs. With slow, steady grace, I laced my fingers together at my waist and met Lysander’s blue eyes.

"A lion does not concern himself with the opinion of sheep," I said.

My voice came out low, flat, and dry—a rough baritone that held no country whine, no crack of youth, no trace of fear. It struck the room like an iron portcullis dropping into stone grooves.

Lysander’s grin died on his mouth. His brows drew together, his fingers freezing on Rosamund’s hip.

"What did you say?" he demanded.

"You spoke of iron and blood," I said, keeping my eyes fixed upon the bridge of his nose. "You think wars are won by men who yell loud oaths and swing glowing steel in the light of the sun. You know nothing of war. You know nothing of the grain that feeds the line, the carts that move the pikes, the timber that builds the wall, or the debts that buy the horses. You are an ornament, Morvan. A gilded weathercock swinging in the wind."

Vivienne’s silver cup stopped halfway to her lips. 

Kallista’s knife went dead on the wood. The border scout squinted at me through the candle smoke, her scarred brow twitching as if she had suddenly seen an adder coiled beneath the bread basket.

Rosamund spat on the floor, taking a furious step forward. "Do not let him talk to you like that, Lysander! Hit him! Put the peasant in the dirt where he belongs!"

I did not look at her. To acknowledge a traitorous bitch with a glance is to treat her as an equal. 

"Keep your copper," I told Lysander, turning my back on the dais with slow, unhurried strides. "Debts always come due, Morvan. And when the bill arrives, your god will not pay it for you."

"Shut your mouth, clerk!" Lysander barked, his voice cracking with sudden fury as his hand went to his hilt. "Walk out that door, and you lose every copper of your share in this company! You won't see a single silver crown from the Red Kiln bounty!"

I did not break my stride. I reached the heavy oak double doors of the hall, resting my palm against the iron handle.

"I am the company," I said, looking back over my shoulder. "You have merely been swinging its sword."

I pushed the heavy oak doors open.

A blast of cold night air from the Grey Gulf hit my face, clean, biting, and smelling of cedar woodsmoke, wet salt, and roasting malt from the lower wharves. 

Behind me, the silence of the hall was complete. Not a cup clinked; not a servant moved; Lysander stood in his gilded plate with his mouth half-open, unable to fathom how a penniless boy had stripped the dignity from his room without drawing a knife.

I stepped across the threshold, letting the heavy oak slam shut behind me.

The sound boomed through the stone corridors of the High Ward like a drumbeat.

I stood upon the terrace of the Upper Tier, looking down upon the city of Dun-Carrow.

Below me, spread across the dark water of the estuary, lay an empire of timber and stone. Slate roofs gleamed under the sickle moon; masts of forty merchant caravels rocked in the tidal basin; the fires of the iron foundries glowed dull red against the western hills. Thousands of men were down there in the dark: dockers hauling salt fish, blacksmiths hammering wagon tires, carters cursing their oxen, factors counting their ledgers.

They were looking for a master. They had only play-actors and debt-ridden lords.

The boy Lucan Holt would have run to an alley to weep. He would have drowned his sorrow in sour cider, nursing a broken heart while dreaming of the girl in green velvet.

I did not weep. 

I checked the leather pouch inside my tunic. The heavy iron keys to the company cellar were there, cold against my ribs. The brass stylus and the seal-wax were there. In my head lay every supply bond, every grain contract, and every debt recorded in the founding charter of the *Dawn-Seekers*.

Lysander had signed every document with his own flourish, never bothering to read a single clause.

I pulled the woolen collar of my cloak tight against the draught, stepped out from the shadow of the cathedral wall, and started down the long marble stair toward the Lower Ward to find a bed for the night.
