# The Stripping of the Sun

*In a coat of gold or a coat of red,*
*a lion still has claws,*
*And mine are long and sharp, my lord,*
*as long and sharp as yours.*

A fool armed with a blazing sword believes that shine equals sharpness. He mistakes the cheering of an arena for the harsh reality of war, convincing himself that divine favor will shield his limbs from cold iron. When a cornered dog lunges, a wise commander does not waste time parrying with chivalric flourishes. He snaps the trap, drops the iron bar, and lets twenty arbalests give the final answer.

***

Chief Magistrate Harlen rose from his high-backed chair on the central dais, holding a heavy roll of black vellum bound with lead ribbon.

The Grand Hall of Justice was cold as a tomb. Three hundred nobles, guild masters, and church prelates sat motionless along the oak benches, their breath pluming white toward the high basalt arches. In the gallery above, the widows of Skelter-Ridge watched with cold, unblinking eyes, their hands clasped over their dark wool shawls.

"By the sworn verdict of the High Assize," Magistrate Harlen announced, his dry voice ringing across the green porphyry flags, "Lysander Morvan is found guilty of grand embezzlement, criminal neglect of command, willful defiance of sovereign orders, and cowardice in flight before the enemy. The court orders his titles stripped, his arms and plate forfeit to the sovereign treasury, and his person remanded to perpetual labor in the flooded stone pits of the Black Bastion."

A sharp, ragged gasp tore from Lysander's throat.

He stood in the center of the dock, his hands shaking against the black iron bar. The last trace of reason drained from his wide blue eyes, replaced by the frantic, venomous fury of a spoiled child whose favorite toy has been smashed upon the hearth.

"The Black Bastion?" Lysander’s voice rose to a shrill, manic pitch, echoing off the stone vaults. "You condemn me to shovel muck in a dungeon? I am the chosen son of the solar heavens! I was crowned by the Radiant Goddess herself! I will not rot in a rat-hole while a thieving ink-swine sits upon my rightful throne! Die, clerk!"

Wild panic lent him sudden, unnatural speed.

With a desperate roar, Lysander vaulted over the heavy iron railing of the dock, his iron boots striking the porphyry floor with a sharp crack.

His right hand ripped the Sun-Sword from its electrum scabbard.

The steel screamed against the throat of the scabbard, and as the six-foot blade cleared leather, blinding white fire roared along its length. The heat was ferocious, scorching the air like an open furnace door, blistering the wax on the nearest candelabras, throwing a blast of hot ozone into the faces of the magistrates on the dais.

Lords and prelates shrieked, scrambling backward in terror, overturning their carved oak chairs and tangling their fur mantles as they scrambled away from the flaming blade. Margrave Leopold threw his arms over his head, cowering behind his daughter's bench, while High Inquisitor Malachai scrambled backward into the laps of his junior deacons.

Captain Korgan lunged forward, drawing his broadsword with a rasp of steel. "Shields up! Protect the Lord Protector!"

"Stand your ground, Korgan," I said.

My voice was flat, calm, and level. I did not move an inch.

I stood five paces from the foot of the dais, my hands resting behind my back beneath the folds of my lynx-trimmed mantle. I drew no steel. I reached for no dagger. I did not step back. I did not blink.

I had anticipated this charge three weeks ago. During my first inspection of the *Dawn-Seeker* company outside the city walls, I had observed Lysander sparring in the training ring. I had watched the solar fire flare bright when he swung through the air, and I had noted how the glow guttered and threw sparks whenever the steel struck wet river clay or touched the iron rim of a buckler. Magic in this world had its rules, its conduits, and its grounding limits, just like wildfire or siegecraft.

Three nights ago, under the cover of darkness and the pretext of repairing foundation settlement caused by the autumn river floods, Korgan’s sappers had worked beneath the hall floor.

They had lifted twenty square yards of green porphyry mosaic. Underneath the paving flags, they had laid an eighty-square-foot subterranean lattice of dense, unrefined bog-iron, cast at low heat in the riverside smelting kilns. The iron beams were bolted together with inch-thick bronze rivets and anchored by heavy copper grounding cables directly into the wet bedrock of the estuary beneath the citadel cellar.

Cold iron does its work without passion or pride.

Lysander closed the distance in two thunderous bounds, raising the blazing greatsword high above his right shoulder with both hands, his face contorted in murderous frenzy.

"Die in the fire, usurer!" he screamed, his muscles knotting to split my skull to the collarbone.

His left iron boot came down hard upon the circular mosaic medallion set into the floor five paces before me.

A subterranean iron *clack* vibrated through the floor joists.

The moment his iron-shod boot struck the center of the grid, the circuit closed. The ambient solar magic feeding the blade met forty tons of grounded bog-iron connected to the river tide.

The white flame died with a wet, choking hiss, like a glowing poker shoved into a bucket of pig slop.

The searing heat vanished into a cloud of greasy, grey smoke. The blinding golden glow collapsed into dull, blackened iron. In a single heartbeat, the divine relic turned into seventy pounds of inert, dead pig-iron, dragging Lysander's arms down by its sheer unyielding weight.

Lysander stumbled, his balance broken, his boots skidding across the slick stone.

He stared down at the blackened, lifeless iron in his fists, his jaw slack, his chest heaving like a blown horse.

"What... what did you do?" he stammered, his voice trembling in horror. "Where is the fire? Where is the light?"

"I showed you the world as it is, Morvan," I said quietly. "Cold iron does not care about your prayers."

I raised my right hand two inches.

From the dark shadows of the upper triforium gallery, forty feet above our heads, twenty heavy steel latches clicked open in unison.

Twenty City Wardens in red wool livery leaned over the stone rail, their heavy arbalests leveled downward into the center of the hall. These were not green boys from the villages; they were veteran crossbowmen from the river forts, drilled to load and loose by whistle signals, paid four silver crowns on the first of each month without fail.

The discharge was a single, deafening whip-crack.

Twenty heavy iron quarrels sliced through the air of the Grand Hall.

They did not strike his heart. A quick death is a cheap mercy, and mercy is coin I do not spend on fools who waste armies.

Two barbed bolts tore through Lysander's right shoulder joint, smashing the ball and socket to powder and pinning the shredded muscle against his plate. Two more iron quarrels punched through his left forearm, nailing his blue velvet cloak to his gambeson. Four heavy square-headed bolts struck both kneecaps at point-blank downward angles, pulverizing patella, cartilage, and joint into fine bone dust.

The dead sword slipped from his paralyzed fingers, clattering loudly against the green porphyry flags.

Lysander crashed to the floor with a wet, strangled shriek.

He lay on his stomach, thrashing like a gaffed cod, bright crimson arterial blood pumping across the cold tiles. The long, muscular legs that had strutted across the tourney grounds of the Grand Duchy were twisted into crooked, grotesque angles beneath his dented armor.

I looked down at the writhing heap of gold and meat.

The scene was an old acquaintance.

A heavy crossbow. Iron quarrels tearing through muscle and marrow. A proud, arrogant man brought low in his own blood and waste, stripped of all dignity in the span of three breaths.

My dwarf son thought he had broken House Lannister when he put an iron quarrel through my belly while I sat upon an oak privy seat in King's Landing. Tyrion believed that dying in filth was the end of my authority, that the name of Lannister would become a shameful tavern joke throughout the Seven Kingdoms.

He understood nothing.

Pride is an illusion of soft-hearted fools. Power does not live in songs, crowns, or clean deaths; power lives in the unyielding will that imposes order upon chaos. A lion does not die because his mortal shell was pierced in the dark; his spirit takes up the claw in another land, with another blade, under another sky. The crossbow had taken my mortal breath; now the crossbow enforced my law.

I took two slow, deliberate steps and stopped beside Lysander's head.

The boy wept into the cold stone, coughing up pink foam, his fingernails clawing uselessly at the mortar between the tiles.

"Lucan..." he wheezed, his voice bubbling with agony. "Please... my legs... my knees are gone... call the cathedral healers... call Rosamund... she knows the herbs... please..."

"Rosamund is wading knee-deep in the brine pans at the mouth of the grey river," I said, my tone as flat and unyielding as a gravestone. "Her fingers are split to the bone from raw salt. Her boots are soaked in lye. She sleeps in a bunkhouse with forty washerwomen, earning two copper bits a day to repay the silver she stole. She will not be coming to bind your knees."

Lysander shuddered, a long, pitiful moan rattling in his throat.

Captain Korgan marched forward with four heavy guardsmen, their boots ringing on the stone flags. Behind them came two burly smiths from the city arsenal, dragging a four-hundred-pound iron anvil across the tiles, followed by Master Armorer Beric carrying a sixteen-pound sledgehammer.

"Strip his plate," I ordered.

Two guards pinned Lysander’s shoulders to the floor, their iron knees pressing his head into the puddle of his own blood. Korgan knelt beside him with a cold chisel and a heavy mallet.

With swift, rhythmic strikes, Korgan sheared through the leather buckles holding the gilded harness together. 

The breastplate was yanked from his chest, baring his sweat-soaked, blood-stained linen gambeson. The shoulder guards came off next, then the crushed leg greaves, pulling away strips of torn flesh where the iron had buckled into his shins. Korgan took the heavy gold medallion of the Radiant Dawn from Lysander's neck, wrapping the thick link chain around his fist and ripping it away with a violent jerk that tore the boy's skin.

Korgan threw the gilded harness upon the face of the anvil with a heavy iron crash.

"Break it," I said.

Master Beric stepped forward, spat into his calloused palms, and swung the sixteen-pound sledgehammer back over his broad shoulder.

*CLANG.*

The hammer descended with crushing force. The dented breastplate buckled, the gilded sunburst flattening into twisted scrap iron.

*CLANG.*

The hammer struck again, folding the metal upon itself.

Korgan took the dead Sun-Sword from the floor by the pommel, resting the thick blade across the horn of the anvil.

Beric raised the hammer high and brought it down with the full weight of his torso.

*CRACK.*

A shriek of overstressed steel shattered the silence of the hall. The blade snapped clean in two, the magical alloy fracturing like brittle winter ice. Beric struck the lower half once more, breaking the blade into three jagged chunks of dark, dead metal that rolled into the bloody sawdust at Lysander's face.

Lysander watched the destruction of his glory with wide, hollow eyes, a thin trail of drool leaking from the corner of his open mouth. 

The Champion of the Sun was gone. In his place lay a broken, crippled beggar in stained wool, weeping on the cold floor of a city that had already forgotten his face.

"Take him down to the Black Bastion," I told Korgan. "Place him in the lower cell beneath the tide line. The cell where the sea water rises to a man's ribs twice a day. Feed him hard-tack and ditch water. Let him contemplate the wages of vanity until the damp rots his lungs."

"It will be done, Lord Protector," Korgan replied, bowing his head.

Two guards seized Lysander by the wrists. They turned and dragged his limp body across the porphyry tiles. His shattered kneecaps dragged along the green stone, leaving two wide, glistening red furrows that stretched fifty yards from the dock to the great oak portal of the hall.

His thin, pathetic whimpers echoed down the arched corridors until the heavy iron-banded doors slammed shut, cutting off his voice forever.

The three hundred nobles, merchants, and priests in the hall sat frozen in their seats, too terrified to speak, too terrified even to cough. 

Chief Magistrate Harlen stepped down from his desk, his hands trembling as he carried the heavy bronze seal-box and the carved ivory gavel of the High Assize. He approached the foot of the dais, knelt on one knee before me, and offered the symbols of sovereign authority.

"The court yields the bench to the Lord Protector," Harlen said, his head bowed low. "The law of Dun-Carrow is your law."

I took the gavel and the seal-box, passing them to Captain Korgan.

I stepped over the red stain upon the green tiles, walked up the marble steps of the high dais, and took the carved oak seat at the center of the High Assize.

I looked out over the assembly of shaken grandees, marching barons, and fat clerics.

"Bring sand and buckets of river water to clean these flags," I commanded, resting my hands upon the oak bench of the dais. "Then call the city bailiffs. The estates of the Morvan house are declared forfeit to the sovereign treasury to pay the widows' pensions. The western trade road will be cleared of robber tolls by daybreak."

I opened the registry of the High Assize, picked up the iron stylus, and instructed Chief Magistrate Harlen to call the first petition on the docket.
