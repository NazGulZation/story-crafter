# The River and the Grain

During the defense of King's Landing, when the fleet of Stannis Baratheon threatened the Blackwater, the High Septon led a procession of weeping holy men into the Red Keep. They swung silver incense burners, chanted hymns, and demanded that the royal granaries be thrown open to the city mob in the name of the Seven. The High Septon claimed that charity was the first duty of the Crown, and that divine favor would multiply the loaves.

I ordered the gold cloaks to lower their pikes and shut the storehouse gates. I told the High Septon that an army under siege survives strictly on grain in the bellies of its archers. The Faith has always traded in pious theft, using gods to steal the harvests of working men. The High Septon went back to his temple, and the capital held because our garrison ate hot porridge while our enemies starved in the field.

War is won in the dirt and the barn long before the first arrow flies. A ruler who gives away his grain gives away his crown.

Winter in the northern reaches of Dun-Carrow did not come with a gentle fall of snow. It hit like an iron gate.

By the middle of the eighth moon, the marsh-grass on the river was rimmed with hard white frost every morning. The river ran slow and black, carrying chunks of shelf-ice from the high hills down to the sea. The wind smelled of woodsmoke, rotting reeds, and the sharp bite of coming snow. 

In the market squares of the Lower Ward, the price of a bushel of rye doubled in four days.

I stood on the loading platform of the Great Granaries of Skelter-Reach, three miles south of the city walls. 

The granaries were huge timber barns built of cedar logs, their roofs sealed with tarred thatch to keep out the wet. Inside, the air was warm, dry, and thick with grain dust. Four hundred heavy sacks of hard wheat, split peas, and oats were stacked six tiers high, guarded by thirty crossbowmen in red coats bearing the Golden Lion.

A heavy thump shook the wooden pier outside, followed by the hoarse shout of boatmen throwing hemp lines.

Morcar walked up the ramp, his hood pulled down over his ears, his breath smoking in the drafty room. Beside him walked Master Garrow of the River League, a thick, broad-chested boatman with sleeves soaked in river spray.

"Every barge from the south is tied to our posts, Lord Factor," Garrow said, tossing a sheepskin manifest on a grain sack. "Ten thousand bushels of rye. Six thousand bushels of barley. Three hundred tubs of salted butter. The farmers sold us their whole crop."

"At what price?" I asked, digging my bare fingers into an open sack of rye. The grain was hard, dry, and clean of rot.

"We gave them three silver crowns a cart in hard coin," Garrow grinned, showing missing teeth. "Plus chits for iron plow-blades from your foundry. The farmers grabbed it. The Archon’s men offered them only worthless paper scrip drawn on an empty treasury."

"Good," I said. "Double the watch on the water gates. Put iron bars over the intake ditch. A starving mob burns a barn out of fury; an enemy burns it out of war."

"Speaking of enemies," Morcar whispered, pushing his spectacles up his nose. "Look down the south track."

I walked to the open door over the river road.

Thirty horsemen were splashing through the frozen mud toward our timber fence. 

They were church knights. They wore white wool coats over bright mail, their iron helmets polished clean. At their head rode an old prelate of the Dawn, bundled in thick yellow robes edged with fur, holding a gold staff topped with a sunburst. 

Beside him rode Kallista Kroll. 

The scout was mounted on a rough pony, her short red hair under an iron cap, a broad boar spear slung over her shoulder. She looked thinner than she had at the tourney, her face pinched with cold rage.

The holy riders pulled up outside the gate. The priest did not get down. He pointed his gold staff straight at my chest.

"By the holy law of the Sun-Temple!" the priest shouted, his voice shrill in the wind. "I, Inquisitor Malachai, take this grain for the holy war!"

I walked down the wooden steps, my boots thudding on the frozen ground. Captain Korgan fell in beside me, his hand on his heavy broadsword, twenty of my crossbowmen cocking their iron winches behind the fence.

I stopped four paces from the priest's horse. 

"What brings thirty priests onto my docks?" I asked.

Kallista Kroll spat in the slush. "Still hiding behind your desk, Lucan? The Archon gave the word. Lysander is marching north to smash the frost-orcs at the ridge. We need five thousand sacks of grain, and we're taking them. The church takes what it needs."

I did not look at her. To speak to a rogue scout is to stoop to her level. 

I kept my eyes on Malachai.

"Show me the writ," I said.

Malachai scowled, his jowls shaking. He pulled a rolled vellum from his yellow sleeves, showing a blob of yellow wax.

"Here is the holy decree, stamped by the High Patriarch himself! The needs of God outrank the greed of river usurers!"

"The High Patriarch rules souls, Inquisitor," I said coldly. "He does not own my grain."

I pulled a folded sheet from my dark leather coat, cracking the seal with my thumb.

"This is the Charter of the Port, signed by Archon Althaus," I said, holding up the heavy lead seal. "Line nine. *'All grain, food, and herds held under a registered factor's mark are sovereign trade reserves. They cannot be seized by church or court without paying full cash across the scale.'*"

Malachai’s face turned the color of raw liver. "Paper! Petty scribbler's tricks! The church answers to the sky, not to old ink!"

"You answer to the law, or you hang for robbery," I said, stepping forward. 

My voice was flat and quiet, but it hit them like an axe on frozen oak.

"Captain Korgan," I said.

"Aye, Lord Factor?" Korgan stepped up on my right, forty seasoned bowmen aiming their steel quarrels through the fence slits.

"If any man steps through that gate without a bag of coin, shoot the horses first. Then shoot the riders."

The church knights shifted in their saddles. Their eyes darted from the heavy timber fence to the forty steel bolts aimed straight at their throats. Holy fire felt warm in a stone cathedral; it cooled fast when faced with four-ounce iron tips that could punch through mail and bone at twenty paces.

Malachai swallowed dry, his fingers white on his golden staff.

"This is treason!" he squeaked, though he pulled his horse back two steps into the mud. "Lysander Morvan’s army will march with empty bellies! The blood of the border will be on your head, Lucan Holt!"

"Lysander may buy all the bread he wants," I said. "At the market price."

Kallista sneered, her hand tight on her spear. "Market price? You bought this grain for three crowns a wagon, you thief!"

"The price has moved," I said, looking her dead in the face. "Winter rye is twelve silver crowns a cart now. Hard coin on the scale. No church notes. No promises. No prayers."

"Twelve crowns!" Malachai yelled. "That is four times the fall rate! You are bleeding us!"

"A Lannister always pays his debts, Inquisitor," I said softly. "And those who buy from me pay mine. In full."

"You aren't a Lannister!" Kallista barked, fury and doubt fighting across her scarred face. "You're Lucan! You're a dirt-poor clod from the Weald who used to bawl when a mule went lame!"

I held her gaze with absolute, dead quiet.

She stopped talking. Her mouth hung open, a creeping chill wiping the red from her cheeks. In my eyes, she found no trace of the boy she had mocked. She saw only a cold predator who had drowned three hundred rebels in the dark vaults of Castamere without losing a wink of sleep.

"Take your priests and clear off," I told Malachai. "If you want bread for Morvan’s war, bring four hundred gold sovereigns to my tower before noon tomorrow. After noon, the price is fourteen crowns."

Malachai turned his horse in frantic haste, mud flying from the hooves. "The Archon will break your house for this!"

"The Archon will do what his creditors tell him to do," I called after him, as the troop galloped away into the freezing mist.

Korgan spat into the slush, wiping water from his scabbard. "The High Inquisitor won't take this kindly, Lord Factor. He will complain to the High Council."

"Let him speak to the council," I told him, turning back toward the granary. "The grain remains under guard. Post twenty pikemen across the dock gates and check every wagon leaving the wharf."

I walked back inside the warehouse, instructed the weigh-master to record the morning delivery tallies, and shut the oak door against the sleet.
