# The Lion's Throne

*And so he spoke, and so he spoke,*
*that lord of Castamere,*
*But now the rains weep o'er his hall,*
*with no one there to hear.*
*Yes now the rains weep o'er his hall,*
*and not a soul to hear.*

Empty halls and cold stone are the natural monument of men who trade in vanity. They build palaces of silk, dance through glittering seasons, and boast of their birthright, believing that loud laughter can turn aside the winter frost. In the end, the singing dies, the banners rot in the damp, and the rain washes away their footprints until not a soul remains to speak their names. Power is not a song; power is the unyielding stone that remains when the floodwaters recede.

***

The salt wind blew off the Grey Gulf, cold and clean, smelling of cedar smoke, pine resin, and drying cod.

I stood upon the highest battery of the Sea Citadel of Dun-Carrow, three hundred feet above the crashing surf. The winter sun was descending behind the western crags, painting the slate rooftops, granite quays, and dark basalt towers in hues of copper and crimson.

From sixty wall-towers across the citadel perimeter hung long red banners, each bearing the rampant golden lion of House Lannister. They snapped in the evening gale with rhythmic, cracking reports, the gold silk thread catching the final rays of daylight.

Along the battery parapet, twenty heavy torsion scorpions sat mounted on swivel blocks of seasoned white oak, their iron frames oiled against the salt air, their winches wound tight and covered with greased rawhide. Behind each weapon stood a three-man crew of City Wardens in red wool livery, their quilted jacks reinforced with steel plates, their faces hardened by months of strict drilling. Every man knew his range markers to the yard; every crew could loose four heavy steel-tipped bolts a minute into the harbor mouth.

Below the citadel cliffs, the deep-water harbor hummed with the steady pulse of an ordered machine. 

Fifty-four heavy merchant cogs and three-masted carracks lay berthed along the stone quays, their hulls laden with Norrmark iron bars, Ost-Reach hard wheat, cured hams, tallow casks, and southern dye-woods. Along the wharves, three thousand dockers, draymen, and warehousemen moved cargo with quiet, practiced discipline under the watchful eyes of the factor bailiffs.

No one rioted for bread in the streets of Dun-Carrow.

The sovereign granaries held ninety thousand bushels of hard winter wheat, bought during the autumn harvest glut at fixed wholesale rates and sold through municipal distribution centers at steady, affordable prices. The bakers had flour; the smiths had coal; the weavers had wool. 

Through the cobblestone avenues below, the City Wardens marched in disciplined columns of fifty. Their iron coats were burnished bright, their eight-foot boar-spears rested upright upon their shoulders, and their monthly wage of four silver crowns was paid directly from the central counting room on the first morning of every month without fail. 

Order had taken root where chaos once festered. Methodical administration had crushed aristocratic vanity into dust.

Archon Althaus lived thirty miles south of the capital, in an elegant country villa surrounded by apple orchards and trout streams. He had his fat roast capons, his sweet honeyed wine, his yellow roses, and a generous annual pension of eight thousand silver crowns paid faithfully by my clerks. The old man was thoroughly content. To secure his peaceful retirement, the Archon had affixed his great seal to the Regency Covenant, permanently transferring all executive, military, judicial, and fiscal powers of the realm to the Lord Protector.

I held the Grand Duchy firmly within my fist.

I lifted a silver goblet to my lips, savoring the dry, tart red vintage pressed from the stony hillsides of the upper valley. It was cold, astringent on the tongue, and free of cloying sugar.

I rested both forearms upon the chilled granite parapet, gazing out across the endless expanse of gray ocean.

In this world, men had attempted to mock me.

Lysander Morvan had tossed ten copper farthings against my ribs in a muddy alleyway, laughing that I was an errand boy, believing that his symmetrical jaw and shiny magic sword elevated him to a living god.

Now, Lysander sat in a damp stone cell twenty feet beneath the high-tide mark in the bowels of the Black Bastion. 

Master Turnkey Godric brought me the prison inspection rolls on the first of every week. Twice every day, the freezing ocean brine pushed through the drainage gratings, rising to the prisoner's armpits and forcing him to stand on the tips of his toes upon a slimy stone bench. His shattered kneecaps had fused into grotesque, crooked lumps of bone and gristle, leaving him unable to take two steps without screaming. He had no mirror, no velvet cloaks, and no fawning court to applaud his swagger. He chewed moldy ship's biscuit, drank brackish well water, and listened to the rats scuttling across his damp straw in the dark.

Vivienne Montclair wore a coarse gray wool smock, sweeping dust, dead flies, and parchment trimmings from the stone aisles of the Grand Library for two copper bits a week. 

Her magical channels had been scorched to dead ash by the bog-iron grounding lattice during her frantic attempt to break the hall guard. Her noble family in the southern hills had formally struck her name from their house register, refusing to acknowledge a disgraced daughter whose debts were owned by The Golden Lion Factor. She subsisted on thin cabbage broth and dry rye heels provided by the very apprentice scribes she had once threatened with her wand, sleeping on a straw pallet in the cellar beside the coal bins.

Kallista Kroll swung a sixteen-pound iron sledgehammer in the high granite quarries of Skelter-Ridge, breaking road-metal from sunrise to dusk in the freezing mountain wind. 

Her slender archer's fingers were split by bloody calluses, her boots were patched with scrap rawhide, and she slept in a draughty timber bunkhouse under the watchful iron arbalests of my wardens. Every fortnight, she produced twenty tons of crushed granite ballast for the western turnpike, earning four copper farthings toward her share of the company's damages.

And Rosamund Cleary.

Three days ago, Master Factor Morcar had delivered the monthly accounting rolls from the river-mouth salt works.

The village girl who had once dreamed of silk gowns and a champion's bed waded knee-deep in the stinging brine pans from dawn till dusk. Her soft hands were cracked and white with raw salt crystals. Her golden hair was matted with dried salt spray and tucked beneath a coarse linen rag. She lived in a drafty bunkhouse with forty washerwomen, eating dried salt herring and black rye bread, working off her apportioned share of the four thousand silver crowns she had helped squander.

The ledger recorded her progress with dispassionate clarity: twelve silver crowns credited against her debt over ninety days of labor. She had seventy years of salt-shoveling remaining on her contract.

She had demanded her freedom; she had demanded luxury.

Now she received her exact contractual wages, counted out to the final brass farthing.

The heavy, measured thud of iron-shod boots echoed upon the stone stairs behind me.

I did not turn around.

Captain Korgan emerged onto the battery platform, his breastplate ringing against his sword belt. The veteran officer wore a splendid coat of blackened steel plates embossed with the gilded roaring lion of Dun-Carrow upon the breast, a long broadsword sheathed at his hip.

"Lord Protector," Korgan said, halting three paces behind me and bowing his head with crisp military precision.

"Report, Korgan," I said, keeping my gaze fixed upon a merchant carrack clearing the harbor boom under full canvas.

"The northern border bastions at Skelter-Ridge are complete, my lord," Korgan reported, pride resonating in his gruff voice. "Three interlocking star redoubts, built of dressed granite blocks six feet thick. Each redoubt is garrisoned by two hundred veteran pikemen, forty heavy repeating scorpions, and six months of salted pork and hard biscuit. The frost-orc tribes have scattered sixty leagues back into the ice fields. Their chieftains are slaughtering one another over dry seal meat; they will not look south again for twenty years."

"And the western trade road?"

"Completely secured," Korgan replied. "Four fortified toll stations along the high pass. Every timber wagon, wool cart, and ore train coming down from the hills is inspected, taxed at the seven percent sovereign tariff, and assigned an armed warden escort down to the docks. We collected eleven thousand silver crowns in road tolls during the past moon alone."

"Good," I said, setting my goblet upon the granite coping. "A road without a toll is an invitation to highwaymen, and an untaxed merchant is an insolent merchant. Ensure the road crews receive their salted beef and beer rations on schedule. I want the western turnpike paved with crushed ballast before the spring thaws begin."

"Understood, my lord," Korgan said. He paused, his boots shifting slightly on the stone flags. He drew a polished leather cylinder from beneath his arm. "One further matter has arisen. An imperial dispatch galley arrived at the Water Gate two hours ago, flying the golden hawk standard of the Southern Sun-Empire."

I turned slowly to face him, folding my hands behind my back. "Speak."

Korgan unfastened the bronze cap of the cylinder and withdrew a roll of heavy purple parchment, bound with gold wire and sealed with seven large discs of imperial yellow beeswax.

"The Emperor of Sol-Khar has taken notice of our victory over Mor-Goth," Korgan said, his weathered brow furrowing. "The imperial scribes claim that Dun-Carrow was an imperial tributary two centuries ago under the Old dynasty. The Emperor demands that Your Grace journey south to the golden palace of Sol-Khar at the spring equinox to kneel before the Sun Throne, pay forty thousand gold ducats in back tribute, and disband our provincial pike regiments."

Korgan looked up, his jaw set in a hard line. "The envoy arrived with forty cataphracts in gilded armor. He waits in the Great Hall below, loudly demanding an immediate audience with the Lord Protector. He boasts that if we refuse the Emperor's terms, thirty thousand imperial legionnaires and three hundred siege engines will cross the Great River the moment the ice melts."

I reached out and took the purple parchment from Korgan's hands.

I did not break the seven yellow wax seals. I did not bother to unroll the vellum to read the Emperor's grand titles or list of ancestral claims.

Thirty thousand men. An empire that believed ancient parchment, purple silk, and haughty heralds could intimidate a ruler who understood the mathematical mechanics of war.

"The Emperor’s scribes understand court poetry, Korgan," I said quietly, turning the heavy scroll in my fingers. "They do not understand grain carts. How wide is the Great River at the border ford?"

"Four hundred paces across the shallows, my lord," Korgan replied. "Deeper in the spring run-off."

"And the roads on the southern bank?"

"Heavy black clay through twenty leagues of willow marsh," Korgan said, a sharp glint entering his eyes as he followed my thought. "In spring, a wagon sinks to its axles in four hours."

"Thirty thousand infantry and ten thousand armored horse cannot march through twenty leagues of black mud without eating," I told him, looking out over the sea. "They require ninety thousand pounds of flour a day, ten thousand draft mules, and four timber bridges across the river channel. If they march in the spring, they will arrive at our border starving, with their draft beasts dead of rot and their baggage train abandoned in the swamp."

I stepped back to the edge of the parapet, holding the bronze cylinder out over the three-hundred-foot drop.

I opened my fingers.

The bronze tube tumbled through the cold ocean air, spinning end over end, until it struck the jagged black granite rocks of the sea wall with a sharp, metallic *crack*, swallowed in an instant by the churning, foam-flecked tide.

Korgan’s eyes widened, but his broad shoulders remained rigid as an iron bar.

"My lord?"

I looked south, across the rolling pine ridges and the wide gray river that wound toward the southern plains.

"Go down to the Great Hall, Korgan," I said, my voice dropping into that cold, stony timbre that had commanded kings and hosts. "Inform the imperial envoy that Dun-Carrow pays no tribute to distant thrones. Tell him that the roads of this realm are paved with cold iron, our granaries are filled with sovereign grain, and our walls are manned by soldiers who know how to hold a pike."

I turned away from the sea and walked toward the arched portal leading down into the torchlit citadel.

"And what shall I say if their thirty thousand legionnaires march across the river in the spring, Lord Protector?" Korgan asked, his hand dropping to the iron hilt of his broadsword.

I halted beneath the stone archway, framed beneath the great crimson banner of the Roaring Lion whipping in the sea wind.

"Tell him to listen to the song they sing in the valleys of Dun-Carrow," I said, looking back over my shoulder with cold, unyielding certainty.

"What song is that, my lord?"

"Tell him that a Lannister always pays his debts."

I turned and walked through the heavy doorway into the torchlit halls of the fortress, the massive oak portal swinging shut behind me with the cold, final weight of an empire built of iron and blood.
