# A Lesson in Command

At the Battle of the Green Fork, young knights and hot-blooded squires rode to my command tent, shouting for the vanguard. They wanted to charge across the river on armored destriers, howling old war-cries, eager to seek out northern lords in single combat to win songs from the bards. My brother Kevan listened with patience. I listened with cold disgust.

A lord who fights duels on the front line is only a sergeant in fine plate. I placed the undisciplined mountain clans on my left flank under Shagga and Timett, knowing their wild ways would tempt the northern foot into overextending their advance across the river. I held the disciplined Lannister pikes and heavy horse in tight, quiet reserve beneath the ridge until the enemy broke their own line in the rush for easy plunder. When the trap snapped shut, we rode through their scattered ranks like scythes through wheat.

War is geometry and iron will. A line holds when each man stands like a cog in an iron winch, locked tight to the crank. A general who forgets the winch to swing his own sword has already lost the field.

Cow horns brayed through the sleet at the third watch of the night.

The harsh call cut from the high timber watchtowers along the North Palisade of Dun-Carrow. Below the mud ramparts, the drainage ditch had frozen into jagged ridges of grey slush. The north wind blew down from the crags, carrying the smell of wet pine, scorched horsehair, and hot wood-pitch hauled up the ladders in iron cauldrons.

I stood on the catwalk of the North Gatehouse, clad in black iron plates under a heavy cloak of boiled wool. Beside me stood Captain Korgan, his scarred hands resting on the timber railing, his eyes squinting into the driving sleet.

"Look at the river bend, Lord Factor," Korgan muttered, pointing a thick finger into the dark.

Two miles north, along the willow banks of the Skelter, six farmsteads burned red against the low snow clouds. Shouts rolled over the frozen marsh—guttural, howling war-cries like wolves tearing the entrails of an elk. The forward scouts of Warlord Mor-Goth had arrived. Two hundred frost-orcs riding shaggy winter wargs, probing the ditches and palisades to test the readiness of our garrison.

Below us in the courtyard, the town militia had gone mad.

A hundred and fifty peasant levies in padded canvas coats ran in blind, frantic circles. Men dropped their twelve-foot ash pikes in the mud, trampling the shafts under their boots. Teamsters cursed as terrified draft oxen kicked through their traces, overturning a wagon of iron-tipped stakes. Sergeants screamed conflicting orders that no one could hear over the whistling gale.

"Look at them," Korgan growled, spitting into the slush. "Green boys and tavern sweeps. The first sniff of orc blood, and they soil their breeches. If the beasts hit the timber now, the gate is lost."

"A rabble behaves like a rabble until an officer gives it an anchor," I told him. "Where is the garrison captain?"

Before Korgan could answer, the double doors of the Sun-Lance Pavilion banged open against the stone wall of the keep.

Lysander Morvan rode out at the head of twenty mounted retainers. 

His plate armor had been polished to a silver mirror sheen, catching the orange glare of the burning farms across the river. Across his broad shoulders hung a cape of white samite lined with ermine, fluttering in the sleet. In his right fist blazed the Sun-Sword—a great six-foot blade of white-hot solar fire that lit the whole courtyard like the midday sun.

"To me, sons of the Dawn!" Lysander shouted, rearing his white charger and lifting his blazing blade to the dark sky. "The foul beasts dare tread on holy ground! Follow my lance, and we purge their filth from the world!"

The young riders cheered, waving their lances in the air.

Lysander dug his golden spurs into his mount, charging straight out through the open portcullis. He did not wait for the crossbowmen to mount the ramparts. He set no guards at the postern gate. He formed no reserve line behind the breach, nor did he order the pikes into a defensive hedgehog to cover his flanks. He galloped blindly out into the sleet-slicked dark, hunting for glory like a drunken squire chasing foxes across an open meadow.

Vivienne Montclair rode twenty paces behind him on an ambling grey mare, tossing handfuls of silver dust into the gale to maintain a shimmering wind-shield around the charging horsemen.

Fifty yards past the ditch, Lysander found his prize.

A frost-orc chieftain, eight feet tall, clad in cured mammoth bone and swinging a double-bitted iron axe, stood atop an irrigation dike. The brute roared, slamming his axe against his chest plate.

Lysander yelled his challenge, vaulted from the saddle with a theatrical flourish, and engaged the beast in single combat.

The meadow became a circus of light. The Sun-Sword flared, striking bone and iron with sharp cracks of ozone that echoed off the cliffs. Lysander spun, parried, leaped, and struck, his golden curls whipping in the gale, carving bright arcs in the dark while his twenty retainers formed a wide ring with their lances to cheer him on.

The duel was a magnificent spectacle. The bards in the taverns would sing of it for months.

And while Lysander danced with his prize in the tall grass, forty frost-orcs crept through the frozen reeds of the drainage canal, slipped past his glowing blade entirely, and hit the unguarded postern gate under the eastern curtain wall.

Screams broke from the ditch.

The orcs smashed the wood postern down with heavy iron-shod logs. Three dozen peasant levies, left without an officer to hold their ground, broke on the spot. They threw down their pikes and ran screaming for the inner streets. The orcs poured through the gap, their bone cleavers hacking down boys from behind, turning the slush black with blood.

Captain Korgan’s jaw set hard as stone. "The postern is breached! The boys are routing! They’ll be inside the granaries in two minutes!"

"A line only breaks when the captain lets it break," I said, my voice flat as cold slate. "Sound the iron bell. Gather seventy syndicate veterans from the barracks. Roll four barrels of pitch from the upper storehouse."

"Right away, Lord Factor!"

I did not draw a sword. A lord who swings steel gives away the battle; his eyes belong on the whole board, not on the throat of one pawn.

I walked down the wood stairs into the muddy yard.

The running levies surged toward the inner town gate, their eyes rolling in their heads with blind animal terror. I stepped directly into the center of the muddy lane, backed by thirty syndicate crossbowmen clad in dark crimson wool.

"Hold," I said.

My voice hit the yard like an iron mallet striking an anvil. It carried the absolute, freezing weight of the Hand of the King who had held the walls of King's Landing against Stannis Baratheon’s fire-ships.

A running sergeant tried to shove past me, his breath coming in ragged gasps. "They're through the postern! They're cutting us to pieces!"

Captain Korgan took one step and drove the steel pommel of his broadsword into the sergeant's mouth. Teeth cracked with a wet crunch; the man dropped into the mud, spitting blood and teeth onto the stones.

"The next man who runs past this post takes a bolt through the eye," I told the crowd, looking over the huddled, shivering conscripts. "Form ranks. Three deep. First rank to one knee, pikes set in the mud at forty-five degrees. Second rank lock your shafts over their shoulders. Third rank, bring the iron hooks."

The men stared at me.

They saw no golden halo. They saw no radiant smile. They saw only a tall, dark man in black plates whose eyes held no doubt, no pity, and the cold, certain promise of an early grave for any man who stepped back.

Fear of the orcs shrank before the colder certainty of my rope.

With trembling hands, the levies scrambled into line. In forty heartbeats, sixty steel-tipped pikes locked together across the narrow lane between the stone granary and the gatehouse wall.

Ten paces behind them, I drew up my syndicate crossbowmen in two ranks.

"Aim for the bellies," I told them. "Fire by tens. When the front rank shoots, the second steps up. Keep the iron winches turning."

Twenty frost-orcs came howling around the corner of the smithy, their jagged bone cleavers dripping with conscript blood, their eyes glowing red in the dark.

"Hold," I said.

Thirty paces. Twenty paces. Fifteen paces.

"Fire."

Ten steel bolts ripped through the cold air like tearing cloth.

At fifteen paces, the four-ounce iron quarrels punched clean through bone breastplates, hide coats, and gristle. Five orcs dropped hard, skidding into the slush with their lungs torn open.

"Second rank, fire."

Another ten bolts slammed into the charging pack. The front rank tripped over the fallen, crashing straight into the bristling wall of planted pikes. Two brute warriors ran full onto the points, their own charging weight driving the ash shafts clean through their backs.

"Cauldrons," I said.

From the catwalk above the lane, three of Korgan's veterans tipped two barrels of hot wood-pitch down upon the postern entrance.

The burning black tar caught twelve orcs in the narrow bottleneck. Howls tore from their throats as the pitch ate through skin and muscle, sending them thrashing in agony into the frozen ditch. The rest of the pack saw the slaughter, turned on their heels, and ran for the river.

"Advance three paces," I told the pike-line. "Secure the postern. Put a bolt into every wounded beast on the ground. Leave none to breathe."

Cold butcher's work. In seven minutes, the gap was sealed, the lane cleared, and the North Palisade held. Forty-two frost-orcs lay dead in the mud.

A bright flare lit the outer field.

The outer timber gates groaned open. Lysander Morvan rode back into the yard, holding the severed head of the orc chief aloft by its matted hair.

His plate armor was splashed with black blood, his golden curls wet with melting sleet. Behind him trotted his twenty horsemen, laughing, slapping their saddles, and singing hymns of victory to the Goddess.

Rosamund Cleary ran out from the porch of the chapel, carrying a heavy fur cloak, her eyes shining with adoration as she grabbed Lysander's stirrup.

"The Champion! The Sun-Gilded has slain the Beast of the Crags!" she cried, her voice ringing across the courtyard. "Dun-Carrow is saved!"

Then Lysander looked down the eastern lane.

He saw the forty-two orc corpses stacked like cordwood. He saw sixty levies standing in tight, quiet ranks, their pikes dripping black blood. And he saw me standing upon the raised timber platform, marking off names on Korgan's muster roll with a lead pencil.

Lysander's grin died. His jaw tightened in defensive rage as he turned his horse toward the platform.

"What is this?" he demanded, looking at the dead beasts. "Who gave the order to engage without my word?"

I did not look up from the sheet.

"Fifty-four peasant boys died at the postern gate while you played with that head in the meadow, Morvan," I said, my voice flat as cold slate.

Rosamund stepped toward the platform, gripping Lysander's stirrup leather, her face flushed with venomous arrogance.

"Hold your tongue, Lucan!" she spat, pointing a finger at my face. "Lysander killed their chief! He saved the whole town with the light of the Goddess! While you hid behind these walls like a coward, he shed his blood for this realm!"

I turned my head.

For the first time since that night in the pavilion, I looked Rosamund Cleary dead in the eyes.

I did not scowl. I did not raise a brow. I looked at her the way a man looks at a beetle on his boot before setting his heel down.

She stopped cold.

The sneer died on her lips. Her hand slipped from the leather. A shiver ran down her neck as she saw the truth: her words, her looks, and her betrayal meant nothing to me. Less than nothing. She was an insect buzzing against an iron wall.

"Captain Korgan," I said, handing the casualty roll to the veteran.

"My lord?"

"Clean the grease from the pikes. Double the watch on the water gates. Have the teamsters drag these orc carcasses to the lime pits before noon. And if this boy tries to lead another charge without leaving a rear guard, lock the portcullis behind him and let him sleep in the ditch."

Lysander drew an inch of his glowing blade, his face dark red with fury. "You insolent clerk—"

"Draw that sword one inch," I said, turning my back on him to inspect the granary lock, "and my bowmen will put three bolts through your horse's neck. You played the hero for the crowd, Morvan. Now drag your dead out of my lane."

Lysander's fingers trembled on his grip. He looked at the forty crossbowmen in crimson wool lining the catwalk, their arbalests cocked, their iron levers resting against their shoulders, their eyes cold and unblinking. He looked at Korgan, whose thumb rested upon the pommel of a heavy broadsword.

Slowly, his jaw working in silent rage, Lysander shoved the blade back into its scabbard with a dull clatter. He turned his white horse without another word, driving his spurs into its ribs and galloping toward the High Ward, leaving his retainers to gawk at the slaughter.

Rosamund stood alone in the mud for three heartbeats, clutching the fur cloak to her chest, looking between the dead boys and the closed doors of the granary. She opened her mouth to yell, found no one listening, hitched her green velvet skirt up out of the black slush, and ran after her champion's horse.

I walked the length of the palisade in the grey dawn light. 

The sleet had stopped, replaced by a bitter, quiet frost that froze the blood on the ash pikes into dark crusts. Korgan fell in beside me, his iron-shod boots crunching in the slush.

"The boys held, Lord Factor," Korgan said, wiping grease from his broadsword. "They ran at first, but they held once the pikes formed."

"They held because they had nowhere left to run," I said. "Order eighty more pikes from the Black Ditch foundries. Double the rations of salt pork for the night watch, and ensure the surgeons in the lower barn have clean lint and hot water."

I walked through the gatehouse arch, checked the watch roster posted on the guardroom door, and headed up the stone stairs to write my morning report for the council.
