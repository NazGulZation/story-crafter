# Chapter 1: The Deep Patrol

The saddle-leather creaks beneath your weight as your gelding steps through the black mire of the drainage ditch. Dawn broke three hours ago behind the cold stone parapets of Thornwall Garrison, but here beneath the primeval canopy of the Ashmere Weald, the morning light dies in the tangled crowns of ancient black oak, towering ironwood, and weeping silver birch. A grey, sodden mist hangs in the hollows between the mossy trunks, smelling of rotting leaf-mold, damp wool, sweat-soaked horsehair, and the sharp, resinous tang of spruce needles crushed beneath iron horseshoes.

You pull your wool-lined leather gauntlets tighter against the creeping chill. The air carries an unnatural, suffocating stillness that sets your teeth on edge. Five leagues to the south, the garrison cookstoves will be smoking with damp peat, and the sergeant of the watch will be carving cold salt-beef for the relief guard while the younger squires scrub tallow and grease from the mess benches. You know that life intimately—the clatter of wooden trenchers, the sour smell of stale small beer, the relentless routine of grease, whetstone, and inspection that has defined your five years of service.

As the third son of a minor landed freeholder near the southern chalk hills, you inherited neither acres nor tenant rents. Your father bought you a coarse wool gambeson, a second-hand broadsword with a notched crossguard, and a commission to the northern border marches to keep you from becoming another idle mouth at his table. Three weeks into your posting at Thornwall, you remain desperate to prove your worth to the garrison captains. You volunteered for the outer perimeter patrol when the senior riders balked at the gathering autumn fog, eager to show that the blood of House Wetherne still carries an edge.

Yet the border-markers have grown sparse. Half an hour back, you passed the last blazed trunk—a stunted Scots pine whose scarred bark wept pale, amber gum where the garrison scouts had notched it six summers ago. Beyond that marker, the beaten sheep-tracks ran dry, choked off by dense screens of thorny blackberry canes and elder scrub. Fresh deer-sign lured your mount across the stony bed of the Alderwash, and now the woods around you feel ancient, alien, and sovereign.

The oak trunks stand thicker than three men with outstretched arms, their grey bark furrowed like weathered granite. Overhead, the interlocking canopy weaves so tight that the sky vanishes behind a seamless ceiling of gnarled boughs and Spanish moss. The temperature climbs with every dozen paces—a strange, humid warmth that smells of sweet clover, heavy honeysuckle, and warm wood-sap, utterly out of season for late autumn.

Your bay gelding snorts, his dark ears pinning flat against his poll. He balks, his iron-shod hooves digging into the thick, spongy bed of rotting leaves.

"Steady, boy," you murmur, leaning forward to pat the damp warmth of his neck. The horse trembles beneath you, the powerful muscles of his withers bunched tight as bridge timber. 

A strange vibration pulses up through your stirrup irons. It is a rhythmic, subterranean thud deep within the loam—slow, deliberate, and resonant, like the pumping of an underground bellows or the beating of a colossal wooden heart. The natural sounds of the border woods have vanished entirely. No thrush calls from the hazel thickets; no woodpecker drums against the dead pine; no wind stirs the needles of the high canopy. 

You reach down with your right hand and ease your broadsword two inches from its grease-stained leather scabbard. The oiled steel clears the throat of the sheath with a clean, dry rasp that seems swallowed by the heavy air.

Before you can turn the horse's head back toward the southern trail, the forest floor buckles.

Thick, fibrous roots erupt through the leaf-litter like striking serpents. They wrap around your mount’s forelegs with wet, tearing cracks. The gelding screams—a high, panicked sound that tears through the humid silence—and plunges sideways into the fern-choked hollow. You kick your boots free of the stirrup irons as the animal collapses, throwing your shoulder hard into the mossy base of an uprooted alder.

The impact knocks the wind from your chest in an explosive gasp. Your steel kettle-hat clatters into the undergrowth, rolling against a patch of glowing bracket fungi. Cold mud smears across your left cheek and the brass buckles of your arming doublet.

You roll immediately onto your belly, gasping for breath, your fingers clamping around the corded grip of your sword. You drag yourself to your knees, blade raised in a high guard stance, blinking the grit and mud from your watering eyes.

Your horse is gone. The roots did not crush the beast; they dragged the thrashing gelding backward through the dense briars with terrifying speed, sealing the wall of brambles behind him as though the earth had swallowed him whole. His panicked whinny fades into the green gloom, smothered by the dense carpet of the canopy.

"Stand clear!" you shout into the mist, your voice sounding thin and small against the massive trunks. "I am Aldric Wetherne, sworn border-rider of the Thornwall Garrison! I bear royal steel and royal warrant!"

A soft, rustling laughter answers you from the canopy above. It sounds like autumn leaves scraping across dry slate, followed by a low, throat-catching murmur that vibrates with ancient amusement.

From the mossy trunk of a towering black walnut to your left, the bark shifts and twists. Wood fibers peel back like supple muscle, twisting and braiding into smooth, athletic limbs. A woman steps free from the heartwood—tall, lithe, her skin the dark, polished grain of seasoned walnut, patterned with fine growth-rings that ripple across her collarbones and bare ribs. Her eyes are bright citrine, glowing with the amber light of trapped sunlight, and her fingernails taper into hard, curved thorn-talons. She wears no cloth, only a garland of dark, pointed leaves clinging to her hips.

To your right, a second figure detaches from a silver birch trunk. Her skin is pale and paper-smooth, peeling in delicate white curls along her slender waist to reveal tender, rose-tinted flesh beneath. Her hair hangs like long strands of pale river-moss, beaded with heavy droplets of fragrant, sweet resin. She looks upon you with wide, child-like wonder, her slender nostrils flaring as she catches your scent.

Two more emerge from behind a curtain of hanging briars. One possesses the silver-grey bark of an ash tree, her fingers twitching like creeping ivy as her lichen-green eyes assess your musculature; the other is broad-hipped and heavy-set, split-oak plates covering her powerful thighs, her body glistening with a steady, honey-thick weeping of fragrant sap that drips from her cleft and ribs into the loam.

Four wood-fey. Dryads of the Ashmere.

The border-guards at the garrison tavern spoke of them in hushed, fearful tones over cheap tallow candles—tales of lost scouts, stripped armor found hanging from willow boughs like molted skin, and men who wandered back to the stockade weeks later with vacant, blissed smiles and fine bark growing beneath their fingernails.

You grip your sword hilt with both hands, stepping back until your shoulders press against the rough trunk of an ancient oak. "Back," you warn, leveling the steel point toward the walnut-skinned huntress. "Keep your distance. The crown claims these lands."

The walnut dryad—Lashvine—bares her teeth, revealing small, pointed pegs of white heartwood. She drops into a low, predatory crouch, her talons gouging deep furrows into the damp loam. "He brandishes cold metal in the sovereign bower of the Ashmere," she purrs, her voice carrying a dry, scraping resonance. "He speaks of crowns while standing on our roots."

"His blood smells of salt and fresh marrow," whispers the pale birch creature, tilting her head. She takes a slow step forward, her bare toes sinking into the moss. "Young. Unbroken. The marrow is rich and hot."

"The thighs are thick from the saddle," Knothollow notes, her ash-barked hand tracing an invisible line through the humid air. "The chest is broad. The furrow will be deep."

You tense to swing your blade, but the oak trunk at your back groans with sudden life.

Thick, woody tendrils shoot out from the bark behind you, wrapping around your forearms and pinning your wrists against the wood. You wrench your arms with all your strength, boots skidding through the wet loam, but the wood holds with the unyielding strength of an iron vise. The broadsword slips from your numbing fingers, plunging point-first into the soft moss between your boots.

Vines whip around your shins, thighs, and waist, binding you tight to the trunk. The thorns press through the boiled leather of your doublet, pricking the skin of your ribs and hips, drawing tiny points of heat.

A fifth figure steps into the clearing.

The earth shudders under her stride. She stands nearly seven feet tall, crowned with dark, spiky yew-needles and grey lichen that trails down her broad shoulders like a queen's mantle. Her body is built of ancient black yew-bark, cracked and furrowed by centuries of storms, with deep amber seams glowing along her sternum and hips like smoldering embers. Her presence carries the heavy, suffocating scent of crushed yew, ozone, and wet heartwood.

The younger four bow their heads, stepping aside to make way for their matron.

The Elder dryad, Yewthara, approaches until her towering frame blocks out what little light filters through the trees. She reaches out with a massive hand. Her fingers are tipped with polished black wood, warm to the touch, and she presses her knuckles under your jaw, forcing your chin upward.

Her amber eyes bore into yours. They hold no pupils—only swirling pools of liquid resin that seem to see straight through your armor, through your skin, down into the hot coursing blood of your veins.

"Three hundred winters," she speaks. Her voice rumbles like heavy timber grinding in a tempest, vibrating through the wood at your back and straight into your spine. "Three hundred winters the Ashmere has withered under the axes of the stone-builders. Our roots grow hollow. Our sap runs thin without seed."

She lowers her gaze, her heavy thumb tracing down the line of your throat, over your collarbone, stopping over the racing thud of your heart.

"You wander past our cairns, little soldier," she rumbles, a faint, ancient smile cracking the furrowed bark of her lips. "You bring warm blood, strong bones, and a full vessel. The grove will take what you owe to the soil."

The younger four close in around you. The pale birch dryad reaches for the buckles of your cuirass, her supple fingers undoing the brass tongue with effortless skill. The walnut huntress grips your thighs, her thorn-talons pricking through your woolen breeches. The split-oak dryad rests her warm, sap-slicked palms against your exposed belly as the armor falls away into the ferns.

The bindings hold you fast against the oak. Your heart hammers against your ribs like a trapped bird. You are stripped of your blade, your mount, and your armor, caught in the deep heart of the sovereign wood.
