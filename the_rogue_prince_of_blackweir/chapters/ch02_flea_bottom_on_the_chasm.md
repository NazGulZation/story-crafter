# Chapter 2: Flea Bottom on the Chasm

The freezing rain of Blackweir did not fall in clean sheets. It whipped sideways off the sheer lip of the Cormorant Chasm, driving sulfurous grit from the smelting furnaces straight into my borrowed eyes.

I pulled the collar of Caspar’s damp woolen doublet tight against my throat, walking down the slick cobblestones of Tallow Street. With every stride, I tested the machine. I rolled my ankles over the wet stones, feeling the bite of the heel-cords. I worked my shoulders, cataloging the stiffness in the trapezoids and the hollow ache in the small of the back.

*Flesh of an ink-pusher,* I thought, letting the icy water wash the stink of the tavern from my hair. *The chest is too narrow. The lungs burn after fifty paces of uphill grade. The knuckles are stiff from damp parchment, and the belly is hollow from five days of moldy rations. But the bones are straight, and the reflexes... the reflexes will obey.*

In my mind, two lifetimes were settling into an uneasy armistice. 

Caspar Thorne’s memories were an orderly library of pack-mule arithmetic: beast-carcass yields, grain spoilage rates, topographical elevations, and pathetic, unrequited yearnings for a girl who had traded him for a gilded sun-brooch. I took the yearnings and drowned them in bile. But the maps, the terrain surveys, and the supply manifests—those I kept. Information was the first currency of war.

I knew this kind of city.

Long before I wore a crown in the Stepstones, I had ruled the gutters of King’s Landing. Flea Bottom, the Fishmarket, the Pig’s Foot—I had run with the thieves and cutthroats before I forged the Gold Cloaks. A frontier city was always the same beast: fat merchants at the throat, armored fools at the gates, and hungry rats in the bowels.

The highborn lords and temple priests looked down on the dregs of the Sump with pious revulsion, stepping over shivering dock-urchins and crippled miners coughing black spit into the drains. They saw vermin. 

I never saw vermin. I saw raw iron waiting for the flame. 

In my youth, the high court had sneered when I gathered the beggars, the disinherited second sons, and the starving cutpurses of Flea Bottom and draped them in gold wool. But when I put castle-forged steel into their hands and pride into their broken spines, they dragged arrogant lords from their litters and made the capital kneel. I had always harbored a wicked, morbid satisfaction in that alchemy: taking the men society had ground into the mud, forging them into weapons, and watching them ruthlessly trample the very masters who had spat upon them.

I ducked under the sodden awning of a cooper’s shop to check my wealth.

Fourteen copper bits. A cracked bronze buckle.

I almost laughed aloud into the rain. Fourteen coppers. In my youth, I had tossed more gold to a singing whore in the Street of Silk just to hear her tune twice.

Then I felt the shadow on my heels.

It was not magic. It was the ancient, prickly tingling at the nape of my neck—the predator’s compass that had kept my throat whole through three assassination attempts on Bloodstone and twenty alley brawls in the capital. Three men. Heavy, ungreased boots trying to step lightly on the slick shale cobbles. The faint, rhythmic *slap* of leather sheaths against heavy buff coats.

I didn't hasten my pace. I didn't reach for a weapon.

Instead, I took a deliberate left into Gallow-Gate Lane. It was a blind gut—a narrow, filthy chute hemmed in by the sheer limestone foundation of the lower aqueduct, ending against a dead wall of weeping stone. A solitary tallow lantern hung from a rusted iron bracket overhead, casting sickly yellow rings across heaps of sodden sheep-fleece and rotting barrel staves.

I walked three paces from the dead end, turned slowly, and leaned my back against the damp buttress.

The three men stepped into the mouth of the alley.

They were not soldiers. Soldiers moved with spacing and checked the high corners. These were taproom vermin—the sort of low-rent muscle that broke fingers for dockside moneylenders and took their courage from cheap gin. The leader was a broad, bull-necked pig in a grease-stiffened leather jerkin, brandishing an iron-shod oak cudgel. Flanking him were two leaner scoundrels with notched iron shortswords, their hoods pulled low against the sleet.

"Well, now," the bull-necked brute rumbled, his breath pluming in the cold like rotten cabbage. "The little clerk took a wrong turn. Lord Lysander said you might be stubborn about that leather book inside your coat."

I rested my thumbs loosely inside my belt, tilting my head. "Did the golden calf send you? Or did the red-haired sow tell you where to find me?"

The pig spat a yellow wad of phlegm onto the cobbles. "Lord Lysander gave us ten silver groats to crack your knees, take the book, and dump your carcass into the lower canal before the midnight tide. He said you were a pest. Said you needed to learn what happens when gutter-trash talks back to highborn blood."

The two men with shortswords fanned out, flanking the mouth of the chute. Their footwork was sloppy, their weight planted too heavily on their heels in the grease.

"Ten groats," I said softly, the words dripping with genuine amusement. "A pitiful price for three lives."

The leader barked a laugh. "You hear this bastard? He thinks he's a soldier because he sharpened arrows for the Dawn Vanguard! Skeg, gut him if he twitches. Joram, get the book."

The one named Joram lunged first.

It was a telegraphed, clumsy thrust aimed straight at my belly, intended to pin me against the limestone wall. The strike of a tavern bully accustomed to terrifying defenseless clerks.

I didn't back away. I didn't panic.

I waited until the cold iron point was four inches from my ribs. Then, with a subtle shift of my hips that required no more than three inches of movement, I pivoted inside the line of the blade. The cold steel hissed through empty air, scraping harmlessly against the bronze buckle of my belt.

Before Joram could register the miss, my left hand snapped out like an iron vise, clamping down on his weapon wrist and wrenching it violently downward. At the same instant, I drove the heel of my right boot straight into the lateral hinge of his kneecap.

There was a sharp, wet *crack*—the sound of green oak snapping in a gale.

Joram’s shriek died in his throat as his knee bent sideways at a grotesque right angle. As he collapsed forward, I hooked my right elbow behind his skull, driving his face downward with the full momentum of his fall onto the jagged rim of a rusted iron barrel-hoop half-buried in the mud.

The crunch of cartilage and teeth was followed by a wet, bubbling gurgle. Joram lay twitching in the muck, his face demolished.

His shortsword slipped from his nerveless fingers.

I didn't let it hit the stones. My right hand caught the leather-wrapped grip out of mid-air.

It was cheap frontier iron—heavy in the point, poorly balanced, and notched near the hilt. But in my grip, it felt like an old friend. The balance was crude compared to Dark Sister, but iron is iron when driven through meat.

The alley went dead silent, save for the patter of freezing rain and Joram's spastic, choking gasps in the mud.

The bull-necked brute stopped dead in his tracks, his cudgel lowering an inch. His piggish eyes bulged, darting from his companion's shattered leg to my face.

"What... what are you?" he stammered.

"A man who has killed better men than you for looking at him crooked," I said.

The third cutthroat, Skeg, lost his nerve. Panic made him reckless; with a wild, two-handed swing, he slashed at my neck, screaming to summon his courage.

Wasted motion. 

I took half a step backward. The whistling blade sliced through the rain an inch before my nose. As his momentum carried him forward, overbalancing his front foot, I stepped inside his reach.

I did not swing. Swings are for bards and gilded boys in tourney rings.

I drove the heavy iron point forward with the full weight of my shoulder behind it.

The blade punched through the boiled leather of Skeg’s collarbone, sliding through the carotid artery and biting into the cervical spine with a harsh, grating crunch. His scream was choked off by a mouthful of bright arterial blood. Hot crimson geysered over my right forearm, steaming in the freezing air.

I twisted the blade and jerked it free. Skeg crumpled like an empty grain sack into the black mud.

Now there was only the pig.

The iron-shod cudgel slipped from the brute's fingers, clattering loudly against the wet flagstones. He backed toward the alley entrance, his boots skidding in the slime, his face pale as lard.

"Wait... mercy! Lord Lysander paid us! We just took the coin—"

I walked toward him.

My boots made no sound on the wet stones. The yellow lantern light caught the dark spatters of blood across my jaw, reflecting in my violet-tinted eyes.

"Mercy is a currency for kings, little dog," I whispered, tapping the bloody flat of the blade against my thigh. "I am not in a forgiving mood tonight."

"I have gold!" the brute shrieked, fumbling frantically at his belt, ripping free a swollen leather purse with shaking fingers. "Take it! Take it all! Twenty silvers! Four gold royals! Take it and let me walk!"

I stopped an arm’s length away. 

I held out my left hand, taking the heavy purse from his trembling palms. I hefted it once. Honest imperial coin. Heavy, solid, and warm with the dead man's sweat.

"A fair beginning," I said.

The pig let out a ragged, weeping gasp of relief. "Can I... can I go?"

I looked at him. And I gave him the smile that had once made the lords of the Seven Kingdoms wake in cold sweats.

"No."

Before the scream could leave his lungs, my blade drew a clean, horizontal seam across his windpipe. 

His hands flew to his throat, trying in vain to hold back the red tide, his knees folding beneath him. I stood alone in the rain among the dead and the broken. 

Near the rusted barrel-hoop, Joram was still twitching in the mud, gurgling through his crushed jaw, his single unswollen eye dilated in petrified terror as he watched me.

I walked over and crouched beside him, resting the bloody flat of my iron blade against his shivering cheek.

"Listen to me, dog," I whispered, the words quiet and sharp as ice. "I am leaving you your breath for one reason. When the red-haired sow comes looking for her thugs, you tell her what you saw. You tell your gilded lord that Caspar Thorne died in the tavern—and the thing wearing his skin is coming to take everything he owns. Carry my words, or I will come back and finish the job."

Joram gave a frantic, sobbing nod against the mud, choking on his own blood.

I wiped the stolen blade clean on the pig’s buff coat, then slid it through my belt. I tucked the leather purse inside my doublet beside Caspar’s maps.

I looked down at my blood-spattered hands, then up into the black, weeping clouds hanging over the Cormorant Chasm. The icy wind howled through the limestone arches above, carrying the roar of the cataracts.

"Lysander of House Morn," I whispered into the dark. 

A low, terrible laugh rumbled in my chest, shaking this frail mortal ribcage.

"You sent three dogs to do a prince’s work. You should have come yourself, boy."
