# Chapter 3: The King of the Stepstones

### Part I: Briony

The rain off the Cormorant Chasm trickled down the back of my neck, cold as melted snow. 

I pulled my oilcloth cape tight around my shoulders, my heavy boots squelching through the black coal-slurry of the Lower Sump. Across my broad back hung my two-handed war-hammer—sixteen pounds of hardened iron that had cracked the skulls of marsh-trolls and fen-goblins. 

I was annoyed. I had spent half the night in the warm luxury of the High Crescent inn, drinking spiced brandy while Lysander stroked my arms and laughed with Morwenna and Althea about how we’d finally trimmed the fat from the Dawn Vanguard. Caspar’s face when Althea told him about the watch nights had been priceless—the scrawny little clerk looked like he’d swallowed an entire handful of rusted fishhooks.

Then dawn had come, and Lysander had frowned over his silver washbasin.

*The three lads from the taproom haven't returned to the stables, Briony darling,* Lysander had said, buttoning his silk undershirt, flashing that sweet, careless smile that made my stomach flutter. *I gave them ten groats to crack Caspar’s knees and bring back that ledger. They probably took the coin and went whoring in the cellar dives. Run down to Gallow-Gate Lane and fetch my book, will you? Give them a tap with your hammer if they’re sleeping it off.*

I’d laughed, kissed his cheek, and swaggered down the stairs. I expected to find Caspar curled in a gutter weeping over his broken shins, or the three drunk cutthroats passed out in a pile of wet hay.

Then I turned the corner into Gallow-Gate Lane.

The smell hit me three paces before I saw the dead end. It wasn't the usual Sump stink of rotten cabbage and coal smoke. It was the sharp, metallic tang of fresh hot blood, mixed with the sickening stench of voided bowels.

My hand dropped instinctively to the haft of my war-hammer. "Joram? Skeg?"

No answer. Only the dripping of rain from an iron lantern overhead.

I stepped through the mouth of the alley, and my breath jammed in my throat.

The alley was a butcher's stall.

The first man—Skeg—was slumped against the weeping limestone wall like a collapsed sack of meal. His eyes stared blankly into the gray sky. A single, terrifying thrust had punched through his boiled leather collarbone, severed his jugular, and shattered his spine. There was no sign of a struggle. One strike. Clean, precise, and fatal.

Three paces away lay the bull-necked leader. 

He was face-down in a pool of congealed, blackening blood. When I nudged his shoulder with my boot and rolled him over, my stomach heaved. His throat had been unzipped from ear to ear in a single, smooth horizontal stroke, clean as a master surgeon’s scalpel. His belt-pouch had been cut away.

*Two dead,* my mind raced, cold panic prickling across my scalp. *Two seasoned tavern bouncers, gutted in the dark.*

Then, from behind a heap of sodden sheep-fleece and broken barrel staves, came a sound.

A wet, rattling gasp.

I spun, raising my hammer. "Who's there?"

Something shifted in the mud. A hand reached out—fingers torn, nails broken against stone. 

It was Joram.

He was alive, but only by the cruelest margin. His left leg was twisted completely backward, the knee joint shattered and bulging sideways through his trousers at a sickening angle. But it was his face that made me choke on my bile. 

The entire front of his skull had been smashed down onto the rusted rim of an iron barrel hoop. His nose was flattened into bloody gristle, his upper jaw was crushed into fragments, and his mouth was a well of bubbling, dark froth. 

When he saw my boots, his single unswollen eye dilated with pure, hysterical madness. He dragged himself across the gravel, his bloody fingers clawing frantically at my leather greaves.

"Joram!" I dropped to one knee, grabbing his shoulder. "What happened? Who did this? Did the Black-Sash gang jump you?"

Joram shook his head with frantic, spastic jerks. A bloody bubble popped between his broken lips as he tried to speak, his throat producing a horrific, wet rasp:

"N-Not... not a gang..." he wheezed, blood spilling down his chin. "Th-Thorne... the clerk..."

I stared at him, my heart hammering against my ribs. "Caspar? You're out of your mind. Caspar can barely hold a paring knife!"

"No!" Joram’s voice rose into a terrified, muffled shriek, his fingers digging into my knee with frantic strength. "Not... not human! He didn't draw steel... he stepped inside my point... broke my leg with his heel... smashed my face like an anvil! He moved like... like smoke! Skeg swung... he didn't even look... drove iron through his throat!"

Joram began to weep, tears mixing with the clotted gore on his cheeks.

"His eyes... Briony... they weren't hazel! They were... smoke and purple fire... cold as the grave! He took the pig's purse... and then he smiled... God have mercy, he smiled while he cut his throat!"

The alley seemed to spin around me. 

I looked back at the two corpses. I looked at the angle of Skeg’s wound. That wasn't the panic of an ink-pusher defending his skin. That was the cold, terrifying economy of a master killer who didn't waste an ounce of breath or a grain of motion.

I shoved Joram away, scrambled to my feet, and ran out of Gallow-Gate Lane as fast as my heavy boots could carry me.

When I reached the High Crescent, panting and drenched in sweat, Lysander was already in his ceremonial gilded plate, admiring his reflection in the mirror while Morwenna adjusted his ermine cloak. Althea was laughing, testing the draw of her new bow.

"Lysander!" I burst through the door, my voice shaking so violently the room went quiet. "The men in Gallow-Gate... they're dead! Two of them slaughtered, and Joram's face is pulverized! Joram said it was Caspar! He said Caspar butchered them in seconds!"

Lysander turned from the mirror. 

He didn't look alarmed. He looked profoundly annoyed, his perfect golden brows knitting in theatrical distaste.

"Quiet down, Briony," Lysander snapped, brushing an invisible speck of dust from his gilded breastplate. "You sound like a hysterical washerwoman. Some cutthroat gang in the Sump rolled them for the ten groats, obviously. Caspar Thorne couldn't butcher a sickly hog, let alone three armed men. Joram is concussed and babbling."

"Lysander, I saw the wounds!" I insisted, stepping forward, my knuckles white on my hammer. "The thrust through Skeg's collarbone—it went clean through the spine! That wasn't a street thug!"

"I said enough," Lysander commanded, his voice turning cold and sharp. "The Duke’s procession begins in twenty minutes. I will not have my vanguard shaken by tavern rumors. Caspar is probably halfway to the border, shivering in some ditch. Now wipe the mud off your boots and fall in behind me."

I looked at Althea. She was smirking, shaking her head at me as if I had seen a ghost in the shadows. Morwenna rolled her eyes, turning back to her spell-books.

I looked down at my hands. They were still trembling.

I adjusted the strap of my war-hammer, but for the first time in my life, the sixteen pounds of hardened iron felt light. Flimsy. Like a child's toy.

And as we marched out toward the Great Guildhall, I couldn't stop looking into the dark alleyways, half-expecting to see a pair of cold, violet eyes watching us from the rain.

### Part II: Daemon

In my first life, when I took the Stepstones from the Triarchy with nothing but a handful of sellswords and second sons, I learned an eternal truth: empires are not built by virtuous men in white cloaks. They are forged by the desperate, the discarded, and the hungry.

With four stolen gold royals and twenty silver groats in my purse, I did not buy warm lodging or soft linen. I went straight down into the bowels of Blackweir.

The lower tier, known to the dock-rats as the Sump, was a suffocating maze of soot-stained timber tenements, charcoal kilns, and stagnant drainage ditches carved into the limestone cliff-face. It smelled of sulfur flux, raw sewage, and burning peat. Up above, the church bells of the Upper Crescent tolled for morning mass, their pious chimes muffled by the roar of the waterwheels.

Before the first bell had finished ringing, I stood in the sweltering heat of a cellar forge beneath the West Aqueduct.

Behind a massive block of scarred iron sat Master Korgan. 

He was as broad as an ox-cart, his thick arms mapped with white burn scars, his right eye clouded over with a milky cataract from a slag explosion years ago. The guild crest had been chiseled off his leather apron—cast out three winters past for splitting the skull of a magistrate's nephew who had demanded ceremonial plate forged from brittle cast iron.

"I don't hammer gilded trinkets for cathedral squires," Korgan growled, quenching a set of heavy tongs in a trough of stagnant water with a furious, spitting hiss. "If you want sun-crests and decorative brass, haul your scrawny carcass up the stairs."

I didn't argue. I drew a heavy gold royal from my purse and flicked it through the air.

It landed squarely on the anvil face, ringing with a clear, bell-like chime that cut through the hiss of the steam.

"Do I look like a cathedral squire to you, smith?" I asked.

Korgan squinted with his single good eye. He looked at my lean frame, the dark, stiffened blood-spatters across the cuffs of my borrowed doublet, and the cold, unblinking stillness of my eyes. In forty years of hammering iron, the old dwarf had seen plenty of killers, but he had never seen a boy who looked at him with the chilling majesty of an emperor reviewing a blacksmith.

"No," Korgan grunted, picking up the gold with soot-blackened fingers, biting the rim with yellowed teeth. "You look like a cutthroat who learned his trade in a slaughterhouse. What do you want, boy?"

"Black plate," I told him, my voice crisp and flat. "Crucible steel. No engravings, no fluting, no gold leaf. A high, overlapping gorget to protect the jugular, articulated lames at the elbows that won't catch on mail, and faulds cut high on the hip so I can pivot without dragging dead weight. And I want a bastard blade. Thirty-six inches of tempered carbon edge, double-fullered, balanced three fingers above the crossguard."

Korgan set the coin down, his heavy brow furrowing. "That's not adventurer gear. That's heavy assault harness. The kind sellsword captains wore when they broke the mountain redoubts forty years ago."

"Adventurers are children who chase glory until an arrow takes them in the throat," I said coldly. "I am building an army."

By midday, I had tracked down my second man: Orrin Pike.

I found him in the reeking cellar of a dive called *The Broken Oar*, slumped over a trestle table stained with dried vomit and sour beer. Once the chief scout of the border legions, Orrin had been tossed into the gutter after a war-horse crushed his left ankle and a stray crossbow bolt tore through his windpipe, leaving him with a voice like iron scraping across granite.

I sat down opposite him without an invitation. I didn't offer sympathy. I spread Caspar Thorne’s stolen maps across the sticky pine.

"You know the terrain of the Cinder-Gorge," I said, stating it not as a query, but as an undeniable fact.

Orrin squinted through bloodshot eyes, his scarred hand hovering over the vellum. His gaze darted across the meticulous elevation contours, the wind-drift vectors, and the notations detailing subterranean fissure depths.

"These are... legion-grade surveys," Orrin rasped, his scarred throat straining to produce each syllable. "Where did a brat like you steal these?"

"I made them," I replied smoothly, claiming the dead boy's brilliance as my own sovereign property. "The fools in the upper tier believe the Cinder-Gorge is an empty grave of poisonous steam and scorpions. They fear it. I intend to take it."

"You're out of your mind," Orrin muttered, reaching for his cracked clay mug. "The gorge is an oven. Chasm-scorpions breed in the shale. Their carapaces deflect arrows, and a drop of their amber bile turns a man's blood to curdled vinegar before he can scream three words."

I leaned forward, dropping a second gold royal directly into his puddle of spilled mash.

"Scorpions hunt by shale vibrations," I said, my voice dropping into that quiet, rhythmic register that had once commanded five thousand gold cloaks in the streets of King’s Landing. "If you know the shale beds, you know where to bottleneck them. I need a man who knows the wind, can handle an eighty-pound siege-crossbow, and has no squeamishness about burning vermin in their holes. You can rot in this cellar, Orrin, or you can help me break the nobility that left you to starve."

Orrin stared at the gold coin, then slowly raised his weathered face to meet mine. In that scarred, bitter ruin of a veteran, an old ember caught fire.

"I have my own quarrels," he rasped, snatching the royal off the wood. "Twelve-grain bodkins. They'll punch through three inches of chitin."

I looked at the crippled soldier—his shattered ankle, his ruined throat, the raw, venomous fury simmering behind his bloodshot eyes. Society had squeezed him dry in its border wars, then tossed him into this reeking tavern cellar like a split boot. A soft-bellied lord like Lysander would have laughed at his limp and chosen a straight-legged boy with groomed curls. 

But I felt that familiar, wicked hunger stirring in my gut. 

*Give me the broken, the discarded, the beaten,* I thought, watching his scarred knuckles whiten around the gold coin. *I will forge them into an iron spearhead, and I will watch them pin the highborn to their own marble halls.*

***

While I assembled my war council in the soot of the Sump, the horns of the Upper Crescent were blowing for a triumph that had not yet been won.

I climbed to the terrace overlooking the Grand Plaza of the High Guildhall. 

From the shadow of a stone arcade, I watched the spectacle unfold beneath the noon sun. Lysander stood upon the marble dais, his golden curls gleaming like polished brass, his broad shoulders draped in his immaculate scarlet cloak. At his side stood Althea, preening in a newly bought hunting tunic of green elven suede, her quiver bristling with silver-dyed arrows. Beside them stood Morwenna, smirking as she spun her crystal staff.

And behind them stood Briony.

I leaned against the stone pillar, my eyes narrowing with quiet amusement. 

The red-haired sow was not preening. Her thick arms were crossed tight across her chest, her knuckles white upon the shaft of her war-hammer. Her heavy face was pale, glistening with cold sweat, her eyes darting nervously toward every shadow and alley mouth bordering the square. She looked like a sentry who knew a wolf was stalking the perimeter.

*She found Gallow-Gate Lane,* I thought, a thin smirk curling my lips. *She saw what happens when dogs bite a prince.*

Beside her, Lysander drew his sword, holding it aloft as a shimmering pulse of golden radiant aether swept across the plaza, dazzling the cheering crowd.

"By the grace of the Mother of Light!" Lysander shouted, flashing his brilliant, empty smile. "Darkness shall be purged! No beast of the earth can stand before holy brilliance!"

The crowd roared in adoration. Althea beamed, pressing against his arm.

Yet as the applause rolled across the terrace, the Guild's Chief Quartermaster stepped forward with the requisition rolls.

"My Lord Lysander," the quartermaster said, his tone dry as chalk. "We require your foraging itineraries, water filtration ratios for the sulfur basin, and defensive marching formations. We noted your former quartermaster, Thorne, did not sign off on the requisitions."

Lysander waved a dismissive, manicured hand. "Thorne was a coward and an incompetent. The Dawn Vanguard has no need of baggage clerks."

The quartermaster peered over his spectacles. "Indeed. But who calculated your water supply? The streams in the upper crags are choked with copper-alum runoff. Without charcoal boiling, your hirelings will be vomiting blood within forty-eight hours."

Lysander scowled in annoyance. "Lady Morwenna weaves pure water from the aether. We do not need charcoal."

I watched Morwenna stiffen. She knew—even if her golden master did not—that weaving water in a sulfur basin burned through mana three times faster than in open air. But her vanity kept her silent.

Beside me in the arcade shadow, Orrin spat a dark plug of tobacco into the gutter.

"The damn fool is marching straight into a defile with thirty green hirelings and four baggage carts," Orrin rasped, shaking his head. "He doesn't even know the behemoth's feeding hours."

I glanced once more at Briony. She had just looked down toward the lower street, shivering beneath her oilcloth cape, jumping at the clatter of an iron wagon wheel.

The seed of dread had been planted. And terror, once seeded, grows faster than any weed.

"A king who does not know the weight of his own baggage," I whispered, turning away from the sunlight, "is merely a corpse waiting for a shovel. Let them march, Orrin. We have an empire to dig out of the ash."
