# Chapter 9: The Banishment of the Vain

The rain returned to Blackweir by late afternoon, a steady, gray deluge that washed the blood and vomit from the yellow sand of the arena.

In the Great Council Chamber of the Duke’s Keep, the air was warm with the scent of beeswax, cedar-smoke, and fear. 

Duke Aldous sat behind his long table of carved bog-oak, flanked by the Chancellor of the Treasury and the Guild Master of Armorers. They did not look at me as if I were a clerk. They looked at me as men look at a loaded siege-catapult with a slipping ratchet.

I had stripped off my black plate, standing before their hearth in a high-collared tunic of dark charcoal wool, my hands resting loosely behind my back. The ink-stains of Caspar Thorne were gone from my fingers, replaced by the calloused palms of an anvil-striker and a swordsman.

On the table lay the new Imperial Charter, its crimson wax seal still soft.

"By this decree," the Duke spoke, his voice measured, polite, and remarkably cautious, "the Cinder-Gorge is severed from the municipal jurisdiction of Blackweir. It is hereby designated a Sovereign March. You, Lord Caspar Thorne, are recognized as Lord High Warden, with sole rights to all mineral deposits, thermal flues, and military fortifications within its boundaries."

The Chancellor cleared his throat nervously, adjusting his spectacles. "In exchange, Lord Thorne... the city requests first right of purchase on all star-iron billets, at standard guild rates."

I let out a slow, quiet breath, letting my smirk chill the room.

"You will buy my iron at five percent above guild rates," I said, my voice low and flat. "And you will deliver forty wagonloads of grain, dry barley, and salt beef to my gatehouse every full moon, free of transit tolls and municipal excise."

The Guild Master opened his mouth to object, but his eyes caught my violet gaze. The words died in his throat. He remembered the sound of Lysander’s jaw splintering against my gauntlet.

"It... it shall be so recorded," the Duke conceded, hastily signing his name with a trembling quill.

***

When I walked out beneath the portico of the High Keep, the rain was coming down in sheets. 

Orrin Pike waited at the base of the marble steps with an escort of eight guardsmen in black plate, their heavy war-horses snorting white plumes of steam into the chill autumn air.

At the foot of the stairs, huddled beneath the dripping eaves of the herald’s lodge, waited three shadows.

They were soaked to the skin.

Briony leaned heavily against a stone pillar, her left arm strapped to her ribs in graying, sodden linen, her broad face pale and hollowed by fever. Beside her stood Morwenna, shivering violently in a wet wool cloak, the right side of her face exposed to reveal the horrific burns and the clouded, dead socket where her focus crystal had burst.

And in the center knelt Althea Reed.

She was on her knees in the cold slurry of street-mud and horse-filth, her head bowed until her forehead nearly touched the wet cobblestones. Her long brown hair clung to her cheeks like rotting seaweed. In her outstretched, shaking palms, she held the single battered copper piece I had dropped in the dust of my hall.

As my boots reached the bottom step, Althea raised her head.

Her eyes were bloodshot, swollen, and filled with a despair so absolute it bordered on madness.

"Caspar..." her voice was a ragged croak that broke against the patter of the rain. "Caspar, please."

I stopped two paces from her. 

Orrin’s hand drifted toward his arbalest, but I raised two fingers, halting my men.

"What do you want, Althea?" I asked, my voice calm, flat, and colder than the wet stone beneath her knees.

"They took everything," she sobbed, tears cutting clean tracks through the mud on her cheeks. "The bailiffs seized our rooms. Factor Osric’s men are hunting us. Lysander..." 

She choked on the name, shuddering with violent, nauseated revulsion. 

"Lysander was stripped of his crest. He is down in the gutter by the Fish Gate, begging for crusts with a shattered jaw. He cursed us, Caspar! He told the bailiffs we were his property to be sold into the southern brothels to pay his debts! The Guild revoked my license! I have nowhere to go. No family. No home."

She crawled forward an inch, holding up the tarnished copper coin as if it were a holy relic.

"Kill me if you hate me," she wept, her forehead pressing against the toe of my riding boot. "Drive your sword through my throat. It would be a mercy! But if there is any piece of the boy who held me by the willow pond left in your heart... don't leave me to the streets, Caspar. I will be your servant. I will sweep your hearth, I will scrub your latrines, I will take whatever blows you give me. Just don't cast me out into the dark."

Behind her, Briony fell to one knee, her voice choked with rough, humiliated tears. "Lord Thorne... I was a fool. I mocked your work. But I have two good legs and a right arm. Let me haul ore in your deep tunnels. Give me dry bread and a pallet, and I will swing a pick until I drop."

Morwenna bowed her head, her voice trembling. "I can still brew reagents, my lord. My mana is cracked, but I know the chemical ratios of vitriol and sulfur. I ask only for a corner of your laboratory and protection from the debt-slavers."

I looked down at the three of them.

There was no petty rage in me. No vindictive joy. A prince does not gloat over the misery of worms; he merely observes the natural order of the world.

I looked at Briony and Morwenna first.

They were broken, maimed, stripped of every shred of their adventurer arrogance. Briony with her pulverized arm in dirty splints; Morwenna with her burned face and burst crystal. They had hit the absolute bottom of the pit—the cold mud where the highborn kick their hounds when their legs go lame. 

A petty tyrant would have had them strangled in the gutter or laughed as Factor Osric’s slavers threw them into iron collars. But looking down at their ruined, mud-streaked faces, a dark, familiar chord struck inside my chest. 

I had always possessed a perverse weakness for the broken things of the world. There was no joy in kicking a dog that was already drowning in a ditch; the real, exquisite thrill was dragging the dog from the water, feeding it raw meat, and watching it tear out the throat of the master who had tossed it in.

"You were sellswords," I said, my voice cutting through the drumming rain. "You followed gold and swagger because you were too stupid to recognize true power. But you have hands, and you understand what it means to bleed."

I glanced sideways at Orrin. 

"Take them to the outer sorting works at the Cinder-Gorge," I commanded. "Briony hauls slag with the third gang. Morwenna works the sulfur distillers under Tobias’s eye. If you work sixteen hours a day without complaint, you get two bowls of pea-soup, a dry blanket, and iron beneath your soles. Work until your marrow turns to basalt. And when the capital lords ride south to beg for my star-iron, it will be your boots they look at from the ditch."

Briony let out a ragged gasp of relief, weeping openly as she pressed her forehead to the wet cobbles. "Thank you, my lord... thank you..."

Morwenna bowed low, clutching her cowl, whispering broken gratitude into the mud.

Then my gaze settled upon Althea.

She was looking up at me, her lips parted in desperate, agonizing hope, waiting for the words that would bring her back to my side.

I leaned down slowly.

With two fingers, I reached out and plucked the copper coin from her open palm. I held it before my eyes, looking at the tarnished metal, then looked down into her hazel eyes—the eyes that had once belonged to the girl of Briar-by-the-Fen.

"You thought you were clever, Althea," I murmured softly. "You thought you could measure a man's worth by the shine of his cloak and the promises on his tongue."

"Caspar... please..."

"You laughed in that tavern when you told me you were rutting in his pavilion," I said, my voice dropping to a near-whisper that felt like an iron spike driving into her heart. "You wanted the capital. You wanted silk. You wanted the golden hero."

I closed my fingers around the copper coin, then flicked my wrist.

The coin flew through the gray rain, clattered across the wet cobblestones, and rolled straight through the iron grating of a street drain.

A hollow, echoing *plink* drifted up from the dark sewer below.

"There is your capital, girl," I said.

Althea stared at the drain. Her mouth hung open, but no sound came out. Her eyes dilated in absolute, soul-destroying horror.

"You're... leaving me?" she choked out, her voice barely a breath. "After everything? We were supposed to build a hall..."

"Caspar Thorne is dead," I said, turning my back on her as I took the reins of my war-horse from Orrin. "And Prince Daemon Targaryen has no need of treacherous whores."

I swung into the saddle with fluid, effortless grace. 

"Ride," I ordered.

The hooves of eight heavy war-horses struck the wet cobblestones with a sound like rolling thunder, throwing plumes of gray mud behind us as we galloped out through the south gates of Blackweir.

Behind us, in the freezing rain, Althea remained on her knees in the mud.

She reached her trembling hands toward the sewer grating, clawing frantically at the cold iron bars, but the coin was gone, swept away into the black, roaring filth of the city.

The cold wind blew off the Cormorant Chasm, carrying the distant scent of burning sulfur from the south. 

She was alone. And she would be alone until she died.
