# Chapter 4: The Rot in the Gold

The clerks at the High Registry of Blackweir laughed in my face when I slammed my thirty silver groats onto their oak counter.

To their ledger-dulled minds, the **Cinder-Gorge** was nothing more than an open ulcer in the earth—a three-mile rift of boiling mud, poisonous brimstone vents, and unnavigable basalt cliffs sixty leagues south of the city gates. The Guild had officially designated it a dead zone after two survey teams died of scorpion stings thirty years ago.

"Perpetual freehold salvage title?" the chief registrar chuckled, dipping his iron stylus into blue wax. "You are paying thirty groats to buy an open grave, boy. Sign the waiver releasing the municipal treasury from burial expenses, and the ditch is yours."

I signed Caspar Thorne’s name with a flourish, took the lead-sealed vellum charter, and walked out into the cold morning air.

Thirty groats for a kingdom. 

By noon, I stood on the southern precipice of the rift.

Beside me, Master Korgan leaned heavily on a mule laden with crowbars, picks, and three oak barrels of Tobias’s refined mineral pitch. At my right hand knelt Orrin Pike, chewing a bitter leaf of weed, peering through the brass sight of his heavy siege-crossbow into the sulfurous haze below.

The air rising from the depths was choking—thick with the stench of rotten eggs, burning vitriol, and pulverized shale. Korgan coughed into his leather sleeve, his one good eye watering from the fumes.

I did not cough.

I closed my eyes and drew the scalding vapor deep into my chest. To these common frontier men, it was the breath of a demon's sewer. But to my soul, it was wine. It was the volcanic heat of my ancestral sea-citadel, the sulfurous wind that had roared through the burning craters of the Old Freehold before the world cracked in two. 

In my borrowed mortal veins, the blood stirred. A prickling, feverish heat ran along my collarbones, tightening the sinews of my arms. For the first time since my soul was wrenched from the waters of the Gods Eye, I felt entirely at home.

"They see an open grave," I murmured, opening my eyes to scan the black basalt tiers below. "I see an unbreachable redoubt. Look at those sheer vertical faces, Korgan. No siege tower could ever reach the lip. No cavalry could cross the razor-glass scree. And down there..."

I pointed with the tip of an iron crowbar toward a glassy, iridescent fracture cutting through the lowest basalt wall.

Korgan squinted, leaning dangerously over the chasm lip. His scarred jaw went slack.

"By the anvil..." the old dwarf breathed, his single eye widening. "That's not stone. That's... star-iron. Deep-earth meteoric slag that never rusts. The imperial legions mined it two centuries ago before the rift collapsed!"

"Enough to forge plate for five hundred men," I said, a savage grin touching my lips. "And weapons that can shear through guild-steel like wet cloth."

"The lower terrace is crawling with scorpions," Orrin rasped, pointing toward the dark fissures. Even from here, the dry clicking of chitinous pincers and the scrape of barbed tails echoed against the basalt like iron tongs. "At least forty of them. A direct assault will get us butchered. Their shells will deflect crossbow quarrels."

"We do not assault," I said coldly. "We hunt."

Just then, a light-footed boy in a torn tunic scrambled up the goat-path behind us, panting hard, clutching a grease-stained strip of linen. It was Bran, one of Orrin’s dock-rat runners from the Sump.

"From the northern defiles, Marshal Orrin," the boy wheezed, handing the linen over. "The Dawn Vanguard."

Orrin unrolled the scrawl, his scarred brow furrowing as he read. A low, gravelly chuckle rumbled in his ruined windpipe.

"Your former company is bleeding, my lord," Orrin rasped, handing the dispatch to me. "Four days into the Wyrm-Tooth Crags. The freezing sleet hit them on the second night. Half their salted pork spoiled because nobody checked the brine casks—maggots in the bread, flux in the ranks. Twelve hirelings are down with gut-rot."

I scanned the report with cool, clinical satisfaction. 

"And the little witch?" I asked.

"Morwenna's mana channels are burned out," Orrin said. "She's been trying to purify runoff water laced with copper-slag. She's raving, snapping at her own retainers. And Lord Lysander..." Orrin spat into the chasm. "...the golden lord is curled up in his pavilion with Lady Cecily, feeding her dried figs while he threatens to dock the hirelings' pay for complaining."

"And Althea?" 

Orrin gave me a sideways, calculating look. "The runner says she’s sleeping in the baggage cart, drenched to the skin. Lysander called her a swineherd’s daughter in front of the merchant’s girl when she tried to warn him about the beast's feeding hours. She's restringing her bow with frostbitten fingers, crying in the mud."

I crumpled the dispatch in my fist and tossed it over the edge.

It fluttered down through the yellow steam, catching fire on a thermal updraft and turning to black ash before it hit the second tier.

I felt not an ounce of pity. 

Caspar Thorne had spent three years shielding that girl from the bitter wind, carrying her water, checking her straps, bleeding so she could sleep dry under canvas. She had looked into his devoted face and told him he made her sick. She had traded his quiet, iron devotion for a pretty face in a scarlet cloak, dreaming of silk and capital ballrooms.

Now she was discovering what a gilded toy does when the rain starts falling.

"Let them rot in the sleet," I said, turning my back on the north. 

I looked down into the black, boiling abyss of the Cinder-Gorge, where forty giant scorpions waited in the dark.

"Korgan. Prepare the pitch casks. Orrin, set the arbalest tripods on the second terrace. Tonight, we purge the vermin and light the fires of our new home."
