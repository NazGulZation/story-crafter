# Chapter 5: Fire and Crucible

The subterranean gallery of the Cinder-Gorge did not echo with prayers or hymns. It echoed with the roar of burning pitch and the frantic, choking hisses of dying vermin.

For three days, I waged war the only way I knew how: with ruthless, calculated brutality. 

I did not send my twelve hired Sump-laborers into the dark fissures to trade blows with monsters. I used the terrain as a weapon. Under my direction, we poured Tobias’s thick, volatile mineral pitch down the natural chimney cracks, then shattered clay jars of sulfur-brine across the vents before firing iron quarrels wrapped in burning tow into the funnels.

The reaction was catastrophic. 

Thick, blinding black smoke billowed through the subterranean labyrinth, stripping the oxygen from the honeycombed nesting chambers. Blinded and suffocating, the chasm-scorpions were driven from their nests into the narrow killing defile of the central caldera.

There, Orrin’s heavy arbalests did their work, punching heavy steel bodkins through the softer ventral plates of their bellies as they scrambled over one another in a panic of clacking claws.

Then the earth beneath our feet shivered.

With a deafening roar of collapsing basalt, the brood-matron erupted from the lowest magma vent. She was a nightmare of armored bulk—the size of a siege wagon, her segmented chitin as black and polished as wet anthracite. Her barbed caudal stinger arched over her back, dripping streams of caustic, paralytic amber that hissed and melted the obsidian shale beneath her eight splayed legs.

My laborers dropped their billhooks and fled toward the upper ramp, screaming in terror.

I did not run.

I stood upon a basalt outcrop six feet above the caldera floor, a forty-pound oak cask of ignited pitch balanced on the rock lip before me. As the matron lunged toward my ledge, her cluster of blind sensory nodes vibrating violently against the heat of my body, I kicked the cask straight into her gaping mandibles.

The barrel shattered on impact. 

A pillar of sulfurous orange fire exploded inside her mouth, blowing apart her lower feeding claws in a spray of boiling greenish ichor. Shrieking with a sound like grinding millstones, the beast thrashed in blind agony, her massive spiked tail lashing out to smash the cliffside, showering me with razor-sharp obsidian shards.

I didn't hesitate. I jumped.

In my youth, I had dived through the clouds from the back of a winged terror of fire. A ten-foot drop into a pit of boiling steam was nothing.

I landed squarely across the creature’s armored neck plates. 

The heat was blistering, the stench of burning chitin nearly choking me, but my boots found purchase in the joints of her carapace. With both hands, I drove a six-foot iron-mining pry-bar down into the nerve cluster behind her skull.

The steel bit deep, crunching through bone and fluid. 

I threw my entire mortal weight onto the lever, planting my heels and wrenching the bar with all the ferocity of my Valyrian soul. 

The matron went rigid. Her legs spasmed in wild, erratic arcs, clawing deep grooves into the volcanic rock, before her massive bulk shuddered and collapsed onto the shale floor with a final, hissing expulsion of black vapor.

I pulled myself up, chest heaving, wiping a smear of reeking green bile from my cheek.

Below me, standing in the doorway of the lower tunnel, Master Korgan and the surviving laborers stared in paralyzed, breathless awe. I was drenched in sweat, soot, and monster blood, holding a bent crowbar atop the carcass of a beast that had slaughtered whole platoons of guild-knights.

I looked down at them, my violet-tinged eyes burning through the drifting steam.

"Clear the floor," I commanded, my voice flat, calm, and absolute. "Drag the carcasses to the sulfur pits. Tobias, harvest every drop of venom into clay amphoras. Master Korgan... light the great furnace."

***

The caldera became our crucible. 

By the end of the second week, Korgan had tapped the main geothermal vent with iron flue-pipes, creating a smelting forge that burned hotter than any charcoal hearth in the kingdom.

I worked at the anvil beside him, stripped to the waist, my skin black with ash and glistening with sweat. The lean, soft frame of Caspar Thorne was hardening day by day; the muscles across my shoulders tightened, corded like hemp rope, answering the demands of my soul. 

*CLANG. HISS. CLANG.*

I held the glowing billet of meteoric star-iron with heavy iron tongs, while Korgan brought the fourteen-pound striking hammer down with bone-jarring force.

"Fold it again!" I roared over the howling drafts of the volcanic chimney. "Seven times we have beaten out the dross! Fold it once more and draw the fuller!"

"The iron will turn brittle, boy!" Korgan yelled back, his single eye watering from the blinding white-hot glare. "Star-metal isn't bog-iron! One misstrike and the whole ingot shatters like crystal!"

"It will bend if you strike where the sulfur runs true!" I snarled, turning the billet on the anvil face. "Strike, old man! Strike for the blood of it!"

The hammer fell. 

Sparks of brilliant violet and white exploded across my bare chest, singing the flesh. I didn't even flinch. I felt the pulse of the metal through the tongs, guiding the shape with instincts honed in a lifetime of war. In my mind, I remembered Dark Sister—the rippled, smoky perfection of Valyrian steel that had tasted the blood of kings and usurpers. I could not resurrect the Freehold here, but out of this black meteorite, I would forge an answer to this world’s gilded pretenses.

When the blade was drawn and ground, we quenched it not in river water, but in a mixture of rendered scorpion tallow, vitriol pitch, and boiling sulfur-brine.

The quench-trough erupted in a geyser of black, oily smoke that hissed with the wrath of a poisoned sea.

When the smoke cleared, I lifted the steel into the firelight.

It was a bastard blade—thirty-eight inches of double-edged, hand-and-a-half lethal perfection. It did not reflect light like the polished parade toys of the High Crescent. The steel was a deep, oily, smoke-black, woven through with veins of dull, brooding crimson that seemed to drink the torchlight like blood spilled upon velvet.

I took the cord-wrapped hilt in my right hand. 

I cut the air with a single, sweeping flourish. The blade made no singing hum; it slipped through the wind with a soft, predatory *shhhk*, balanced three fingers above the crossguard.

Korgan stood staring, his jaw slack, the tongs hanging limp in his grip. 

"By the seven hammers..." he whispered, his rough voice hushed with reverence. "I've hammered steel for fifty winters, lad. I've never seen metal like that. It looks... hungry."

I ran my thumb along the razor edge. 

A bead of bright red blood welled from my skin, dropping onto the crimson vein of the blade. The blood did not run; it was absorbed into the microscopic grain of the dark metal, vanishing into the steel with a faint, resonant warmth.

"Its name is *Bloodthorn*," I murmured. 

I slid the blade into a sheath of cured scorpion-hide, feeling its weight settle against my hip like a familiar crown.

I did not stop with a single sword.

In the weeks that followed, I took the thirty Sump-laborers who had followed me down into the ash—runaway serfs, debtors marked for the quarry-chains, and starvelings who had spent their winters coughing black mud into the canals. I did not offer them pious sermons. I fed them salt beef, hot pea-stew, and clean water boiled over volcanic flues. I drilled them in the narrow basalt defiles until their shivering limbs learned the brutal economy of the arbalest and the billhook. Korgan fitted each man with a breastplate of oil-quenched star-iron, turning ragged wretches into an unyielding wall of black steel.

Standing upon the high gallery, I watched a former sewer-sweeper named Kellan rack his eighty-pound arbalest with cold, rhythmic precision, his scarred chin held high, his eyes reflecting the furnace glow.

*Look at you,* I thought, feeling that ancient, wicked thrill singing in my blood. *Yesterday, a highborn lord like Lysander would have had the city bailiffs whip you for dirtying his courser's hooves. Tomorrow, you will hold his throat under your boot.*

I had always loved this morbid alchemy: taking the vermin of the world, teaching them the religion of steel, and setting them loose upon the masters who had ground them into the mud.

By the end of the month, the whispers had reached every tavern in Blackweir. Men spoke of the dead clerk who had conquered the volcanic ditch, who had opened the star-iron mines and was paying three silver groats a day in honest coin to any veteran, smith, or deserter willing to swear fealty.

The Dawn Vanguard was marching to their doom in the north. 

And in the south, the Rogue Prince was ready to reap the harvest.
