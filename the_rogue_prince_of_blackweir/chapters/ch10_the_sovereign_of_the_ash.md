# Chapter 10: The Sovereign of the Ash

Six months after the duel in the amphitheater, the southern horizon of the March of Blackweir never slept.

Day and night, three massive bastions of black basalt—the Citadel of the Red Kilns—dominated the jagged lip of the Cinder-Gorge. Where once only poisonous vents and wild scorpions hissed in the dark, an industrial fortress of iron and steam had taken root. Massive thermal flues vented orange fire into the midnight sky, turning the low-hanging autumn clouds into a canopy of bruised violet and burning ember.

Along the battlements, four hundred heavy infantrymen marched in disciplined, silent shifts. They wore articulated plates of oil-quenched star-iron, immune to rust, armed with double-wound arbalests and dark-tempered shortswords. They did not answer to the Duke, nor did they kneel to the priests of the Luminescent Mother. Their tabards bore my mark—a single crimson drop upon an iron anvil—and their fealty belonged entirely to their High Warden.

Down in the lower industrial tiers, the sound of five hundred hammers was a continuous, rhythmic heartbeat.

Master Korgan, his milky eye now shielded by a tinted glass lens forged in the volcanic fires, bellowed orders to teams of smelters. Among the ore-sorters in the lower yard, Briony swung a heavy sledgehammer with her right arm, her scarred jaw set, her breath steady. Beside the bubbling distillation vats, Morwenna stirred vats of vitriol pitch with calloused hands, her single good eye focused and calm, grateful for the dry roof and the kettle of hot broth that arrived at sunset.

They were no longer adventurers chasing glory; they were the iron foundation of an empire being hammered into existence.

From the city of Blackweir to the north, trade caravans arrived daily, paying tribute in grain, timber, and livestock in exchange for wagonloads of star-iron ingots that were quickly becoming the most coveted currency in the realm.

News had drifted south with the merchants:

Lysander of House Morn was no longer named in polite society. His family in the capital had formally expunged his name from their ancestral rolls, erasing his bloodline to wash away the shame of his cowardice. He lived now in the damp squalor beneath the South Aqueduct—a toothless, half-blind beggar with a shattered jaw that leaked drool down his filthy neck, shouting mad prophecies about holy light to barge-hands who threw rotten turnip heads at his feet.

And in the lower tanneries of Blackweir, along the freezing waters of the canal, a young woman with chapped, cracked hands spent her days scraping salt and fat from rotting sheep-hides. Her back was bent. Her skin was bleached and scarred by lye. Every evening, when the cathedral bells tolled for vespers, Althea Reed would climb the rickety steps of the drying terrace and look south.

She would watch the orange fires of the Cinder-Gorge painting the horizon in streaks of crimson and gold.

She would remember the boy who had bought her first bow with his grandfather’s ring. She would remember the cruel laughter she had thrown in his face across that greasy tavern table. And she would weep until her voice gave out, shivering alone in the bitter canal fog.

***

High atop the southern parapet of the Citadel, I stood alone.

The wind off the volcanic chasm was hot, smelling of brimstone, scorched ore, and ozone. It whipped through my dark hair, fluttering the hem of my black wool mantle.

I rested both hands upon the rough basalt crenels of the battlements, looking out over the frontier.

Caspar Thorne’s memories were quiet now—a distant, peaceful murmur sleeping in the deep foundations of my soul. The boy’s broken heart had healed, forged over in volcanic fire and quenched in the blood of his enemies until it was as hard as the star-iron beneath my boots.

I reached down to the scabbard at my hip.

I drew *Bloodthorn*.

The dark blade cleared the boiled hide with a soft, predatory rasp that resonated with the heat of the subterranean flues below. Along the smoke-patterned steel, veins of dark crimson pulsed faintly in the orange glare of the sky.

I raised the blade, turning it slowly before my eyes.

In my past life, I had died beneath the roaring skies of the Gods Eye, plunging into the black water with Dark Sister buried in my nephew's eye, knowing my ancestral house was tearing itself apart in civil war. I had lost my throne. I had lost my fell winged mount whose roar had once made kingdoms tremble across the Narrow Sea.

Yet here, in a world that worshiped porcelain faces and hollow idols, I had built something far more enduring.

I had found ash. I had found iron. And I had found men who understood the language of steel.

Orrin Pike’s heavy footsteps sounded on the stone stairs behind me. The scarred scout stopped three paces away, holding out a sealed cylinder of lead.

"My lord," Orrin rasped. "A dispatch from our northern scouts. The King’s Grand Army has crossed the River Silt. Three thousand royal knights and heavy cavalry, sent to seize the star-iron mines in the Crown’s name. They demand our unconditional surrender within three days."

I did not turn around.

I looked down from the parapet into the central courtyard. Four hundred men—runaway serfs, scarred miners, debtors, and discarded cripples—stood in tight, disciplined ranks. They wore matte star-iron plate, testing their arbalest cords and adjusting their heavy billhooks in grim, beautiful silence. Six months ago, every one of them had been starving in a ditch, spat upon by the very knights now riding south. 

Now they looked up at my banner with eyes of cold, unyielding iron.

Three thousand gilded highborn sons riding under silk standards, expecting to crush a rabble of cowering peasants. And waiting for them were four hundred men who had been forged in volcanic flame, hungry to drag those pretty lords from their gilded saddles.

I held *Bloodthorn* horizontally against the rising wind, watching the sparks from the furnace stacks dance along its dark edge.

A slow, terrible smile curved my lips—the smile that had once terrified kings, councils, and great lords across the world.

I felt that ancient, wicked thrill singing through every fiber of my soul. There was no sweeter spectacle in this life, or my last, than watching the beaten dregs of the earth rise up to ruthlessly trample the masters who had ground them into the mud.

"Three thousand?" I whispered, my violet-tinged eyes reflecting the fires of the caldera. "Good. The furnaces are hungry."

I sheathed the blade with a single, sharp *clack*.

"Tell the captains to sound the muster, Orrin," I commanded, my voice ringing like iron across the battlements. "Let the kings come to the Cinder-Gorge. We will teach them what fire and blood truly mean."
