# Chapter 15: Labor Reallocation (The War Engine)

An individual’s worth is measured by productive throughput.

In romantic lore, women of power are celestial muses: ethereal priestesses bestowing blessings upon noble knights, exotic sorceresses weaving destructive spells from ivory towers, or mysterious forest stalkers whispering to ravens. Their existence is divorced from labor, their survival sustained by the unmentioned sweat of unheralded peasants.

Under the Directorate, every biological capability is directed toward the survival of the collective.

Four weeks following the trial in the Arena of the Black Sun, I conducted the monthly inspection of the Central Munitions Complex in Sector Epsilon.

My first stop was Plant No. 4—the Synthetic Fuel and Propellant Refinery.

The hall was filled with the deafening hiss of high-pressure steam pipes, the sharp chemical bite of toluene vapors, and the intense, baking heat of the catalytic cracking retorts. Towering distillation columns of riveted steel rose thirty feet to the rafters, transforming crude coal tar into eighty-seven-octane synthetic aviation fuel (*Flugbenzin*).

Standing atop the central cracking platform, encased in heavy asbestos overalls, safety goggles, and thick leather gauntlets, was Isolde of the Amber Spire.

Her long raven hair was bound tightly beneath an oilcloth cap. She held a calibrated brass pyrometer in her left hand, monitoring the mercury column with intense, unblinking concentration.

“Regulate retort three!” the shift foreman shouted through a brass speaking tube. “Temperature dropping below three hundred and eighty! Catalytic cracking threshold destabilizing!”

Isolde did not panic. She did not chant high-flown incantations to ancient gods.

She extended her right gauntlet toward the ceramic thermal port of Retort No. 3. A controlled, steady cone of brilliant violet elemental flame erupted from her palm, directed with surgical precision into the combustion pre-chamber.

She watched the mercury column rise: three hundred and eighty-five... three hundred and ninety... four hundred.

She throttled her flame back with an expert twitch of her wrist, maintaining the thermal balance with chronometric discipline.

“Temperature stable at four hundred Celsius!” Isolde called out, wiping sweat from her brow with the back of her asbestos sleeve. “Feed the next batch of coal tar!”

I stepped onto the platform.

Isolde turned, saw my charcoal uniform, and immediately snapped to rigid attention, pressing her gloved fist against her chest.

“Supreme Commander!” she barked.

“Report your operational metrics, Specialist Isolde,” I commanded.

“Shift Charlie has completed two thousand liters of refined aviation spirit in the past eight hours, sir,” Isolde reported crisply. “We maintained the catalytic retorts within two degrees of optimal tolerance. Our cordite drying beds in Sector Gamma have reached zero point four percent moisture content.”

I looked at the clean pyrometer in her hand and the steady, focused calm in her eyes.

“Your precision has improved,” I observed. “You have ceased complaining regarding your academic status at the Royal Academy.”

A faint, tired smile touched her soot-smudged lips.

“An archmage at the Royal Academy spent thirty years debating the poetic soul of fire while peasants froze in winter, Supreme Commander,” Isolde replied. “Here, my fire refines fuel that powers an air wing defending an entire basin. The work has purpose.”

“Continue your shift,” I said. “Director Weidmann will credit your account with five additional *Arbeitschein* for zero-defect thermal control.”

I descended the gantry and crossed the paved courtyard to the Optical & Ballistics Bureau in Sector Gamma.

Here, the atmosphere was quiet, characterized by the gentle hum of grinding wheels and the scent of optical rouge and pure alcohol.

At a wooden workbench beneath large skylights sat Brigit the Wood-Stalker. She wore a clean wool smock, a magnifying loupe attached to her left eye, and handled delicate quartz lenses with calloused, steady fingers.

Mounted in front of her was the prototype of the *Revi-Pattern 1* reflector bombsight, designed for the *Black-Wing* dive-bombers.

Brigit adjusted a miniature brass screw, aligning an illuminated crosshair reticle against a calibrated collimator grid across the room.

“Sergeant Major Kester,” she murmured without looking up. “The elevation cam on batch forty-two has a two-minute angular deviation at eight hundred meters. I have marked it for re-milling.”

“Approved, Specialist Brigit,” Kester replied, making a note in his inspection pad.

I stepped to her bench. Brigit removed her magnifying loupe and stood up, her spine straight, her grey eyes calm and clear.

“Specialist Brigit,” I said.

“Supreme Commander,” she acknowledged with a respectful nod.

“The reports from the tank proving ground indicate your periscope prisms have doubled the combat engagement range of the *Sturmwagen* crews,” I noted. “Your spatial judgment has eliminated targeting errors.”

“Glass does what an eye asks of it, sir,” Brigit answered smoothly. “In the forest, I judged the drop of an arrow by the bend of the grass. In this shop, I measure it in thousandths of an inch with brass calipers. The calipers do not lie.”

“You have been promoted to Senior Optical Inspector,” I informed her. “Your quarters are transferred to the Technical Staff Quarters in Sector Alpha. You receive full officer rations and fifty *Arbeitschein* weekly.”

“Understood,” Brigit replied simply, her face showing quiet, genuine satisfaction. “The Directorate has my gratitude.”

I left the optical shop and entered the final facility on the inspection schedule: the Munitions Factory Trauma and Chemical Burn Ward.

The air inside the hospital ward smelled strongly of carbolic wash, zinc ointment, and boiled linen. Two dozen beds lined the whitewashed walls, occupied by lathe operators with crushed fingers and chemical workers recovering from minor acid burns.

Moving between the beds with a tin basin of warm saline wash and a roll of clean gauze was Clara of the Weeping Spring.

Her white silk robes were long gone, replaced by a stiff, unbleached cotton nurse’s apron, coarse grey wool sleeves, and heavy rubber gloves. Her hands, once soft and scented with lavender, were red and scrubbed raw from strong carbolic soap.

She was bandaging the forearm of an elderly machinist whose skin had been splashed with nitric acid.

“Hold still, Uncle Hanns,” Clara murmured softly, her voice trembling slightly. “This will sting for a moment.”

She placed her gloved palm over the raw, blistered skin. A soft, pale golden light flickered from her fingers. The jagged chemical burns began to knit together, the blistering skin cooling and smoothing as her cellular mitosis magic accelerated biological regeneration.

The old machinist let out a sigh of relief. “Bless you, sister. My arm feels whole again.”

“Rest for two hours, Hanns,” Clara said softly. “Drink the salt broth. You’ll be back at your lathe by tomorrow.”

She turned, carrying the basin to the sink, and froze.

I stood in the doorway, my hands clasped behind my back.

Clara’s lips parted. Her breath caught in her throat. She set the basin down with trembling hands, wiping her rubber apron, her eyes welling with sudden, overwhelming emotion.

She walked toward me, stopping three paces away, her head bowed.

“Lorenz...” she whispered, her voice choked with tears. “I... I have worked every day. I have washed their burns. I have scrubbed the floors. I haven’t complained once.”

“I have reviewed the hospital records, Orderly Clara,” I replied, my voice steady and professional.

Clara stepped closer, looking up into my eyes with desperate, pleading hope.

“Lorenz... do you remember when we were ten winters old? We sat on the bank of the river, and you carved me a little wooden sparrow. You told me you would always protect me. I know I was blind. I know I let Marek seduce me with his gilded words and his promises of courtly balls. But that is all ashes now! I see who you are! You are a great leader! Lorenz, please... let me come back to you. Let me be your companion again.”

I pulled a typed administrative chart from my coat pocket and held it before her eyes.

“Look at this document, Orderly Clara.”

Clara blinked through her tears, staring at the black ink columns. “What... what is it?”

“This is the Monthly Labor Attrition and Recovery Chart for Sector Epsilon,” I stated. “Last month, forty-eight skilled machinists suffered second-degree chemical burns or high-pressure steam scalds. Under feudal medicine, thirty of those men would have died of gangrene, and eighteen would have been permanently crippled, destroying twelve thousand hours of precision tooling output.”

I tapped the bottom row of the ledger.

“Through your accelerated cellular mitosis, forty-six of those men were returned to active production within forty-eight hours. Two hundred rifles and six thousand artillery shells were manufactured because those men remained at their lathes.”

I lowered the paper and met her gaze with absolute, unyielding clarity.

“In three years of wandering behind Marek, your existence was a decorative luxury that produced zero tangible value for human society. You were an emotional indulgence. Today, your hands saved forty-six productive lives. Your existence now possesses measurable, objective worth.”

Clara’s shoulders trembled. “Is that... is that all I am to you? An objective metric? Do you feel nothing for me?”

“Sentimentality is a luxury for stagnant, dying societies,” I replied calmly. “The Directorate does not trade in nostalgic tears. We trade in survival, order, and steel. Continue your duties, Orderly Clara. You are useful.”

I turned on my polished heels and strode down the corridor, leaving her standing in the clean, smelling ward, her tears falling upon the report of forty-six saved lives.
