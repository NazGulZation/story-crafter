# Chapter 15: Labor Reallocation

Romantic sentimentality is an unseeded liability that produces zero caloric surplus.

In the decadent melodramas favored by the southern nobility, the discarded protagonist—having achieved supreme sovereign power through great trials—summons the women who betrayed him to his grand throne room. He sits upon velvet cushions, surrounded by golden candelabras, while the weeping heroines crawl before him in silken rags. He lectures them on their perfidy; they tear their hair in remorse; and either he forgives them in an emotional crescendo or banishes them with a grand, tragic gesture.

Both outcomes are administratively absurd.

They waste valuable candle wax, expend unnecessary dramatic energy, and fail to optimize the manual labor allocation of three able-bodied female units.

At 1630 hours, in the Reassignment Bureau of Sector 1, there were no velvet cushions.

There were two whitewashed pine desks, three iron stools bolted to the slate floor, an ungreased coal brazier providing seventy degrees of ambient warmth, and a pervasive, stinging stench of boiled carbolic lye and wet hemp cloth.

Clara, Isolde, and Brigit sat on the three iron stools.

They were dressed in the standardized female convict uniform of the *Ordensstaat*: high-collar smocks of undyed grey hemp, thick wool stockings tied with twine below the knee, and heavy unpadded wooden clogs. Their heads, shaved down to bristling grey-blonde stubble, accentuated the sharp, gaunt lines of their cheekbones.

Across the desk sat Director Weidmann, his brass spectacles reflecting the blue flame of a small alcohol burner he was using to melt sealing wax.

I sat to his right, reviewing three blue-tinted cardstock booklets: *Form 14-Labor: Permanent Citizen Allocation*.

Clara had not ceased weeping for four hours.

Her eyes were swollen into red, puffy slits; her nose ran continuously, which she wiped against the coarse hemp of her sleeve because handkerchiefs were categorized as an unauthorized bourgeois luxury.

When I closed the first booklet with a sharp, dry tap, Clara jerked violently, falling from the iron stool onto her knees. She crawled forward on the damp slate, clasping her hands together in a desperate, trembling prayer against my boot toe.

“Lorenz... please,” Clara sobbed, her voice hoarse, cracking with complete exhaustion. “Look at me. Please, just look at my face. Don’t you remember the elderberry grove behind the mill? Don’t you remember when we were twelve, and you picked wild strawberries for me because my mother beat me? You said... you said you would always protect me.”

I did not move my boot. I did not recoil.

“The physiological event to which you refer occurred nine years, four months, and twelve days ago,” I said, looking down at her shorn scalp. “At that time, both biological units were suffering from childhood caloric deficits and lacked basic operational comprehension of state logistics. Citing obsolete childhood anecdotes does not alter your current adult productivity index.”

“I made a mistake!” Clara wailed, clutching the leather of my boot as if it were an altar in a burning temple. “I was weak! Marek was so big... he had a golden tongue... he promised me the world, Lorenz! But I never loved him! When he touched me, I felt dirty! I wanted to run back to you, but I was afraid he would kill you! I did it to protect you! Can’t you see that?! I sacrificed my honor so Marek wouldn't crush your skull!”

Beside her, Brigit let out a sharp, bitter snort.

“Gods above, Clara,” Brigit muttered, leaning her head back against the whitewashed wall, her scarred hands resting flat on her knees. “Shut up. Just shut your lying mouth. You were moaning his name so loud on the mountain that the wolves stopped howling. You didn't sacrifice a damn thing. You wanted his silk dresses and his greatsword, and now we’re wearing wooden shoes. Accept it.”

Clara gasped, glaring at Brigit with hysterical venom. “Traitor! You were in his bed too!”

“Aye,” Brigit said coldly, her dark eyes hollow and unblinking. “I was. Because I thought he had gold and strength. He had neither. I made a bad scout's bet. Now I’m paying the tab.”

She looked at me across the desk. Her posture was straight, her jaw set with the grim stoicism of a veteran hunter who had stepped into an iron bear-trap and was waiting for the skinning knife.

“What’s the labor quota, Lorenz?” Brigit asked, her voice steady. “Give it to me straight. No poetry.”

I opened Brigit’s folder.

“Asset 802: Brigit. Your assignment to *Timber Clearance Unit 4* is confirmed. You will be deployed to the high pine bluffs in the North-Eastern Defile. Your team is tasked with felling ninety mature larch trees daily to supply the pit-props for the anthracite mines. Due to your documented balance and familiarity with uneven terrain, you are designated Assistant Sawyer.”

Brigit nodded once. “Shift length?”

“Fourteen hours. Rations: two thousand calories of rye bread, four ounces of salt pork, and one pint of boiled pine-needle infusion daily for scurvy prevention. If your team meets its weekly quota, you will be issued a pair of greased leather boots with woolen liners.”

A faint, almost imperceptible breath of relief escaped Brigit’s cracked lips. “Boots. Real boots.”

“Real boots,” I confirmed. “The State rewards productivity. Sign on line four.”

Brigit picked up the iron stylus, dipped it in black ink, and firmly signed her name with steady fingers. She stood up, stepped backward into the corner, and folded her hands behind her back, assuming the position of an obedient, functional worker.

I opened the second folder: *Asset 803: Isolde*.

Isolde sat rigid on her stool. Her face was deathly pale, her thin lips pressed into a tight, haughty line, though her knuckles were white where she gripped the folds of her smock.

“You cannot do this to me, Lorenz,” Isolde said, attempting to infuse her voice with the cold authority of an academy scholar. “I have studied at the High Conclave of Osterlund. I possess third-tier theoretical certification in thermal dynamics and elemental projection. To reduce an educated mage to a manual laborer is a barbaric crime against human intellect!”

I picked up her personnel audit.

“Your academic credentials have been reviewed by Director Weidmann,” I replied. “During your three years at the Conclave, you accumulated six hundred and forty silver florins in debt to local wine merchants, passed zero examinations in structural civil engineering, and specialized exclusively in 'aesthetically luminous personal pyrotechnics'—in other words, theatrical fire-dances for noble garden parties.”

Isolde flushed crimson, her eyes flashing with indignant fury. “I can summon a wall of flame thirty feet wide!”

“A wall of flame that burns for six seconds, consumes three ounces of rare sulfur powder, and generates zero pounds of pressure per square inch,” I retorted cold-bloodedly. “Blast Furnace No. 2, by contrast, operates at two thousand degrees continuously for eight months without interruption, producing four hundred tons of structural steel.”

I pointed to the technical diagram clipped to her file.

“You have been designated *Thermal Stabilizer Asset 02*. You will sit in the refractory chamber beneath the pre-heater manifold of Furnace No. 2. When the coke fire fluctuates due to damp limestone flux, you will channel your magical resonance into the intake air to maintain a constant eleven-hundred-degree pre-heat. You are an energetic thermostat.”

Isolde’s eyes dilated in pure horror. “A... a thermostat?! You want me to sit in a brick cellar... and heat air?!”

“Air heating is an essential component of metallurgical autarky,” I said. “Shift: twelve hours. Ration: eighteen hundred calories. If you refuse your assignment, the furnace manifold will simply be operated by four conscripts burning additional anthracite, and you will be transferred to the human fertilizer processing vats. Sign.”

Isolde looked at the paper. She looked at Director Weidmann, who was watching her with the unblinking, reptilian gaze of a tax clerk about to foreclose on a widow's cottage.

Her lower lip began to tremble. The last shreds of her academic vanity dissolved into cold, crushing reality.

Shaking violently, Isolde picked up the stylus and signed the parchment. She lowered her head, weeping silently into her lap, her pride extinguished forever beneath the soot of the blast furnaces.

Finally, I turned to the third folder: *Asset 804: Clara*.

Clara was still on the floor, her cheek pressed against the cold slate, shivering as if afflicted with swamp-ague.

“Lorenz...” she whimpered, her voice barely audible over the crackle of the coal brazier. “Please... I beg you... I can’t carry the legs. I can’t touch the sick men. I get sick so easily... you remember when I had the lung-rot? You sat by my bed for two weeks... you held my hand... you sang to me...”

“I did,” I replied. “A catastrophic expenditure of seventy-two hours of potential labor that nearly resulted in the loss of my own biological capabilities. I was young, unregimented, and profoundly stupid.”

I stamped the red ink seal onto her assignment sheet with a sharp, concussive *THUD*.

“Your assignment to the *Field Dysentery Isolation Ward* is finalized,” I announced.

Clara let out a choked shriek, clutching her head in her hands. “No! No! Please! Anything else! I’ll clean your office! I’ll wash your clothes! I’ll sleep on the floor beside your bed like a dog! Just don’t send me to the rot-house!”

“My office is cleaned by a disabled veteran of the Frontier Guard who possesses a sixty-percent lung pension and a medal for artillery loading,” I said. “He requires eighteen minutes to scrub this floor to a hygienic tolerance of ninety-eight percent. You would spend four hours crying about your fingernails and attempting to seduce the guards.”

I looked at Director Weidmann.

“Director.”

“Sir!”

“Deliver Asset 804 to Head Matron Gertrude at Isolation Block 3. Inform the Matron that the asset has a documented history of emotional manipulation and malingering. If she drops a bedpan, she is to be docked three hundred calories from her evening soup.”

“With the greatest pleasure, Supreme Commissary,” Weidmann squeaked, his quill scratching the transfer slip with vicious delight.

Two female orderlies in rubber aprons entered the room. They reached down, grabbed Clara beneath her armpits, and hauled her to her feet.

She did not walk. Her knees were limp; her wooden clogs dragged helplessly across the slate, leaving two wet, squeaking trails.

“Lorenz!” she screamed, her voice echoing down the stone corridor in a frantic, rising spiral of absolute madness. “I’m your Clara! You loved me! You’ll always love me! You can’t do this! The Dawn will curse you! The gods will strike you down! Lorenz—!”

*SLAM.*

The iron door closed.

The screams were cut off instantly by three inches of solid, rubber-gasketed oak and sheet iron.

The room returned to absolute silence.

The alcohol lamp flickered. Director Weidmann carefully filed the three signed contracts into the steel cabinet behind his desk, turned the heavy brass key, and tucked it into his waistcoat.

“All three assets successfully integrated into the provincial labor grid, Supreme Commissary,” Weidmann reported. “Net municipal administrative efficiency increased by zero point twelve percent.”

“A satisfactory outcome,” I said, rising from my chair and buttoning my storm-coat. “Order has been restored.”

I turned toward the exterior door, stepping out onto the covered timber walkway.

The winter blizzard had arrived in full force. The air was thick with blinding, swirling sheets of white snow, howling through the pine ramparts, covering the roofs of the barracks in a clean, immaculate blanket of white.

Down in the valley, the three blast furnaces burned with fierce, unyielding orange fury against the storm, their smoke rising in dark, triumphant plumes into the frozen sky.

Through the blizzard, a courier on a heavy, iron-shod mountain horse galloped through the outer gate, pulling his mount to a skidding halt before the guardhouse.

Corporal Brandt caught the horse’s reins as the rider vaulted from the saddle, clutching an oilskin dispatch pouch sealed with three drops of purple wax.

Brandt took the dispatch, sprinted up the stairs, and executed a breathless, snow-dusted salute.

“Supreme Commissary! A royal rider from the provincial capital! Urgent dispatch from the High Count of Val-d'Or!”

I took the pouch, sliced the purple wax with my thumb-nail, and unrolled the vellum inside.

It was written in ornate, flowery calligraphy, bordered with gold leaf, and stamped with the heraldic stag of the southern aristocracy:

> *To the Self-Appointed Bailiff of Karst-Hold:*
> *Know that His Illustrious Grace, Count Leopold of the High Marches, has received word of your unauthorized levies, your unlawful seizure of the King’s mines, and the harboring of three thousand runaway serfs.*
> *His Grace approaches with eighty knights of the Golden Order and two thousand southern foot to reassert royal dominion and hang all rebels from the battlements.*
> *Surrender your keys upon our arrival, or face total annihilation.*

I read the missive twice, folded the vellum neatly along its creases, and handed it to Director Weidmann.

“What are your commands, Commissary?” Weidmann asked, his voice trembling slightly at the mention of the High Count. “Do we prepare a parley?”

I looked out over the battlements, where four newly mounted heavy iron siege mortars were already being covered with waterproof tarpaulins by my disciplined sentries.

“Parley is an unnecessary expenditure of paper,” I said calmly.

“Inform Master Torvald to prepare sixteen hundred canisters of heavy canister shot. The feudal aristocracy has requested an administrative audit.”
