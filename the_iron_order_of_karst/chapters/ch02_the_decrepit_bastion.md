# Chapter 2: The Decrepit Bastion

The outpost of Karst-Hold stood as an appalling municipal health hazard with crenellations.

I arrived at 0530 hours, leading the grey mule Karl by his halter through a swirling, grey dawn drizzle. The animal’s hooves made wet, rhythmic squelching sounds in the unpaved limestone slurry of the outer perimeter. Karl was breathing with commendable regularity, his head low, his iron shoes clicking softly against submerged river rocks. A disciplined beast.

The military facility before us exhibited zero signs of discipline.

The primary ditch was half-filled with rotting cabbage leaves, dead sheep carcasses, and stagnant brown water that smelled strongly of human excrement. The timber palisade was suffering from severe dry-rot, its oak posts leaning outward at a sixty-degree angle as if exhausted by their own existence. Overhead, the portcullis hung suspended by a frayed hemp cable that looked incapable of supporting a sack of grain, let alone two tons of iron-bound timber.

No watchfires were lit. No sentries were posted on the ramparts. The heavy oak gates stood half-ajar, wedged open by a mound of discarded turnip peels.

I paused at the gatehouse entrance, tapped my index finger twice against my collar seam, and felt an involuntary twitch of administrative rage behind my left eye.

In the *Ordensstaat*, an unsealed gate after curfew resulted in the garrison commander spending forty-eight hours in a concrete immersion cell. Here, a marauding war-band could have ridden through the portal in four-abreast cavalry formation, set fire to the magazine, and departed with the regimental payroll before anyone noticed.

I unslung the steel crossbow from my shoulder, rested it across the crook of my left arm, and led Karl into the inner courtyard.

The courtyard smelled of stale peat smoke, sour mash, and unwashed bodies. A scrawny pig was chewing on an old leather greave beneath a sagging lean-to. In the center of the yard, slumped against a stack of ungreased oak firewood, sat a man in a rusted iron kettle-helm.

His chin rested on his breastplate. A half-empty ceramic jug of fermented turnip beer was clutched in his greasy right gauntlet. A long line of dried yellow drool extended from the corner of his grey-bearded mouth to his rusted gorget.

I walked over, stood directly above him, and kicked the base of his iron kettle-helm with the steel toe of my ox-hide boot.

*CLANG.*

The acoustic resonance was sharp and metallic. The man gasped, choked on his own phlegm, and jerked violently, sending the ceramic jug tumbling into the mud.

“Wha—! In the name of the Dawn’s Lance—who goes—!”

He scrambled backward into the firewood, his left hand grasping blindly for a notched iron broadsword lying five feet away in a puddle of pig grease. He blinked through rheumy, bloodshot eyes, trying to focus on my figure through the morning mist.

I remained motionless, my posture erect, hands clasped behind my back over the oiled storm-coat.

“Name. Rank. Unit designation,” I said. My voice was pitched at conversational volume, completely flat, devoid of emotional emphasis.

The man wheezed, wiping his mouth with the back of a filthy leather glove. “Who the hell are you, boy?! Sneaking into the bailey like a cutpurse! Do you know where you are?! This is Karst-Hold! Royal frontier post of the High March! I am Sergeant Kester, and if you don’t drop that cart handle, I’ll have the lads flay your hide for a drumhead!”

“Sergeant Kester,” I noted, committing the data point to memory. “Forty-eight to fifty-two years of age. Severe cardiovascular degradation from alcohol abuse. Uniformity violations: rusted kettle-helm, untied armor buckles, lack of sidearm retention lanyard. Sentry violation: sleeping on watch during pre-dawn operational hours in an unfortified perimeter. Penalty per Field Regulation 14-B: forty lashes and reduction to labor conscript.”

Kester stared at me, his jaw hanging slack. He looked around the empty, drizzle-soaked courtyard as if expecting an audience to explain the joke.

“What... what regulations? What are you babbling about, you scrawny whelp? Where did you get that coat? Did you rob an officer? Lads! Hannes! Wulf! Get out here! We’ve got a lunatic with a mule!”

From the low, timber-framed barracks across the yard came the sluggish sounds of waking men: groans, coughing fits, the clatter of a kicked chamberpot, and the creak of ungreased door hinges.

Within three minutes, eleven men stumbled out onto the porch.

The garrison assembled as a staggering biological catastrophe.

Not one of them wore a complete harness. One corporal was wearing an iron breastplate over bare, hairy skin; another had boots on the wrong feet; three were completely unarmed, clutching wool blankets around their shivering shoulders. Their skin had the sickly, grey-yellow pallor of scurvy and chronic turnip consumption. Several of them smelled of untreated tooth decay and cold sweat.

“What’s the racket, Sarge?” the bare-chested corporal grunted, scratching his groin. “Is the supply wagon from the valley here? Did they bring the smoked bacon?”

“He’s got a mule,” another whispered, pointing a blackened thumb at Karl. “Look at the sacks in that cart. That’s rye. Real rye!”

A murmur of predatory hunger ran through the group. Two men took half-steps forward, their eyes fixing on the thirty-pound grain sacks visible beneath my tarpaulin.

I kept my hands resting deliberately at my sides, projecting unshakeable authority.

Instead, I reached into the breast pocket of my storm-coat, drew out the iron-banded parchment certificate I had extracted from the Silver Dawn's strongbox, and unrolled it with a sharp, snapping flick of my wrists.

The red wax seal of the Provincial Chancery of Osterlund gleamed wetly in the grey light.

“Pursuant to Imperial Decree 109, Article 4,” I announced, projecting my voice so that it bounced off the rotting timber barracks like a gunshot, “all civilian and paramilitary authority within the Karst Frontier Sector is hereby placed under Direct Administrative Commissariat. I am Commissary Vogel. As of 0530 hours, this facility is under martial audit.”

The men blinked. The bare-chested corporal frowned, squinting at the red wax. “Decree what? Who’s the king? Old King Theobald died two winters ago, didn't he?”

“It doesn't matter who occupies the throne in the south,” I said coldly. “The state exists independently of biological figureheads. The state is logistics. And this facility is operating at an administrative deficit that borders on treason.”

Sergeant Kester finally managed to get his boots beneath him. He stood up, towering over me by six inches, his broad beer-belly straining against the rusted links of his mail coif. He took a heavy, intimidating step forward, beer-breath washing over my face like hot sewage.

“Listen here, little clerk,” Kester snarled, resting a heavy, callused hand on the pommel of his rusted broadsword. “I don’t know what village notary you stole that parchment from, but out here, paper doesn't stop steel. We haven't seen a pay-wagon from the capital in fourteen months. We eat turnip mush and salt grass. The local robber-baron, Master Vorn, takes half our grain, and the Adventurers’ Guild laughs in our faces when we ask for patrol support. If you’ve got grain in that cart, you’ll hand it over for the garrison mess, or we’ll bury you under the latrine trench and say the wolves got you.”

The eleven men behind him shifted, several picking up rusty bills and wood-axes.

I looked Kester directly in the pupils. I did not blink.

“A predictable sociological response,” I observed calmly. “A population subjected to systemic supply deprivation naturally reverts to primitive brigandage. However, your threat assessment is fundamentally flawed.”

Kester’s brow furrowed. “My what?”

“Your tactical calculations, Sergeant. First: you are standing five inches inside the fatal traverse arc of my steel crossbow, which is currently cocked and aimed at your liver beneath this coat. At this range, the tempered steel quarrel will penetrate your mail, your abdominal wall, and sever your descending aorta. You will bleed out into your abdominal cavity in forty-two seconds. Medical treatment in this facility is non-existent, meaning your survival probability is zero percent.”

Kester froze. The color drained slightly from his red, alcohol-flushed cheeks. His fingers tightened on his hilt, but he did not draw.

“Second,” I continued, my voice measured and cold. “You stated that this garrison has gone without pay for fourteen months. A military garrison functions as a mechanical component of state infrastructure. Your needs are physical and operational: caloric fuel, functional equipment, and disciplined purpose.”

I pointed a gloved finger at the lean-to behind the barracks.

“During my approach, I observed a plume of blue woodsmoke rising from the rear revetment. Blue smoke indicates high-temperature combustion of seasoned birch. You are not using birch to heat the barracks, because your men are shivering. You are using it to fuel an illicit copper-pipe pot still. You are converting thirty percent of your emergency winter turnip and barley allocation into sixty-proof rotgut liquor, which you and your corporals consume or trade to passing caravans for tobacco.”

The bare-chested corporal gasped, taking an instinctive step backward. Kester’s eyes widened in genuine panic.

“Third,” I said, stepping past Kester as if he were an uncarved fence post, walking directly toward the center of the courtyard. “The roster board beside the mess hall lists forty-two active personnel. There are twelve men standing in this courtyard. Where are the remaining thirty?”

Silence fell over the bailey. The only sound was the drip of rain from the rotting eaves.

“Six died of winter-rot,” one of the younger conscripts stammered, his teeth chattering. “Eight... eight ran off to join Master Vorn’s road-gangs down at the Red Kilns. The rest are... asleep in the hayloft with the fever.”

“Gross manpower attrition through administrative neglect,” I stated. “A criminal dereliction of asset management.”

I turned, facing the twelve shivering men. I unclasped the leather flap of the supply cart and pulled back the tarpaulin, revealing the three sacks of highland rye, the casks of cured pork, and the strongbox.

“In this cart are ninety pounds of clean, dry rye flour,” I said. “Forty-five pounds of cured pork. Salt. Clean tallow. And sixty iron-tipped quarrels.”

The men stared at the food with the wide, hollow eyes of starving hounds.

“Under my administration,” I announced, “the illicit still behind the revetment will be dismantled before midday. Its copper tubing will be melted down and recast into functional rivets and crossbow stirrups. All remaining barley and turnips will be seized into the central commissary. The daily caloric intake per man will be raised from nine hundred calories of fermented mush to two thousand four hundred calories of hot rye bread, pork broth, and clean boiled water. The sick men in the hayloft will be bathed and administered willow-bark tea. You will have dry straw in your bunks and boots that fit your feet.”

A young soldier with a swollen jaw swallowed hard, his eyes wide. “Two... two thousand four hundred calories? Bread? Clean boots? Every day?”

“Every day,” I confirmed. “I am a pragmatist, soldiers. Cruelty is the resort of an incompetent feudal warlord who does not understand biology. Caloric energy and disciplined engineering construct fortresses. Cold, abused men collapse in ditches and squander state capital. Well-fed, warm soldiers held to unbending standards hold the gate against an empire.”

I stepped forward, looking directly at Kester and his men.

“This is the covenant of the *Ordensstaat*: The Carrot and the Stick. Perform your duties with precision, maintain your equipment, and you will live with dignity, security, and plenty such as you have never known under your drunken feudal lords. If you make an honest mistake, I will train you. If your muscles are weak, I will strengthen them.”

My voice dropped, turning into cold, unyielding iron.

“*However.* The line between discipline and treason is absolute. If you steal grain from the commissary, if you consume alcohol on duty, or if you deliberately betray this garrison—the stick will fall without hesitation. Insubordination directly sabotages the survival of the collective. Deliberate treason earns immediate, permanent liquidation.”

Kester’s mouth opened, but no words emerged. His gaze darted between the gleaming red wax seal in my left hand, the steel crossbow concealed beneath my right arm, and the enormous sacks of rye flour in the cart.

A military mind—even one rotted by turnip beer—understands the profound genius of fair, unyielding power.

I offered both life and bread with absolute, terrifying certainty.

“Sergeant Kester,” I said, my voice cutting through the damp chill like an iron blade. “You have sixty seconds to decide whether you wish to die as a mutinous drunkard in your own foul ditch, or whether you wish to eat hot pork porridge, lead well-fed men, and learn how to march like a soldier of the *Ordensstaat*.”

Kester’s throat bobbed. He looked at his men. The eleven conscripts stared at me with the desperate reverence of starving animals beholding the master of the grain bin.

Slowly, awkwardly, Kester’s right hand fell away from his sword hilt.

He pulled his boots together in the mud, stiffened his sagging spine, and raised a trembling, dirt-caked right gauntlet to the brim of his rusted kettle-helm.

“Awaiting... awaiting your orders, Commissary,” Kester rasped.

My expression remained rigid, conserving facial muscle tension.

“At ease,” I said. “Unload the cart. Sergeant, bring me the armory keys and the facility payroll ledger. We have sixteen hours of daylight remaining, and this fortress is an embarrassment to human civilization.”
