# Chapter 1: The Cold Sentry

Freezing mountain sleet lashed my face with the bite of crushed ice.

A foul cocktail of cheap elderberry wine, vomit, and wet woolen fibers coated my cracked lower lip. My lungs burned with the thin, rattling rasp of advanced pleurisy. When I tried to flex my right hand, the knuckles refused to close, frozen into stiff grey claws coated in greasy limestone mud.

This was an intolerable breach of post-mortem expectations.

Twelve seconds prior—or perhaps twelve lifetimes—I had been leaning against the shattered granite balustrade of the Municipal Telegraph Bureau in Sector 4 of Ost-Paris. A Charbonnier partisan rifle bullet had torn through my right lung and shattered my sixth rib. Blood, thick and arterial, had foamed through my teeth, staining the silver collar-tabs of my charcoal high-collar tunic. As my vision narrowed to a pinprick of grey static, I had gripped the field telephone with the last strength in my forearms, issuing the only logical directive left to an officer of the *Ordensstaat*:

*“Kommando Eins... cordon the ninth arrondissement. Cleanse the entire sector, brick by brick. Permit no survivors.”*

I had closed my eyes with a single teardrop rolling down my cheek, grieving only that my eyes would not behold the final thousand-year triumph of supreme order. I had expected the void. I had expected the cold Nordic hall of eternal iron.

Instead, I was sitting in a puddle of freezing clay on the side of a godforsaken mountain, listening to three women scream in synchronized, breathless ecstasy through the oiled canvas of a nearby silk pavilion.

I pushed myself upright.

My spine gave a sickening, cartilaginous crack. The body was small. Scrawny. The collarbones protruded beneath a tattered sheepskin vest like plucked chicken ribs. My feet were shoved into cracked rawhide moccasins bound with frayed hemp twine, soaked through with freezing water that had turned my toes into numb tallow candles.

*“Ah—Marek! More! Sunder it, please!”*

The voice belonged to a girl named Clara.

The memory bloomed in my cerebral cortex like a burst boil, accompanied by a nauseating wave of biological sorrow that belonged to the previous occupant of this wretched carcass.

His name had been Lorenz Falk. Twenty-one winters. An orphan. A baggage-hauler, herbalist, and scout for the "Silver Dawn"—a freelance adventurer company operating out of the frontier marches.

For four years, this pathetic organism had starved himself to finance the education of three orphaned girls. He had sold his dead mother’s silver locket to buy Clara’s novice priestess robes and her gilded holy focus. He had contracted swamp-fever in the sunken peat bogs collecting sulfur crystals so that Isolde could learn second-circle pyromancy. He had hand-carved the yew bow for Brigit, the ranger, nearly losing three fingers to cave viper venom while milking poison for her hunting shafts.

He had loved them with the trembling, submissive, mewling desperation characteristic of an unregimented civilian.

And then, precisely twenty-two days ago, Marek of High-Crest—a six-foot-four itinerant mercenary boasting a two-handed iron greatsword, chestnut hair drenched in perfumed lavender oil, and an intellect barely superior to that of a rutting boar—had joined the party.

Marek had not contributed thirty copper farthings to the party's operating ledger. He had simply smiled, flexed his deltoids during an encampment fire, and cleaved a mountain wolf in two with a theatrical flourish.

Within forty-eight hours, Brigit had moved her bedroll to Marek’s side of the camp, declaring that Lorenz’s movement speed was "slowing down the party’s tactical velocity." Within a week, Isolde was feeding Marek candied figs while laughing at Lorenz’s soot-blackened fingers. And last night, Clara—the childhood friend whose virtue Lorenz had guarded like a consecrated relic—had slipped into Marek’s heated three-room silk pavilion without looking back.

*“Keep watch on the perimeter defile, little mule,”* Marek had grunted two hours ago, tossing a cracked ash-wood spear into the mud at Lorenz's feet. *“The crags are swarming with night-crawlers. If a beast approaches, scream loudly so I have time to buckle my cuirass. Consider it your contribution to the team.”*

Lorenz had stood in the sleet, shivering, weeping silently into his cracked hands, listening to the bedframe groan under the rhythmic weight of four bodies, until his cardiovascular system collapsed from acute hypothermia and cardiac failure.

And in that vacant, shivering vessel, I had arrived.

I stood there in the dark, blinking rain from my lashes, conducting a cold administrative analysis of the biological memories.

The previous tenant’s emotional despair was completely baffling.

To feel romantic distress over the physical copulation of three unproductive dependents was an absurd misallocation of mental faculties. What mattered—what made the bile rise in my throat with pure, professional disgust—was the **catastrophic operational failure** of this encampment, combined with the vilest social crime known to military science: **deliberate, conscious betrayal**.

I am a man of uncompromising pragmatism. In the *Ordensstaat*, statecraft rejected mindless sadism. Gratuitous cruelty is the amateurish vice of weak, decadent warlords who do not understand caloric conservation. A leader governs by the rational application of the carrot and the stick. Hard work, loyalty, and competence earn predictable bread, warm shelter, and advancement. Honest errors demand correction through instruction and drill.

*Deliberate treason—a conscious breach of order and bad faith—belongs to an entirely different category.*

These four individuals had violated that sacred principle. They had entered into a mutual covenant of survival with Lorenz Falk. Lorenz had poured his own lifeblood, his calories, and his meager coin into their training and equipment. In return, the moment an arrogant mercenary offered them cheap glamour, they made a **conscious, cold-blooded decision to betray their benefactor**. They stripped him of his boots, mocked his starvation, and deliberately ordered him into the freezing sleet to die of exposure so they would not have to share their rations.

Incompetence can be reformed. Physical weakness can be trained. But deliberate betrayal—turning your blade against the hand that fed you while basking in unearned luxuries—is a malignant social cancer.

A social cancer demands immediate, surgical excision.

This was Raven's Gap: an unmapped defile in the northern limestone crags, notorious for mountain banditry and predatory highland wolves.

And yet:
1. No defensive ditch had been excavated around the perimeter.
2. No barbed abatis or trip-flares had been placed along the gorge entrance.
3. The main lantern inside the pavilion was casting a massive, three-hundred-yard light signature into the pitch-black valley.
4. The noise discipline was non-existent; the rhythmic screeching and animalistic grunting from the tent could be heard halfway down the mountain.
5. The designated sentry was left to freeze to death without weapons, shoes, or medical support.

*Gross negligence. Dereliction of duty in an active combat sector. Premeditated abandonment of a comrade. Class-1 Treason against military doctrine.*

If this had been the Western Wall in 1962, every occupant of that tent would have been lined against a revetment and liquidated before breakfast.

*“Marek! Ah—harder! You’re so much stronger than Lorenz ever was!”* Isolde’s voice trilled through the canvas, followed by the wet slap of bare flesh and Marek’s deep, self-satisfied chuckle.

*“The runt couldn't lift my scabbard, let alone handle three thoroughbreds,”* Marek bellowed from within. *“Forget him. He’s out in the rain where he belongs. Hey! Lorenz! Keep your eyes on the road, you useless dog! If my boots get mud on them tomorrow, you’re skipping breakfast!”*

I kept silent.

Engaging in verbal dialectic with undisciplined combat personnel is the mark of bureaucratic decay. Directives require execution; logistics demand an audit.

I turned my back on the pavilion and walked toward the two-wheeled supply cart tethered beneath a scrawny pine sixty yards away.

My limbs were trembling violently, but the trembling was merely thermodynamic physics—the biological reaction of cold muscle fibers. My mind was entirely clear. The cold was an operational parameter to be mitigated through immediate resource acquisition.

I climbed into the bed of the wagon and pulled back the tarpaulin.

The inventory was worse than I anticipated, but salvageable:
- One waterproof oilskin storm-coat lined with boiled sheep's wool (Marek's spare, bought with Lorenz's coin).
- One pair of heavy, greased ox-hide riding boots stuffed with dry straw.
- Three thirty-pound sacks of coarse highland rye grain.
- Forty-five pounds of cured salt pork in wooden firkins.
- One wooden ammunition case containing sixty iron-tipped hunting quarrels and a heavy hand-crank steel crossbow.
- One iron-banded timber strongbox with an unpickable tumbler lock.

I picked up a rusty iron felling axe from the floor of the cart. With two precise, mechanical strikes, I sheared off the brass hasp of the strongbox.

Inside lay the accumulated liquid capital of the "Silver Dawn": seventy-four silver florins, two hundred and twelve copper groschen, and three sealed parchment vouchers issued by the Provincial Guild Bureau of Osterlund, certifying the bearer as an authorized logistical commissary with rights to commandeer road transit.

Lorenz had saved this coin for four years to purchase an apothecary shop in the provincial capital, so that Clara would not have to sleep in temple dormitories.

*An appalling misallocation of municipal capital.*

I stripped off Lorenz’s soaking sheepskin rags. My skin was mottled blue and grey, goosebumps rising like gravel along my scrawny forearms. I pulled on the dry wool-lined tunic, buttoned the oiled storm-coat up to my throat, stepped into the thick ox-hide boots, and laced them tight. The heat was immediate, trapped against my chest by the heavy canvas.

From the wagon seat, I picked up Marek's backup hunting knife—seven inches of forged carbon steel—and tucked it into my belt. I slung the steel crossbow over my shoulder and secured the quiver across my chest.

Next, I turned my attention to the pack animals.

Tethered beside the cart were two beasts: Marek’s prize destrier—a massive, vain, white-maned stallion draped in decorative red wool trappings—and a sturdy, thick-boned grey mountain draft mule named Karl.

A warhorse requires four thousand calories of grain per day, produces enormous noise, and possesses thin ankles vulnerable to limestone sinkholes. A mule consumes scrub grass, maintains balance on a forty-degree scree slope, and pulls three hundred pounds without complaint.

I sliced the tether of the white stallion. With the flat of the felling axe, I struck the beast sharply across the flank. The stallion let out a terrified whinny and bolted down the jagged mountain path into the freezing fog, its red wool trappings fluttering into the darkness.

Inside the tent, the rhythmic slapping abruptly paused.

*“What was that?”* Brigit’s voice asked, laced with lazy post-coital breathlessness. *“Did you hear a horse?”*

*“The wind,”* Marek grunted. *“Or the runt tripped over his own feet. Come here, Clara. The night’s young.”*

*“Oh, Marek... you’re a monster...”*

I took the felling axe and walked calmly around the perimeter of the silk pavilion.

I ignored the sweat-slicked commotion within, leaving the flap untouched.

Instead, I located the eight primary tension guy-wires anchoring the tall central timber mast of the pavilion.

With eight swift, clean strokes of the axe, I severed every single hemp cable.

*CRACK.*

The heavy timber mast snapped sideways under the unbalanced tension. The entire twelve-foot silk pavilion, laden with thirty pounds of freezing rainwater that had pooled along its roof seams, collapsed instantly in on itself like a suffocated lung.

A chorus of shrill, muffled shrieks erupted from beneath the heavy, soaked silk.

*“What the—!”*
*“Get off me! I can’t breathe!”*
*“Marek! The tent fell! My hair—the candle tipped over!”*
*“Where’s my sword?! Lorenz! You worthless peasant, what did you do?! Help us up!”*

Beneath the wet, thrashing mounds of fabric, limbs flailed in complete, undignified chaos. A small ember from an overturned brazier began to singe the edge of the silk, filling the cold air with the stench of scorched velvet and singed goose-down.

I kept a steady, measured pace to conserve caloric reserves across the uneven terrain.

I walked back to the wagon, hitched the grey mule Karl to the traces, took the reins in my gloved hands, and turned the cart toward the northern road.

Down in the valley, six leagues to the north through the swirling freezing rain, lay the derelict garrison outpost of Karst-Hold. A forgotten frontier fort, manned by broken men, abandoned by a decaying feudal kingdom, surrounded by lawless bandits and untamed wilderness.

A blank sheet of vellum.

A disordered, chaotic wasteland begging for the cold, methodical scalpel of the *Ordensstaat*.

Behind me, Marek’s muffled roars echoed across the rocky gorge, competing with the howling mountain wind and the distant, mournful baying of hungry wolves closing in on the smell of terrified flesh.

*“Lorenz! When I get out of this canvas, I’ll skin you alive! Do you hear me?! Lorenz!”*

I adjusted the collar of my oiled storm-coat against the driving sleet, clicked my tongue at the mule, and started down the mountain.

*Prisoner Falk, Lorenz, is dead,* I thought, my gaze fixed on the dark, jagged horizon. *Your insubordination has been noted. The audit has begun.*
