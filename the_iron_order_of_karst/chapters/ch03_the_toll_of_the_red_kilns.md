# Chapter 3: The Toll of the Red Kilns

By noon of the third day, the air in Karst-Hold smelled of boiled carbolic lye, hot vinegar, and the sharp, alkaline tang of freshly slaked lime.

The transformation served strictly sanitary and logistical imperatives, stripping away the frivolous rot of the frontier.

The decomposing sheep carcasses had been hauled out of the primary ditch, dragged three hundred yards downwind, drenched in pitch, and incinerated. The ditch itself had been deepened by four feet, its floor lined with sharpened stakes fashioned from the dry-rotted remains of the old pig-pen. The eleven conscripts, now reinforced by fourteen recovering fever-cases who had been routed from the haylofts with cold well-water and mandatory doses of boiled willow-bark tea, had ceased shivering.

They had ceased shivering because they were working sixteen hours a day.

When men are given twenty-two hundred calories of dense rye bread and salted pork broth, their bodies generate heat. When that heat is channeled into shoveling wet limestone, carrying granite ballast stones, and scraping forty years of rust from iron breastplates with sand and vinegar, they lack the cardiovascular reserves for mutinous contemplation.

Sergeant Kester was standing at my left elbow on the newly reinforced gatehouse platform. His beard had been trimmed with sheep-shears; his iron kettle-helm, scrubbed down to raw grey metal, sat squarely on his forehead without tilting. He held a wooden clipboard—an unvarnished pine shingle fitted with an iron clasp—clutched against his ribs with the desperate rigidity of a convert holding sacred scripture.

“Commissary,” Kester muttered, his breath misting in the cold drizzle. He did not smell of fermented turnips. He smelled of coarse soap and fear. “Lookouts at the southern bend signal dust on the old quarry track. It’s them. It’s the Red Kiln gang.”

I kept my gaze fixed on the armory inventory ledger.

“Specify strength and composition,” I said.

“Twenty-two riders,” Kester said, his voice tightening. “Led by Horgan the Cleaver. Ex-mercenary out of the border marches. Wears boiled bull-hide and swings a spiked horse-flail. He... he comes on the fifteenth of every month, Commissary. By ancient custom, the garrison gives him three sacks of grain, forty copper pieces, and a cask of turnip liquor. In return, he doesn’t burn the outer granary or slit the throats of our woodcutters.”

“Ancient custom,” I repeated. The phrase was foul, tasting like rotten marrow. “In other words: state-subsidized extortion by an unauthorized paramilitary cartel. A catastrophic balance-sheet leak.”

“He has twenty-two heavy horses, sir,” Kester stammered. “And his men have iron-tipped lances and short bows. If we don’t pay the tribute, he’ll lay siege to the well-house.”

“Bandits do not conduct sieges,” I stated, closing the ledger with a dry, papery snap. “Sieges require sustained logistical supply lines, engineering doctrine, and centralized command. Bandits conduct disorganized raids governed by predatory opportunism. A rudimentary biological pest.”

I stood up, adjusting the collar of my oiled storm-coat.

“Order Section One and Section Two to their designated stations behind the timber parapets. Crossbows cocked, quarrels in the grooves, safety pins removed. No man discharges a bolt until I drop my right hand. If any soldier fires prematurely, he will spend three days breaking granite in the ditch.”

“Understood, Commissary!” Kester swallowed hard and scurried down the rampart stairs, his hobnail boots clicking with uncharacteristic rhythm.

Five minutes later, the thud of heavy hooves echoed up the limestone defile.

Emerging from the grey pines near the abandoned brick kilns came twenty-two horsemen. They were wrapped in greasy furs, studded leather, and mismatched pieces of plate armor looted from dead caravaneers. At their head rode a barrel-chested man with a braided black beard, wearing a battered iron cuirass smeared with dried boar grease. In his right hand, he rested a heavy spiked steel ball on a five-link chain against his saddle horn.

They did not advance in tactical formation. They trotted in a loose, arrogant gaggle, laughing loudly, spitting tobacco juice onto the road, their horses kicking up wet gravel.

When Horgan reached the outer perimeter of the ditch, he pulled his mount to a halt and frowned.

The landscape had changed. The mound of rotting turnip peels that usually greeted him was gone, replaced by a steep, freshly excavated earthen berm and an eight-foot palisade of pointed pine stakes. The sagging gate was shut, newly banded with strips of raw sheet-iron scavenged from the dismantled distillery.

And sitting directly in front of the gate, outside the walls, sat a single pine desk and an unpainted wooden stool.

Sitting upon the stool was a scrawny young man in an oiled storm-coat, meticulously dipping an iron nib into a square ink pot, writing upon a sheet of vellum.

Horgan stared. His men chuckled, nudging each other with their elbows.

“What in the nine hells is this?” Horgan rumbled, walking his black stallion forward until the horse’s nostrils flared six feet from the edge of my desk. “Hey! Runt! Where’s Kester? Did the old drunkard finally choke on his own vomit?”

I finished writing the numeral *4*, blotted the vellum with a pinch of dry limestone dust, blew the residue away, and looked up.

“Sergeant Kester is currently engaged in facility maintenance,” I replied. “State your legal name, place of birth, and tax identification code for the provincial record.”

Horgan’s smile vanished. His dark, piggish eyes narrowed in disbelief.

Behind him, a scarred rider with a notched scimitar barked with laughter. “Boss, the garrison got themselves a schoolteacher! Look at him! He’s got an ink pot!”

Horgan leaned forward over his saddle horn, resting the heavy spiked flail against his thigh.

“Listen to me, little scribe,” Horgan sneered, his voice dropping into a menacing growl. “I don’t know what provincial office sent you out here to die, but you’re sitting on my road. The 15th of the month is tribute day. Forty coppers. Three sacks of flour. One barrel of sixty-proof turnip mash. Fetch it in sixty seconds, or I’ll loop this chain around your neck and drag you through the briars until your skin peels off like an onion.”

I picked up the sheet of vellum, adjusted the collar of my coat, and cleared my throat.

“Pursuant to the Administrative Reorganization Ordinance of the Karst March,” I read in a dry, monotone cadence, “the entity colloquially designated as the 'Bloody Boar Syndicate' is classified as an Unlawful Armed Consortium operating within a Sovereign Defensive Sector. Over the preceding forty-eight months, your organization has extracted seventy-two sacks of municipal grain, nine hundred and sixty copper groschen, and approximately six hundred gallons of untaxed alcohol from this facility.”

Horgan’s jaw tightened. “Are you mocking me, boy?”

“Furthermore,” I continued, ignoring him completely, “your unauthorized transit across the public highway has caused severe structural degradation to the culverts and gravel packing between Mile Marker 4 and Mile Marker 9. Accounting for back-taxes, extortion penalties, highway maintenance fees, and an eight-percent administrative surcharge for paper and ink, your aggregate debt to the State is calculated at four hundred and twelve silver florins and twenty copper groschen.”

A heavy, bewildered silence descended upon the mountain pass. The wind hissed through the pine needles.

Horgan blinked twice. He looked at the spiked flail in his hand, then at the sheet of paper in mine.

“You... you’re taxing me?” Horgan whispered, as if struggling to comprehend a foreign language. “You’re presenting an invoice... to Horgan the Cleaver?”

“Payment is due immediately,” I stated. “Due to your lack of accredited banking credentials, liquid specie, or bullion, the State will accept remittance in the form of twenty-two draft horses, thirty-four iron-tipped edged weapons, and four hundred and eighty hours of compensatory manual stone-breaking labor per individual.”

Horgan’s face turned the color of an overripe plum. The veins in his thick neck bulged against his collar.

“Kill him,” Horgan roared, rearing his black stallion onto its haunches, swinging the spiked flail in a whistling iron arc over his head. “Tear down the gate! Kill every dog inside! Flay this little pen-pusher alive!”

The twenty-two bandits let out a chorus of feral battle-howls, digging iron spurs into their mounts, drawing axes, scimitars, and short bows.

I remained motionless on the wooden stool, calm and centered.

I raised my right hand, held it motionless for precisely two seconds, and dropped it sharply to the surface of the pine desk.

*THWACK-THWACK-THWACK.*

From behind the pine palisade forty feet above us, twenty-four greased steel crossbows discharged in a synchronized, mechanical volley.

The acoustic shock echoed like the dry, industrial clatter of heavy machinery stamping sheet metal.

At a range of fifteen yards, against unarmored horses and soft boiled-leather jerkins, the tempered steel quarrels pulverized flesh and bone.

Three bolts struck Horgan’s black stallion simultaneously in the chest and throat, dropping the twelve-hundred-pound beast instantly onto its forelegs. Horgan was launched violently over the pommel, crashing face-first into the limestone mud three feet from my desk with a sickening crunch of broken cartilage and cracked ribs.

Behind him, the bandit charge dissolved into catastrophic, screaming slaughter.

Eight riders were blown out of their saddles before their horses could take three strides. Bolts punched through leather, muscle, and collarbones with concussive thuds. A horse screamed as a bolt sheared through its shoulder, collapsing sideways and crushing two dismounted bandits into the ditch stakes.

“Section Two—crank and reload!” Kester’s voice bellowed from the parapet, cracking with hysterical, newfound authority. “Section One—quarrels loaded! Present!”

*CLACK-CLACK-CLACK.*

The sound of twenty-four iron goat-foot levers pulling back steel bowstrings echoed across the defile.

“Wait! Hold! Don't shoot! By the gods, hold!”

The surviving nine bandits, covered in blood, mud, and horse-gore, threw themselves out of their saddles. They dropped their axes and scimitars into the dirt, falling to their knees with their hands raised high, trembling violently as they looked up at the twenty-four steel points leveled at their foreheads.

Horgan groaned, spitting out three bloody teeth and a mouthful of yellow clay. He clutched his broken collarbone, looking up at me with terror-stricken, uncomprehending eyes.

“What... what kind of soldiers are you?” he wheezed, blood bubbling from his nose. “You didn't... you didn't even issue a challenge... no parley...”

I dipped my iron nib back into the ink pot and made a neat, vertical tally mark at the bottom of the ledger.

“Parley wastes valuable operational time,” I said. “Banditry represents an unauthorized budget deficit, and budget deficits are eradicated immediately.”

I stood up, walked around the desk, and looked down at the bleeding chieftain.

“Sergeant Kester,” I called out.

“Sir!” Kester’s head appeared over the palisade, his eyes shining with awe.

“Secure the livestock. Collect all iron weapons, buckles, and horseshoes for the forge. Strip the dead of all salvageable cloth, wash it in lye, and shred it for wound dressings. The deceased will be processed into the lime pit.”

“And the prisoners, Commissary?” Kester asked, gesturing to the nine weeping survivors. “Do we string them from the gallows?”

“Execution is a waste of caloric investment,” I replied coldly. “And cruelty is the amateurish resort of savages. The State operates on rehabilitation through production. These men represent two thousand one hundred and sixty hours of potential quarry labor. Splint their broken bones, wash their lacerations in boiled vinegar, and issue each man an iron-banded sledgehammer. They are hereby designated *Forced Labor Unit Beta*.”

I turned to the shivering prisoners.

“The covenant applies to you as well,” I announced. “If you work with diligence and meet your three-ton daily quarry quota, you will receive two thousand two hundred calories of hot rye bread and pork broth—clean, abundant food you never once tasted while living like animals in the brush. If you exceed quota by twenty percent, your debt sentence will be shortened, and you will have the opportunity to earn full citizenship within the construction brigades. But if any man attempts escape, feigns illness, or commits deliberate sabotage—the stick will fall without mercy, and he will be laid beneath the ditch stakes.”

I looked down at Horgan, who was staring at me in stunned, uncomprehending awe. Offered hard labor, medical treatment, hot food, and an unbending mathematical path to redemption, the bandit chieftain bowed his head into the mud.

“Welcome to the *Ordensstaat*, Chieftain Horgan,” I said. “Your debt repayment begins at dawn.”
