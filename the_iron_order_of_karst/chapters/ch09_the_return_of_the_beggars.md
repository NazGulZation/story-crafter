# Chapter 9: The Return of the Beggars

A hero without a supply clerk is merely an aggressive, unwashed vagrant wandering through the bushes.

This biological truth was currently manifesting twelve miles south of Karst-Hold, on the rocky scree of Raven’s Defile, where four individuals were discovering that physical attractiveness does not prevent trench foot.

Eighty-six days had passed since the night the silk pavilion collapsed.

Those eighty-six days had not been a glorious saga of valiant conquest. They had been an unmitigated logistical apocalypse.

Leading the miserable procession down the mountain trail was Marek of High-Crest.

His six-foot-four frame, once the envy of tavern wenches and the terror of highway cutpurses, had suffered severe muscular wasting from eighty consecutive days of caloric deficit. The mirror-finish plate armor that had once dazzled onlookers was covered in orange rust, dented by falling rocks, and held together across the ribs with frayed strips of greasy pig-skin. His flowing chestnut locks were matted with burrs, pine resin, and dried mud, resembling the tail of an abused donkey.

At his hip, the legendary two-handed greatsword *Dawn-Sunderer* hung crookedly.

Its edge was notched like a crosscut saw. When the party’s iron felling axe had mysteriously vanished on the first night—along with their dry boots, three sacks of rye, and the draft mule—Marek had discovered that pine timber does not split on chivalric command. He had spent three weeks using a masterwork tempered carbon-steel blade to chop wet birch firewood, dulling the edge into a blunt iron club.

“I’m telling you, the village is just around the ridge,” Marek snarled, kicking a loose rock with a boot whose sole was flapping open like a hungry fish mouth. “Stop whining! Both of you! My head is pounding!”

Behind him, stumbling through the rotting slush, was Clara of the Weeping Spring.

Her lavender-scented silk chemise had disintegrated into grey, grease-stained ribbons. Her bare knees, poking through torn velvet skirts, were purple with cold and scabbed from multiple falls on the limestone scree. Her delicate priestess hands, which had once held silver chalices, were blackened with soot and covered in festering frostbite blisters.

“You said that yesterday, Marek!” Clara sobbed, tears cutting pale, dirty channels through the grime on her cheeks. “You said the town was two hours away! My boots rotted through four days ago! I can’t feel my left toes! I can’t walk anymore!”

“Then crawl, woman!” Marek barked, wiping green snot from his mustache with the back of a rusty gauntlet. “Whose fault was it that we lost the ration bags? You dropped the dried mutton down the ravine because you saw a field mouse!”

“It was a mountain viper!” Clara shrieked hysterically. “And where were you?! You were supposed to slay the beasts! You promised me a carriage! You promised me a villa in the capital with twelve servants! Look at me! I have lice! In my hair! Actual lice!”

“Shut up! Just shut your mouth!”

Walking three paces behind them was Isolde the Pyromancer, leaning heavily on a cracked hazel-wood staff.

Her scarlet scholastic robes, once the proud badge of an academy-trained elementalist, were scorched around the hem and stiff with dried mountain sheep grease. Her eyes were sunken, dark purple bruises of exhaustion ringing her hollow cheeks.

“We have no reagents,” Isolde croaked, her voice dry and rasping like dead leaves. “Not an ounce of brimstone. Not a pinch of sulfur. When the mountain cat attacked our camp last week, you told me to burn it, Marek. I had to burn our last dry spell-scroll just to spark a campfire because you don’t know how to strike flint!”

“The flint was wet!” Marek roared, turning around with veins bulging in his neck. “Everything was wet! That little rat Lorenz took the waterproof tarpaulins! He stole my reserve boots! When I find him, I swear by the Dawn’s Blade, I’ll chop his fingers off one by one and feed them to the crows!”

At the rear of the column trudged Brigit the Wood-Stalker.

She did not join in the screaming. She lacked the lung capacity. Her lips were cracked and bleeding; her yew recurve bow was unstrung, its belly warped from exposure to three weeks of continuous mountain sleet. Her quiver contained exactly two arrows, both with loose goose-feather fletchings bound with dirty wool yarn.

She had spent three days trying to trap alpine rabbits, only to discover that setting wire snares requires precise knowledge of animal tracks, soil compaction, and wind direction—mundane, unglamorous skills that Lorenz had performed every evening while she had lounged on Marek’s bedroll drinking mulled wine.

“He isn't going to be there,” Brigit muttered, her teeth clicking together.

Marek stopped, turning his bloodshot eyes upon her. “What did you say?”

“Lorenz,” Brigit rasped, staring blankly at the muddy trail. “He isn't going to be waiting in Karst-Hold. He walked away eighty days ago with the mule and the strongbox. He left us to die. He’s probably halfway to the southern coast by now, laughing at us.”

“Nonsense!” Clara cried, clutching her ruined holy icon to her chest. “Lorenz loved me! He worshipped the ground I walked upon! He starved himself for three years so I could have this silver focus! He was just... he was just hurt! He had a tantrum because he was jealous of Marek! Once he sees how much we’ve suffered—once he sees my blisters—he’ll cry and beg for my forgiveness! He always did! He’ll make us hot soup and mend my boots!”

“She’s right,” Marek sneered, regaining a shred of his old, arrogant bravado. “The boy was a dog. A loyal, groveling little cur. Where else would he go? He has no family. He has no strength. He couldn't kill a wild goat if it was tied to a post. He probably ran down to the garrison at Karst-Hold and begged Sergeant Kester for a bed in the straw.”

Marek adjusted the rusty scabbard of *Dawn-Sunderer*, puffing out his hollowed chest.

“When we reach the Hold, I’ll handle it. Old Kester knows me. I slew a swamp-troll for the garrison two years ago. We’ll commandeer the officers' quarters. We’ll demand three roast geese, four flagons of dark ale, and hot baths. And then I’ll have that little worm Lorenz brought before me in chains. I’ll make him shine my armor until it reflects the sun, and then maybe—just maybe—I’ll let him sleep by the hearth.”

Clara sniffled, wiping her nose on her torn sleeve, a faint, pathetic glimmer of hope returning to her hollow eyes. “Do... do you think they have soap? Scented rose soap?”

“Of course they have soap,” Marek laughed, though the laugh sounded like gravel rattling in an empty bucket. “It’s a border post! Drunk soldiers, fat bailiffs, and terrified peasants who don’t dare look an S-Rank adventurer in the eye! Come on! Another mile!”

They rounded the final jagged limestone shoulder of Raven’s Defile.

The valley of Karst-Hold opened up before them.

Marek opened his mouth to deliver another self-aggrandizing speech about heroic triumph.

The words died in his throat.

The party stopped dead in the middle of the road, freezing like stone statues in the rotting slush.

“What... what is that?” Isolde whispered, her hazel staff slipping from her numb fingers and clattering into the mud.

The sleepy, drunken, wooden hamlet of Karst-Hold was gone.

In its place sat an industrial nightmare of grey stone, iron, and black smoke.

A massive, eight-foot-deep anti-cavalry ditch, lined with geometric rows of sharpened iron stakes, stretched across the entire valley floor from bluff to bluff. Behind the ditch rose a towering rampart of tamped earth and squared limestone masonry, topped by an eight-foot palisade of charred pine logs.

Above the central gatehouse rose three massive timber watchtowers. Mounted upon their parapets stood silent sentries in matching charcoal wool greatcoats, motionless behind heavy, pre-sighted mechanical ballistas.

And above the highest tower, fluttering against the leaden, overcast sky, was a massive circular iron standard:

A stark, twelve-spoked geometric wheel forged of black pig-iron.

In the distance, three colossal blast furnaces belched thick pillars of grey sulfur smoke into the clouds, the rhythmic, concussive *THUD-THUD-THUD* of water-driven triphammers vibrating through the very soles of their rotted boots.

Across the valley, there was no sound of tavern laughter. There was no barking of loose mongrel hounds. There was only the deafening, mechanical roar of industry and the synchronized cadence of marching boots echoing off the dolomite cliffs.

Marek blinked, his jaw dropping so low that a string of dried saliva snapped between his lips.

“What... what happened to the tavern?” Marek stammered, staring at the concrete-faced blockhouses. “Where... where are the whores?”

“Marek...” Clara whimpered, clutching his rusty elbow in sudden, instinctual terror. “Look at the road.”

Two hundred yards ahead, where the mountain trail met the valley floor, sat a fortified concrete-and-timber redoubt:

*State Security Checkpoint No. 4.*

Two iron gates barred the thoroughfare, flanked by barbed timber abatis. Above the portal, a stark black sign with painted white runes declared:

> **ORDENSSTAAT KARST — SECTOR 4**
> **UNAUTHORIZED TRANSIT STRICTLY PROHIBITED**
> **PRESENT IDENTITY BOOKLETS AND CARBOLIC PASSES**
> **VIOLATORS WILL BE PROCESSED INTO LABOR COHORT BETA**

Beneath the sign stood twelve men in matching black coats, steel breastplates, and polished iron kettle-helms. They held heavy steel crossbows at the low ready.

Beside them, tethered by iron chains, were two massive, muscular wolf-hounds with clipped ears and spiked leather collars, their breath misting in the cold air, their red eyes fixed intently on the four ragged figures stumbling down the path.

Marek swallowed hard, his Adam's apple bobbing in his throat.

He looked down at his rusted armor. He looked at his notched, firewood-chopping greatsword. He thought of his empty belly, his lack of coin, and the two weeks of turnip-soup dreams he had entertained on the trail.

He forced a trembling, arrogant smirk onto his face, raised his chin, and flicked his matted hair.

“Don’t worry, girls,” Marek announced, his voice cracking slightly on the high notes. “It’s just a frontier levy. Some local warlord must have taken over the bailiff's office. Once I show them the blade of *Dawn-Sunderer* and give them my guild credentials, they’ll fall to their knees.”

He took a heavy, squelching step forward into the mud.

“Follow me,” Marek declared. “Let’s go claim our dinner.”
