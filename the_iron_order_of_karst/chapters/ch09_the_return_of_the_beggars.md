# Chapter 9: The Return of the Beggars

A party of fantasy adventurers without an unpaid pack-mule is an administrative corpse waiting for burial.

For three years, the Silver Dawn had functioned under the delusion of self-sufficiency. In their minds, their glory stemmed from Marek’s gilded bicep, Clara’s pious morning prayers, Isolde’s theatrical fire chants, and Brigit’s silent scouting. In their minds, Lorenz Falk was merely an awkward shadow who walked behind them carrying the pots.

They had forgotten who oiled the leather harness. They had forgotten who boiled the drinking water to prevent dysentery. They had forgotten who walked five miles into the dark to gather dry pine needles for kindling, who patched Marek’s shredded gambeson, who bargained with extortionist innkeepers, and who stayed awake in freezing rain so they could slumber in heated silk.

Three months without their pack-mule had extracted a savage mathematical toll.

The mountain track descending from the northern crags into the Karst Basin was a trench of grey slush, rotting pine needles, and freezing mud. Through this mire crawled the remnants of the Silver Dawn.

The stench preceded them by fifty paces: a rancid miasma of unwashed bodies, gangrenous saddle sores, damp moldering wool, and the sour reek of turnip mash gone rotten in an unwashed kettle.

Marek the Gilded walked at the vanguard, his glorious swagger reduced to a painful, splay-footed limp. The polished mirror-finish of his gilded plate armor was gone, replaced by a greasy crust of dried swamp mud, troll bile, and red rust along the joints. The five-foot greatsword *Dawn-Sunderer* was strapped across his back, its scabbard frayed, its pommel dented from having been used to crack stubborn walnuts. His flowing chestnut curls hung in greasy, tangled hanks caked with wood-ash and lice.

Behind him stumbled Clara of the Weeping Spring. Her pristine cleric’s habit of white samite and silver lace was torn to ribbons, dragging in the black peat mud. Her silk slippers had rotted away six weeks prior; she now wore strips of raw wolf-hide bound to her blistered feet with dirty hemp cord. Her pale cheeks were sunken from malnutrition, smeared with charcoal, and her hands trembled uncontrollably around a cracked prayer focus.

Beside her limped Isolde the Pyromancer. Her embroidered velvet robes were scorched along the hem from her own misfired cantrips. Her satchel of arcane reagents had been ruined during a river crossing two weeks into their expedition; she now carried four dried leaves and a handful of sulfurous mud in an unwashed tin cup. Her lips were cracked, bleeding from cold and salt deficiency.

Bringing up the rear was Brigit the Wood-Stalker. Her leather brigandine was missing half its copper rivets. Her eyes, sunken beneath dark circles, darted warily into the mist. Her yew recurve bow was unstrung, and her leather quiver contained exactly three splintered ash arrows.

“Are we... are we almost there, Marek?” Clara whimpered, her voice cracking as she stumbled over an exposed pine root. She dropped to her knees in the freezing slush, sobbing into her filthy palms. “My ankles are swollen... I can’t walk another mile. Please, Marek. Let us stop and make camp.”

Marek paused, leaning heavily on a dead spruce branch that snapped beneath his weight. He grimaced, rubbing his inflamed knee joint, and glared back at her.

“Stop and camp with what, Clara?” Marek snapped, his voice hoarse and stripped of courtly charm. “We have no tent. The guy-wires were cut by whatever mountain goblin raided our camp on Raven’s Gap three months ago. We have no salt pork. We have no dry kindling. We keep walking until we reach Karst-Hold!”

“You said the dungeon would be easy!” Isolde rasped, coughing violently into her sleeve. “You promised a hoard of ancient elven silver! We spent six weeks trapped in a flooded basalt tomb eating blind salamanders! My mana is depleted! My fingers are frozen! And what did we get?”

Marek’s bloodshot eyes flashed with sudden, desperate vanity. He reached into his leather tunic and pulled out an object wrapped in a torn strip of grease-soaked oilcloth.

He unfolded the cloth. Resting in his blackened palm was an irregular, fist-sized crystal of crystalline amber, pulsing with a deep, internal vermilion light: the *Wyrm-Heart Crystal*.

“We got this,” Marek snarled, brandishing the stone before their exhausted faces. “Do you understand the value of this relic, you stupid cows? This is the crystallized heart of an ancient swamp-wyrm! The royal guild masters in the capital will pay ten thousand gold florins for this stone alone! With this crystal, I am not merely an adventurer—I am a lord!”

He turned, facing the direction of the southern pass, his chest heaving with delusional pride.

“Karst-Hold is a miserable frontier cesspool,” Marek boasted, flipping a greasy lock of hair from his brow. “A drunken sergeant, twenty peasant levies with rusted pitchforks, and a few dozen turnip farmers. When I march through their gate with *Dawn-Sunderer* and this wyrm-heart, that drunken old fool Kester will drop to his knees. We’ll take the bailiff’s manor. We’ll drink their cellar dry. We’ll commandeer their finest feather beds and have their maidens wash my boots!”

Brigit stopped on the ridge, her sharp eyes squinting through the shifting veil of freezing mountain mist.

“Marek,” Brigit said quietly. Her voice was flat, devoid of emotion.

“What is it, elf-friend?” Marek sneered.

“Look at the sky,” Brigit said, pointing her dirt-caked finger down into the basin.

The clouds above the Karst Basin were unusual. They were not the soft, pale white mists of an untamed frontier.

Thick, towering plumes of dense, oily black and charcoal-grey smoke rose into the overcast sky from five distinct masonry stacks. The horizon was illuminated by a fierce, continuous orange glow that pulsed with industrial heat.

And then, through the freezing mountain air, the sound reached them.

It was an acoustic phenomenon none of them had ever encountered in all their years of wandering.

*THUD... THUD... THUD...*

The mathematical, rhythmic cadence of massive steam-driven forging hammers striking tons of white-hot steel vibrated through the limestone bedrock beneath their boots.

Suddenly, a sharp, supersonic crack echoed off the dolomite cliffs three miles to the south, followed by an earth-shattering detonation that shook dead pine cones from the branches above their heads.

*CRACK... KA-BOOM.*

Clara shrieked, clamping her hands over her ears and throwing herself into the mud. Isolde gasped, dropping her reagent cup into the snow.

“What was that?!” Clara wailed, trembling violently. “Is it a behemoth?! A demon-lord?! An earthquake?!”

“A beast operates with biological irregularity,” Brigit observed with grim clarity, her eyes wide as she studied the valley floor. “Listen.”

Across the valley floor, a terrifying new sound cut through the drizzle: a sustained, mechanical drone that rose into a high-pitched, metallic howl—the test running of a six-cylinder supercharged internal combustion aero-engine on an outdoor test bench.

Marek stood frozen on the edge of the limestone outcrop.

Down in the basin, the landscape he remembered had ceased to exist.

The squalid timber palisades of Karst-Hold were gone. In their place stood towering bastion walls of reinforced concrete, studded with steel-shuttered embrasures and searchlight towers. Paved asphalt and limestone roads cut straight geometric lines through the forest scrub. Along narrow-gauge iron rails, small steam locomotives hauled long trains of four-wheeled steel carts filled with coal and crushed iron ore.

Above the central fortress, snapping stiffly in the icy gale, hung an enormous black banner bearing the stark, geometric emblem of the twelve-spoked iron wheel: the Black Sun.

“What... what happened here?” Marek stammered, his jaw slack, his grip on the wyrm-heart loosening. “Did the High Crown send an imperial legion? Did an archmage conquer the valley?”

“The banners display an iron wheel,” Brigit said, her jaw tight. “And those roads... those walls... no mason constructs walls of smooth grey stone without mortar seams.”

Marek swallowed hard, then forced a loud, booming bark of laughter that rang hollow against the dolomite rocks.

“Hah! So some petty warlord built a few stone kilns and hired a company of dwarven blacksmiths! What of it?” Marek slammed his greatsword against his rusted breastplate. “A blacksmith is still a peasant! An artisan still bows to an S-Rank hero! We have the Wyrm-Heart! We have *Dawn-Sunderer*! When they see an S-Rank vanguard arrive at their gate, they’ll offer us their treasury just to hire our swords!”

He turned to the shivering women, his teeth bared in a desperate, ferocious grin.

“Form up! Shoulders back, Clara! Stop crying, Isolde! Walk behind me! We are the Silver Dawn! We take what we want!”

Marek turned and marched down the slope toward State Checkpoint 4, entirely unaware that the world of swords and heroes had died ninety days ago.
