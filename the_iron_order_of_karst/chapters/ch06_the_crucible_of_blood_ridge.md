# Chapter 6: The Crucible of Blood-Ridge

Feudal warfare is an embarrassing, inefficient display of individual vanity.

A feudal warlord assembles four hundred men as a chaotic horde of armed narcissists. Each rider wears whatever armor he managed to steal, carries weapons of irregular caliber, and charges across unsurveyed terrain to shout his lineage into the wind.

Against such biological incompetence, a modern defensive doctrine operates as an abattoir.

The terrain at Blood-Ridge had been prepared over seven consecutive days by Labor Cohorts Alpha and Beta. It was a narrow, marshy bottleneck between two sheer limestone bluffs, through which the southern mountain road was forced to pass.

To the naked eye of an undisciplined scout, the pass appeared lightly held: an unfinished earthen berm, a handful of flickering watchfires, and forty men in grey wool tunics standing awkwardly behind a low timber rail.

What the naked eye could not perceive—because it lay submerged beneath eighteen inches of stagnant marsh-water and peat moss—was a network of interlocking, graduated geometric hazards:
1. Three thousand four-pronged iron caltrops, manufactured from the melted remains of Gunter’s confiscated carriage springs.
2. Two concealed, four-foot-deep anti-cavalry drainage trenches filled with sharpened, fire-hardened pine stakes.
3. Four pre-sighted crossfire firing lanes, mathematically marked with white limestone boundary stones at seventy-five, fifty, and twenty-five meters.

At 0615 hours, beneath a sky the color of a bruised lead plate, Warlord Vorn arrived.

He brought four hundred men. Half were nomadic steppe raiders riding shaggy highland ponies, armed with curved horn bows and bone-handled sabers; the remainder were mountain reavers, deserters from the southern civil wars, wrapped in wolf pelts and rusted iron mail, brandishing two-handed battle-axes and boar-spears.

At their center rode Vorn himself: a towering barbarian chieftain with a shaved scalp covered in ritual blue woad tattoos, wearing a heavy lamellar coat of boiled buffalo-hide and riding a massive, snorting red mountain stallion.

Neglecting skirmishers and flank security, Vorn focused entirely on the small, scrawny figure in an oiled black storm-coat standing atop the earthen berm, unleashing a booming, chest-rattling roar that echoed off the dolomite bluffs.

“Is this the little clerk who locked Horgan in a ditch?!” Vorn roared, brandishing a massive iron-headed war-pick in a wide, sweeping arc. “Is this the dog who thinks he can tax the blood of the mountain?!”

His four hundred warriors howled like a pack of rabid hounds, banging their iron-banded shields and brandishing axes.

I stood atop the central command revetment, holding a brass field telescope in my left hand. At my right stood Sergeant Kester, whose fingers were wrapped around the wooden frame of a signal paddle painted half-black, half-white. Behind the earthen berm, one hundred and twenty men of the newly christened *Karst Frontier Guard* crouched in three ranks: front rank kneeling with sixteen-foot ash-wood pikes; rear ranks cocked with heavy steel-strung crossbows.

“Wind velocity: four knots, west-northwest,” I observed calmly through the lens. “Precipitation: freezing drizzle, negligible ballistic drag. The adversary’s cavalry is bunched at a density of three mounts per meter. An optimal target profile.”

Kester swallowed, his knuckles white. “They’re... they’re charging, Commissary! Look at the red horse! That’s Vorn!”

Across the marsh, Vorn dropped his war-pick toward the pass.

“Ride them down!” Vorn bellowed. “Trample the ditch! Split the clerk from his crown to his codpiece! Plunder to the swiftest!”

With a thunderous, earth-shaking roar, two hundred horsemen kicked their mounts into a gallop.

They came on in a dense, chaotic wedge, hooves churning the grey mud, screaming blood-curses, their curved sabers glinting wetly in the gloom. It was a spectacle intended to shatter the nerve of green feudal conscripts, to cause them to drop their spears and flee in screaming terror.

My men did not flee.

They did not flee because they were terrified of something infinitely worse than four hundred screaming barbarians: they were terrified of my administrative penal code.

Every soldier in that trench knew that if he abandoned his station, Director Weidmann’s Black Cloaks would arrest his entire family before sundown, strip them of their ration cards, and assign them to the sulfur pits for three generations. Between the uncertain death of an enemy charge and the mathematical, bureaucratic certainty of state annihilation, the human mind instinctively chooses to hold the line.

“Hold,” I murmured, checking the white limestone marker at seventy-five meters.

The thundering wall of horses surged past the eighty-meter mark. Mud sprayed ten feet into the air. The screams of the riders grew deafening; I could smell the sour grease of their wolf-skins and the hot, rancid breath of the galloping stallions.

Sixty meters.

Fifty meters.

The leading horses struck the submerged caltrop field.

The effect was immediate and catastrophic.

Steel caltrops, four inches long and razor-sharp, pierced the soft frogs of the horses' hooves with sickening crunches. Mounts shrieked, their legs collapsing out from under them at full gallop. Horses tumbled end-over-end, cartwheeling into the frozen mud, snapping forelegs, throwing riders face-first into the concealed stake-ditches.

The charging wedge disintegrated in three seconds. The rear ranks plowed into the flailing, broken mass of front-rank horses, creating a squirming, screaming pile of crushed bone and thrashing hooves.

“Signal,” I said with steady calm.

Kester raised the black paddle.

*FWEET.*

“Section One—loose!”

Sixty steel crossbows discharged as a single instrument.

*THWUMP.*

At forty meters against a stationary, tangled mass of unarmored horsemen, the six-ounce steel quarrels tore through flesh and bone like industrial hole-punches. Men were pinned to their dead horses; lamellar hide coats were pierced clean through to the spine. A spray of dark arterial mist hung over the mud like a crimson cloud.

“Section One—crank and reload!” Kester’s voice cracked with grim, mechanical rhythm. “Section Two—step and present! Loose!”

*THWUMP.*

Another sixty bolts slammed into the slaughter-pen. The reavers screamed in terror, scrambling backward on their hands and knees, their glorious charge reduced to a butcher’s floor.

Vorn’s red stallion had gone down in the first ditch, its chest impaled on two pine stakes. Vorn, miraculously thrown clear, scrambled to his feet in the sucking mud. His helmet was gone, his blue woad tattoos smeared with black peat and horse gore, his war-pick lost in the mire.

He looked around in frantic, horrified disbelief.

Over two hundred of his elite raiders were dead or thrashing in the caltrops. The surviving mountain infantry, having witnessed the total annihilation of their cavalry in under three minutes without striking a single blow, dropped their axes and fled in screaming panic back toward the mountain defile.

“Pikes forward,” I commanded.

Kester flipped the paddle to the white face.

The front rank of sixty conscripts rose from behind the earthen berm. Sixty sixteen-foot steel-tipped ash pikes snapped down in unison, locking into an impenetrable wall of grey steel points.

*THUD-THUD-THUD.*

The company advanced with locked, synchronized strides, boots slamming into the mud in perfect cadence.

*“Halt!”*

The pike wall stopped ten feet from where Warlord Vorn stood knee-deep in the marsh, his chest heaving, clutching a broken iron dagger.

“You... you coward!” Vorn snarled, his voice trembling with exhausted, manic rage. “You hide behind dirt and iron thorns! Face me! Draw steel like a man, you scrawny rat!”

I walked down the wooden ramp of the revetment, stepping around the puddles of muddy water, until I stood behind the central pike tip.

I adjusted the collar of my oiled storm-coat and pulled my brass pocket notebook from my breast.

“Personal combat between operational commanders is a primitive barbarism,” I said, looking at him with clinical dispassion. “A general who draws a sword has already confessed to an intellectual failure of logistics.”

“I’ll tear your throat out with my teeth!” Vorn roared, lunging forward.

*CRACK.*

Corporal Brandt swung his heavy oak riot truncheon from the side, striking Vorn squarely across the back of his right knee. The joint buckled with a sickening pop. Vorn crashed to his knees in the mud with a strangled gasp of agony.

Two Black Cloaks immediately dropped a heavy, iron-weighted hemp net over his broad shoulders, pinning his arms to his torso. Within six seconds, forged iron manacles were clamped around his thick wrists.

I stepped forward, looking down at the kneeling warlord.

“Name: Vorn. Biological classification: Sub-regional paramilitary chieftain,” I recited, jotting a notation into the ledger. “Crimes: Class-1 Armed Rebellion, Unauthorized Deployment of Paramilitary Biomass, Highway Obstruction, and Destructive Waste of Horse Flesh.”

Vorn spat a mouthful of bloody bile toward my boots. “Kill me, then. Send me to the halls of my fathers!”

“Execution?” I tilted my head, genuinely offended by the suggestion. “A man of your bone density and muscle mass represents at least four thousand kilocalories of potential work output per day. You will not die. You are hereby cataloged as *Labor Asset #001-A*.”

I tapped my pencil against his shaved, blood-spattered scalp.

“The deep anthracite coal adit beneath Sector Gamma requires an overseer capable of dragging two-hundred-pound slag carts through unventilated shafts. You have been selected for the position. Your shifts will be fourteen hours. Your bread ration will be pegged directly to metric tonnage extracted.”

Vorn stared at me, his eyes wide with a terror deeper than any threat of execution. In a warrior's culture, death in battle is an honorable release; eternal, unglamorous, bureaucratic enslavement in a dark hole is an existential nightmare.

“You’re... you’re not a man,” Vorn whimpered, his voice breaking. “You’re... you’re a devil in a black coat.”

“Incorrect,” I replied, closing the notebook. “I am an administrator.”

I turned back to the ramparts, looking out over the blood-soaked marsh of Blood-Ridge.

Across the entire northern basin, the smoke of chaotic feudalism was clearing. The bandits were broken. The robber-barons were chained in the quarries. The borders were sealed.

I looked up at the central watchtower, where Sergeant Kester and his men were hoisting a massive, circular iron standard into the grey sky:

A stark, twelve-spoked geometric wheel forged of black pig-iron.

To the superstitious peasantry, it would be known as the Black Sun of Karst. To me, it was the only thing that mattered in a broken, miserable universe:

The beginning of the *Ordensstaat*.

“Sergeant Kester,” I called out, my voice carrying over the silent battlefield.

“Commissary!”

“Clear the road. Strip the iron from the dead horses. And inform Director Weidmann to prepare three hundred additional registration dossiers.”

I pulled my watch-less left sleeve back, checked my wrist by habit, and turned toward the fortress.

“The pass is secured,” I said. “Now, we begin the industrialization of the valley.”
