# Chapter 5: The Black Directorate

Order without surveillance is merely temporary luck.

The human animal, when left unmonitored for more than seventy-two consecutive hours, exhibits an innate thermodynamic tendency toward entropy: skimming grain, hoarding copper coins beneath floorboards, arriving six minutes late to shovel details, and complaining about the lack of fermented fruit in the commissary mess.

To prevent this civilizational rot, the state requires eyes. It requires ears. Above all, it requires small, bitter men who find profound spiritual fulfillment in categorizing the moral failings of their neighbors into alphabetical pigeonholes.

I found such an instrument in the cellar of the former bailiff's residence: Master Notary Weidmann.

Weidmann was fifty-four years of age, possessed a spine curved like a sickle from forty winters of hunched scribal labor, and wore thick, round brass-framed spectacles that magnified his watery, rheumy eyes into those of an inquisitive toad. The previous feudal authorities had dismissed him for "excessive pedantry and unchivalrous accounting"—specifically, he had refused to certify the Duke's forged hunting expense ledgers.

When I entered the converted vaulted cellar—now designated the *Central Administrative Archive*—Weidmann was sitting beneath a flickering whale-oil lamp, meticulously organizing three thousand index slips of parchment using an index-tab system I had sketched on a napkin forty-eight hours prior.

The room was damp, smelling of vinegar-washed sheepskin vellum, lamp oil, and the dry, powdery dust of ground gall-nut ink. Twelve clerks, recruited from literate conscripts and elderly former weavers, sat at two long trestle tables, writing in complete silence. The only sound was the synchronized *scratch-scratch-scratch* of iron nibs biting into rough parchment.

“Director Weidmann,” I said, stepping onto the flagstones.

Weidmann did not jump. He completed the stroke of a capital *D*, carefully set his pen upon an iron rest, blotted the line with powdered slate, and rose with agonizing slowness. He smoothed the lapels of his dyed-black wool coat—the standardized uniform of the newly established *Sicherheits-Direktorat*—and bowed at a precise fifteen-degree angle.

“Supreme Commissary,” Weidmann squeaked, his voice like dry vellum rasping against slate. “The morning postal sumps have been emptied. Twenty-eight submissions received from the four iron boxes installed across Sectors Alpha, Beta, and Gamma.”

“Summarize the intelligence harvest,” I ordered.

Weidmann picked up a brass-clipped sheaf of reports, squinting through his thick lenses with an expression that bordered on erotic ecstasy.

“Fourteen reports of minor domestic insubordination,” Weidmann read. “Including an elderly washerwoman who accused her neighbor of drying laundry on an unauthorized communal fence-rail during high-wind hours. Six reports of unauthorized card games behind the charcoal kilns—culprits have already been sentenced to three days latrine-scouring per Section 4.”

“And the strategic concerns?”

Weidmann’s posture stiffened. He adjusted his spectacles, his watery eyes narrowing.

“A commercial anomaly in Sector Beta, Commissary. Specifically: the salt ledger.”

I tapped my index finger twice against my collar seam. “Elaborate.”

“Citizen Gunter,” Weidmann purred, his thin lips curling into a cruel, bureaucratic sneer. “Former merchant guildsman from the valley. Operates the dry-goods commissary concession near the south ditch. Over the past seventy-two hours, four separate informants—a baker's apprentice, two lime-shoveler wives, and a crippled teamster—noted that Gunter claimed a 'severe regional shortage' of coarse sea-salt. He refused to accept standard State Labor Scrip, demanding unclipped silver coin, and increased the exchange rate from two scrip per pound to eight silver groschen.”

“Price-gouging,” I said coldly. “Hoarding strategic mineral assets during active defensive consolidation. A textbook capitalist parasitic subversion.”

“Worse, Commissary,” Weidmann whispered, leaning forward over his pine desk. “At 0230 hours last night, an operative from the Third Washerwoman Network observed two covered carts arriving at Gunter's private storehouse behind the old granary. They unloaded eighteen wooden casks. The markings on the staves indicate refined rock-salt from the southern mines. There is no shortage. The supply is intact. He is manufacturing an artificial famine to corner liquid bullion before winter.”

I nodded slowly. The predictability of the merchant class was almost comforting in its banality. Whenever chaos loosens its grip, the petty bourgeois emerges from the rubble, seeking to convert the blood of working men into private gold.

“Director Weidmann,” I said.

“Sir!”

“Dispatch Section Four of the Black Cloaks. Seize the storehouse. Confiscate all salt, grain, and ledger assets into the Sovereign Strategic Reserve. Citizen Gunter is to be arrested immediately and brought to the Central Assembly Yard.”

“And the formal charge, Commissary?” Weidmann asked, his quill already hovering over a fresh arrest warrant.

“Class-1 Economic Sabotage. Aggravated Treason against the General Welfare. Conspiracy to undermine State Price Parity.”

“A delightful phrasing, sir,” Weidmann whispered, signing the warrant with a flourishing slash of black ink. “I shall have him in chains before the noon bell.”

At 1215 hours, the Central Assembly Yard was packed with eight hundred citizens.

The crowd stood in neat, rectangular blocks by labor cohort: quarry workers in grey canvas, ditch-diggers in brown hemp, timber-fallers in sheepskin, and women from the weaving workshops. They were silent, their breath rising like grey smoke into the overcast sky.

In the center of the yard, upon an elevated timber platform beneath the newly raised Twelve-Spoked Iron Wheel, knelt Citizen Gunter.

He was a stout, soft-bellied man with jowls like a bloodhound, wearing a fine woolen tunic dyed forest green—a garish, obscene splash of color amidst the uniform grey and black of the *Ordensstaat*. Heavy iron manacles bound his plump wrists; his cheeks were streaked with tears and snot.

Beside him stood Sergeant Kester, flanked by two towering Black Cloaks armed with four-foot ash-wood truncheons.

At the edge of the platform sat my pine desk.

I stood behind it, holding Gunter’s personal ledger in my left hand. The wind whipped the skirts of my oiled storm-coat against my boots.

“Citizen Gunter,” I addressed the kneeling merchant. The silence across the yard was absolute. “The Directorate has audited your commercial accounts. In your subterranean cellar, hidden beneath three layers of rotting straw, my officers recovered eighteen casks of pure rock-salt—two thousand one hundred and sixty pounds of essential biological sustenance.”

Gunter sobbed, raising his chained hands in a desperate, trembling prayer.

“Commissary! Lord Vogel! It was an investment! I paid good silver for that salt in the south! An honest merchant has the right to set his price! Supply and demand, my Lord! The market must be free! By the laws of the King of Osterlund—”

“The King of Osterlund,” I interrupted, my voice cutting through his squeals like an iron cleaver, “is three hundred miles away, drinking spiced wine in a marble hall while this frontier starves. The King’s laws permit bandits to slit your children's throats and allow merchants to starve their neighbors for silver florins. Those laws are null and void within this sector.”

I looked out across the sea of eight hundred silent faces.

“Salt is not a commodity for private speculation,” I projected my voice across the assembly. “Salt is cellular electrolyte regulation. Without salt, labor productivity drops twenty-eight percent within fourteen days. Without salt, muscle cramping incapacitates the ditch-brigades. Without salt, winter pork stores putrefy in their casks.”

I pointed a gloved finger at Gunter’s green tunic.

“This man attempted to hoard the health of your children to extract sixteen ounces of silver from your pockets. He wished to see your rations cut so that he might wear dyed wool and sleep upon feather mattresses while you break dolomite in the rain.”

A low, guttural growl rippled through the assembled workers.

The ditch-diggers gripped their pick-handles; the quarrymen leaned forward, their scarred knuckles whitening. In a feudal realm, this merchant would have bribed the bailiff with three casks of salt and walked free while the peasants starved in the ditches.

“Is he to be hung, Commissary?!” a voice shouted from the front rank—one of the Weeping Oak refugees. “String him up from the gate!”

“Feed him to the lime pits!” another screamed.

The crowd surged forward, an ugly, violent wave of collective rage.

*WHISTLE.*

Kester blew his brass whistle. Twelve Black Cloaks stepped forward on the parapets, leveling heavy steel crossbows with cold, mechanical indifference.

The crowd instantly froze, retreating three paces in disciplined terror.

“Silence,” I commanded.

The yard fell dead quiet once more.

“The *Ordensstaat* rejects spontaneous mob lynchings,” I stated coldly. “Vigilante violence squanders human energy through emotional chaos. True justice restores structural and economic equilibrium.”

I turned back to the trembling merchant.

“Citizen Gunter. The eighteen casks of rock-salt are permanently confiscated into the Central Commissary. They will be distributed to every registered household at the fixed statutory rate of two *Arbeitschein* per pound.”

A collective gasp swept through the crowd, followed by stunned, disbelieving murmurs of relief.

“Your green wool tunic, your silver coins, and your private warehouse are hereby annexed by the State. However, because physical liquidation destroys potential caloric labor value, and because gratuitous cruelty is an insult to administrative science, your life is spared.”

Gunter looked up, tears of pathetic gratitude streaming down his fat jowls. “Thank you! Oh, gods bless you, Commissary! Thank you—!”

“Do not thank me,” I said flatly. “Sentence: Fifteen months of compulsory labor in the Quicklime Slaking Pit. You will shovel raw calcium oxide into the water vats ten hours a day. Because blinded or incapacitated laborers produce zero metric tons of lime, you will be issued protective horn-rimmed goggles, heavy rawhide mittens, and a vinegar-soaked linen respirator. You will receive two thousand calories of clean rye broth and cabbage daily. If you meet your daily hydration quota without complaint, your debt to society will be fully discharged in fifteen months, and you may re-apply for a commercial retail license as a regulated state factor.”

My gaze pinned him to the timber platform.

“*However.* Your crime represents a conscious, deliberate conspiracy to starve working families for private silver. If you hoard so much as an ounce of state lime, if you attempt to bribe an overseer, or if you subvert the public ledger—the stick will fall without a second chance, and your smock will be buried beneath the foundation stones.”

Gunter’s face went pale, but he swallowed his tears, nodding frantically. He had expected to be blinded or flayed alive by a mob; he was being offered safety gear, clean broth, and an exact, statutory path back to civil life.

“Understood! I will shovel, Commissary! By the Dawn, I will shovel!”

“Sergeant Kester. Escort the citizen to Equipment Fitting.”

“Aye, Commissary!” Kester barked, guiding Gunter down the stairs with firm, professional hands.

I stepped to the front of the platform, looking down at the eight hundred workers.

They stared with a terrifying mixture of absolute dread and fanatical reverence. To these battered laborers, my sentence had struck down their oppressor with the unbending hand of divine retribution.

They loved the machine because the machine crushed the petty parasites who had bled them for decades.

They did not realize that the machine now owned their bodies, their calories, their waking hours, and their very thoughts.

Director Weidmann stepped up beside me, clutching his black leather portfolio, his brass spectacles glinting in the pale, sickly light of the overcast sun.

“A magnificent demonstration of public pedagogy, Supreme Commissary,” Weidmann murmured obsequiously. “Denunciation submissions will likely increase by three hundred percent before nightfall.”

“Ensure the collection boxes are emptied twice daily, Director,” I said, turning back toward my office. “And double the guard on the eastern perimeter road.”

“An imminent threat, sir?”

“Warlord Vorn has not received his monthly extortion tribute from the Red Kiln gang,” I said, glancing at the dark, jagged horizon of Blood-Ridge. “His scouts will be arriving within forty-eight hours. It is time to test our pike-squares.”
