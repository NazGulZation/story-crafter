# Chapter 11: The Processing Cells

The process of de-romanticization begins with hot water, lye, and an iron comb.

When an individual is stripped of velvet ribbons, perfumed body oils, and aristocratic illusions, they revert to their foundational biological reality: approximately sixty-five kilograms of carbon, water, and nitrogen, currently hosting an unacceptable population of body lice.

In Quarantine Block C, this reduction was executed with industrial thoroughness.

Clara of the Weeping Spring stood shivering on the cold, wet slate floor of the disinfection chamber. High-pressure hoses, pumping near-boiling water mixed with pine-tar and carbolic acid, had blasted eighty days of mountain filth from her skin in four agonizing minutes. Two female orderlies in rubber aprons and oilskin masks had scrubbed her from scalp to heel with stiff horsehair brushes, leaving her pale skin raw, stinging, and the color of boiled shrimp.

Her matted golden hair had been sheared to the scalp with sheep-shears to eliminate typhus vectors; her lavender-scented silk chemise had been tossed into an incinerator chute before her very eyes, officially categorized as *Contraband Biological Waste*.

Now, she stood clutching a coarse, scratchy smock of undyed grey hemp around her trembling frame, her feet shoved into unpadded wooden clogs. Beside her, Isolde and Brigit were in an identical state of shaved, shivering, sanitized humiliation.

“Move,” a Black Cloak guard barked, tapping his ash baton against the slate doorframe.

Clara shuffled forward, her wooden clogs *clack-clacking* down the damp corridor.

She was weeping silently, her hands trembling so violently she could barely hold the hem of her smock. But beneath her terror, a frantic, desperate hope was taking root in her chest.

During the intake processing, she had overheard the orderlies whispering:
*“The Supreme Commander personally ordered the dossier on the four mountain vagrants.”*
*“Aye... he’s waiting in Interrogation Room 1.”*

Clara’s heart pounded against her ribs.

*A commander!* A man of supreme power, ruling this vast, terrifying city of iron and smoke!

She had seen powerful lords before. In the capital, lords were proud, vain, and susceptible to the tears of a beautiful woman. If she threw herself at his feet, wept convincingly, and blamed everything on Marek’s brutal coercion—if she whispered that her virtue had been stolen by force—the Commander would surely show mercy. Perhaps he would even be captivated by her delicate features, despite her shaved head, and elevate her to his private chambers!

The heavy iron door of Interrogation Room 1 swung open with a hydraulic hiss.

“Prisoners halt,” the guard commanded.

Clara stepped across the threshold, blinked against the blinding glare of a magnesium-oil lantern mounted directly above an unpainted iron table, and looked at the man sitting behind it.

The breath vanished from her lungs.

Her knees turned to water. She grabbed the edge of the iron table to keep from collapsing onto the floor.

“L-Lorenz...?” she gasped, her voice barely a strangled squeak.

Sitting behind the desk was Lorenz Falk.

Yet it was not Lorenz.

The scrawny, trembling boy who had carried her baggage, wept when she ignored him, and stood shivering in the rain was gone.

The man before her sat with a spinal posture so unnaturally, terrifyingly rigid it looked cast from liquid steel. He wore a tailored high-collar tunic of charcoal wool, fastened to the throat with silver-plated iron buttons stamped with the Twelve-Spoked Wheel. His hair was cut high and tight, severe and angular. His scrawny cheeks had filled out with disciplined bone and muscle; his skin, though pale, had the cold, granite clarity of an ascetic zealot.

Most terrifying of all were his eyes.

There was no warmth in them. There was no longing, no resentment, no wounded pride, and no recognition.

They were the eyes of a tax auditor inspecting an invoice for gravel.

“Lorenz!” Clara wailed, tears bursting from her eyes as she lunged forward, throwing her hands onto the iron desk. “Oh, gods! Lorenz! It’s you! You’re alive! You’re the Commander! Oh, thank the heavens! Tell these monsters to take these wooden shoes off me! They cut my hair! They burned my silk dress! They beat Marek into the mud—!”

I did not flinch. I did not lean back.

I completed the stroke of a capital *P* in my ledger, blotted the line with powdered slate, laid the iron pen upon its brass rest, and looked up.

My gaze traveled from her shaved, reddened scalp to her raw collarbones, down to the coarse grey hemp smock, and finally rested upon her trembling, blistered fingers clutching the edge of my desk.

“Contact with official state administrative furniture without authorization is an infraction under Subsection 4,” I stated. My voice was completely flat, a low, mechanical monotone that carried the temperature of an ice floe. “Remove your hands from the iron, Prisoner 804.”

Clara froze. Her mouth fell open, her tears hanging suspended on her raw cheeks.

“L-Lorenz...? Don’t you... don’t you know who I am? It’s Clara! Your Clara! Your childhood friend from the village!”

“Prisoner Falk, Lorenz,” I recited with clinical dispassion, “is legally deceased per Municipal Record 01-A, dated eighty-six days prior. Cause of death: systemic cardiovascular collapse resulting from hypothermia, malnutrition, and gross dereliction of sentry duty in Raven's Gap.”

I tapped my index finger twice against my collar seam.

“You are currently speaking with the Supreme Directorate of the *Ordensstaat Karst*. In this office, biological nostalgia does not constitute legal tender. Take two paces backward and assume the standard position of interrogation compliance: feet together, hands behind the back, chin level.”

Clara gasped, stumbling backward two paces as if struck by a concussive shockwave.

Behind her, Isolde and Brigit stood paralyzed. Isolde’s eyes were wide with a terror so profound she appeared unable to draw breath. She stared at my uniform, at the silver collar-tabs, at the iron wheel sigil on the wall, and the reality of the past eighty-six days crashed down upon her like a collapsing mine shaft.

*The vanished wagon.*
*The cut guy-wires.*
*The stolen mule.*
*The missing strongbox.*

“It was you,” Isolde whispered, her voice cracking into a dry, breathless croak. “That night... at Raven’s Gap... you didn't die. You took everything. You left us on the mountain to freeze.”

“Incorrect,” I replied calmly. “I did not 'take everything.' I conducted an emergency strategic requisition of state-actionable logistics. The ninety pounds of rye flour, the forty-five pounds of cured pork, and the seventy-four silver florins in that cart were the legal property of the bearer of the Chancery Seal. You were four non-productive combat dependents engaging in unauthorized recreational copulation during an active reconnaissance watch. To leave strategic assets in the custody of such personnel would have constituted gross administrative negligence.”

“Non-productive... dependents?!” Clara shrieked, clutching her face with her hands, her voice rising to a hysterical, shrill pitch. “I was your friend! I grew up with you! You said you loved me! You promised you would build a house for us in the valley! You spent three years saving coin for my silver focus! How can you look at me like this?! How can you be so cruel?!”

I pulled a thick, brass-bound folder from the stack on my left and flipped it open.

“Let us review the historical audit,” I said, looking down at the parchment. “Subject: Clara of the Weeping Spring.
Between Year 1 and Year 4 of the 'Silver Dawn' enterprise, the deceased host organism Lorenz Falk expended the following liquid capital upon your maintenance:
- Temple tuition fees: four hundred and twelve silver florins.
- Consecrated vestments and silver focus: one hundred and eighty silver florins.
- Specialized caloric sustenance, including refined white sugar, candied almonds, and imported rose-scented bath unguents: two hundred and forty florins.
- Total capital investment: eight hundred and thirty-two silver florins.”

I turned the page with a sharp, dry snap.

“In return for this capital expenditure, your recorded economic output across four years is as follows:
- Minor superficial wound dressings applied: fourteen.
- Holy blessings administered to healthy individuals: thirty-six.
- Net agricultural production: zero kilograms.
- Net industrial or metallurgical production: zero kilograms.
- Capital return on investment: negative one hundred percent.”

I closed the folder and looked directly into her horrified, uncomprehending eyes.

“You believe this is about wounded pride,” I said, my voice level and cold as a frozen lake. “It is not. I am a pragmatist, Clara. Cruelty for cruelty’s sake is the vice of a weak, amateurish feudal lord. If your failure were merely physical frailty, I would assign you to light textile spinning. If it were lack of education, I would send you to scribal school. But what you committed was **conscious, premeditated betrayal**.”

Clara gasped, her mouth trembling.

“For four years,” I continued, “you accepted the sacrifice of another human being who starved himself so you could wear clean linen and hold a silver icon. You knew his devotion. And with full, deliberate consciousness, the instant an arrogant brute flexed his muscles and offered you scented ribbons, you cast Lorenz into the freezing sleet to die of exposure, laughing as you closed the tent flap so you wouldn't have to look upon his misery. Deliberate, bad-faith betrayal of the hand that preserved your life is a malignant social cancer. And in the *Ordensstaat*, deliberate treason is never excused.”

“No!” Clara sobbed, dropping to her knees on the cold slate, her hands reaching out in frantic supplication. “He forced me! Lorenz, I swear it! Marek threatened to leave me in the forest! I hated him! Please... forgive me! Take me back! I’ll clean your office! I’ll do anything you want!”

“Director Weidmann,” I called out, ignoring her theatrical tears.

From the dark corner of the room, Weidmann stepped into the lantern light, quill poised over the ledger. “Present, Supreme Commissary.”

“Record the labor allocations.”

“Asset 802: Brigit. Assignment: *Timber Clearance Unit 4*. She will fell mountain pines in the northern sector twelve hours daily. Baseline ration: two thousand two hundred calories of rye bread, salt pork, and boiled spruce-needle infusion. If her team meets quota, she will be issued leather winter boots.”

Brigit bowed her head once, her jaw set. “Understood.”

“Asset 803: Isolde. Assignment: *Thermal Maintenance Detail, Blast Furnace No. 2*. She will sit in the refractory chamber and channel low-intensity arcane resonance to stabilize intake air at eleven hundred degrees. Because heat-stroke degrades magical output, her shift is capped at ten hours, followed by fourteen hours of mandatory rest in the heated dormitories. She will be issued asbestos mitts, clean water, and two thousand calories daily. If she maintains temperature tolerances, she will receive sugar rations. If she deliberately lets the fire drop, her privileges will be revoked.”

Isolde swallowed hard, wiping cold sweat from her brow. She had expected to be burned at the stake or enslaved in chains; instead, she was being given a technically demanding, properly provisioned industrial position that stripped her of her academic ego while keeping her warm and fed.

“I... I understand,” Isolde whispered, bowing her head.

Finally, I looked down at Clara.

“Asset 804: Clara. Allocation: *Field Hospital Sanitation Detail No. 3*.”

Clara shuddered, looking up with red, tear-streaked eyes. “The... the hospital?”

“I will not send you to the mines,” I stated. “Your bone density is inadequate, and your death would be an inexcusable waste of state quicklime. Cruelty produces zero economic return. You will be fed two thousand calories of hearty rye bread, vegetable broth, and boiled lard every day. You will sleep in a dry, heated dormitory. You will be issued vulcanized rubber gloves, a starched apron, and boiled carbolic soap to protect you from contagion.”

I signed the order with a sharp, crisp stroke of black ink.

“Your duty is to scrub bedpans, boil soiled linens, and tend to the hygiene of injured working men who break their bones building this civilization. You will learn the true, unglamorous value of human labor. You will earn every calorie you eat through honest service. But your coquettish tears, your pious hypocrisy, and your manipulative smiles have zero purchasing power in this office. If you scrub diligently, you will live in safety and peace. If you neglect the sick or attempt to shirk your duty—the stick will fall, and your bread ration will be halved.”

Clara stared at me, the last shreds of her romantic delusions evaporating into the sharp, antiseptic smell of the room.

There was no hatred in my eyes. There was only the unbending, immovable weight of justice.

She looked at her raw, soap-scrubbed hands, bowed her head until her forehead touched the slate flagstones, and whispered through her tears:

“I... I will scrub, Commissary. I will scrub the pans.”

“Take them to their quarters,” I commanded.

Two Black Cloaks seized Clara by her upper arms, hoisting her bodily from the floor.

She began to scream—a shrill, ear-splitting shriek of unadulterated madness and grief—as her wooden clogs dragged across the flagstones, her wails echoing down the concrete corridor until the heavy iron door slammed shut, cutting off the sound like an executioner’s axe.

Silence returned to Interrogation Room 1.

The whale-oil lamp flickered. The smell of carbolic acid and ink lingered in the air.

Director Weidmann carefully blotted the final entry, his lips twitching with quiet, bureaucratic bliss.

“A most efficient allocation of municipal biomass, Supreme Commissary,” Weidmann murmured. “And what of the fourth asset? The male? The one who called himself 'The Gilded Cleaver'?”

I picked up the next file on my desk: *Asset 001-M: Marek of High-Crest*.

“He remains in the isolation pen,” I said, checking my bare wrist by habit. “Let him reflect upon his biceps in the dark for another forty-eight hours. We have more pressing matters to attend to.”

At that moment, the heavy brass alarm horn atop the Central Watchtower sounded.

*HOOOOORN.*
*HOOOOORN.*
*HOOOOORN.*

Three long, concussive blasts. The emergency signal for immediate tactical mobilization.

Sergeant Kester burst into the interrogation room, his kettle-helm buckled tight, his face pale beneath a sheen of cold sweat. He executed a sharp, trembling salute.

“Supreme Commissary! The northern lookouts at Ridge 9! Signal fires are burning across the entire frontier!”

I stood up, buttoning the collar of my charcoal storm-coat. “Identity of the hostile force?”

“The Karst Steppe Coalition, sir!” Kester stammered, his knuckles whitening on his sword hilt. “Six thousand horsemen and highland hill-tribes! They’ve crossed the frozen river! They’ll be at our outer ditch by dawn!”

I picked up my brass field telescope and my pencil.

“Six thousand,” I noted calmly, my voice steady as an iron plumb-line. “An exceptional operational challenge. Inform Master Torvald to prepare the mortar batteries. It is time to test our industrial harvest.”
