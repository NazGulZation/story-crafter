# The Pit of Barrow-Gate

The subterranean cellar beneath the Old Tanneries was hot, damp, and smelled of things that had died twice.

Rotting cow-skins soaked in lime vats across the alley, their reek drifting down through the wooden ceiling grates to mingle with the stink of tallow grease, wet dog, bloodied sawdust, and the sharp, chemical burn of cheap plum brandy. Two hundred men and women packed the timber galleries that looked down into the twenty-foot square fighting pit. 

Mule-skinners with scarred brows, wool-factors in grease-stained beaver caps, off-duty watchmen, and river-barge captains packed the railings, clutching leather betting slates.

At the edge of the pit stood Deputy Brand, his heavy iron cudgel resting against his hip, shouting wagers above the roar of the crowd.

"Ten silver marks on the brute!" a fat wool-merchant with a missing tooth hollered, tossing a leather coin bag onto the chalk line. "Three rounds! If the ditch-boy stays on his feet, I pay double!"

I stood in the corner of the pit, my boots sunken two inches into damp sawdust.

I wore my new leather brigandine, the iron plates cold against my ribs. On my head sat the wide-brimmed steel kettle-helm, buckled firmly beneath my jaw with an oiled leather chin-strap. In my right hand, I held my plain three-foot arming sword, the blade freshly honed from my hours on the granite rock in the pine woods. At my left arm hung a round wooden buckler, ten inches across, faced with boiled hide and rimmed with a band of hoop-iron.

Across the chalk line stood my opponent.

His name was Torvald the Cleaver. 

He was thirty years old, broad as a beer wagon, with shaved jowls covered in blue ink tattoos depicting coil-horned river serpents. He wore a hauberk of heavy blackened chainmail that fell to his knees, iron greaves upon his shins, and held an eight-pound iron-headed bearded axe in his right hand.

At his neck hung an iron pendant shaped like a bull's skull. It pulsed with a dull, earthy brown light—the low-tier blessing of Stone-Bone, an enhancement that hardened muscle tissue against crushing blows.

"That's twenty pounds of chain on him, lad," Brand muttered, leaning over the timber rail five feet above my shoulder. "His father was a quarryman from the Crags. When that axe comes down, do not try to catch it on your buckler. It will drive the wood into your teeth."

"I have no desire to eat ash wood," I replied.

My voice was quiet, dry, and leisurely. 

I rolled my right wrist. The arming sword made a small, singing loop through the humid cellar air. The soul-brand from the Anvil of Ash lay quiet beneath my breastbone, an icy seed of power. 

I would not need the Sovereign Duel for this clown.

A knight who invoked a divine trump card against a common tavern brawler was no knight at all. A master of the blade used his gifts for kings and gods; a butcher in a pit was handled with simple geometry and clean footwork.

Brand raised his iron cudgel, slamming it against an iron rim hanging from the rafters.

*CLANG.*

"Bout is called!" the deputy bellowed. "No eye-gouging, no poisoned steel, no killing blows after the bell! First man to yield or sleep takes the sawdust!"

Torvald took three heavy, stomping strides forward, his chainmail clinking like a sack of iron horseshoes. 

He grinned, revealing yellowed fangs, and raised his bearded axe with both hands. The brown aura of Stone-Bone flared around his forearms, swelling his veins till they looked like thick hemp cordage.

"Little ditch-boy with a pig-sticker," the brute grunted, his breath reeking of pickled herring and raw garlic. "I'll break your knees first, then I'll sell your boots to the carters."

He swung.

The axe hissed through the air in a flat, horizontal sweep meant to shear my torso in half. 

The swing held terrifying momentum—enough force to sever an oak post. But he launched it from his rear shoulder, his hips turning late, his belly floating open for half a heartbeat. 

Stepping backward in a small pit was a coward’s mistake, surrendering ground and pinning a man against the timber stakes.

I took a short passing step forward and to the left, angling forty-five degrees into his blind quarter.

The heavy iron axe-head whistled through empty air two inches past the brim of my kettle-helm. 

Before Torvald could arrest the swing’s massive inertia, I drove the iron rim of my small buckler straight into his left ear.

*CRACK.*

The iron band took him on the temple bone. The impact rattled his skull inside his iron mail coif, spraying sweat and spit across the sawdust. 

Torvald roared, stumbling sideways, his balance ripped from under him. 

The crowd in the gallery erupted into a collective howl.

"He moved inside!" someone screamed. "Look at the boy's feet!"

Torvald swung his left elbow backward in a blind, savage arc. 

I ducked beneath the flailing leather sleeve. I came up on his rear flank and snapped the flat of my steel across the back of his right knee. The blow rattled his mail, the shockwave vibrating through his tendon and forcing the heavy leg to buckle.

Torvald went down on one knee with a heavy grunt.

Yet the brute was durable. The brown stone-bone aura flared around his spine, absorbing the kinetic shock that would have crippled a normal man. He surged upward like a wounded boar, spinning with a short, brutal backhand chop of his axe haft.

The wooden shaft clipped my left shoulder.

Pain flared through my deltoid. 

The force drove me back two steps, the sawdust sliding under my boots. My breath caught in my throat.

*Friction,* I reminded myself, tasting the sour tang of copper in the back of my mouth. 

Theron Marrow’s nineteen-year-old body had fast twitch-fibers, but his muscle mass was light. An eighty-pound blow from an armored quarryman transmitted shock straight through the bone of my clavicle, sending a dull ache radiating down to my left wrist. My left hand grew numb around the buckler strap.

If I tried to trade blows with this brute, Theron’s unconditioned frame would crack. 

*Fight with the mind, Jaime.*

The words of my uncle Kevan Lannister echoed through forty years of memory. *A bull has more meat than a lion, but a bull spends its life hauling plows while the lion feasts.*

Torvald shook his shaggy head, spitting blood into the sawdust. His small pig-eyes burned with murderous humiliation. 

"Tricky little flea," he snarled. "I'll turn your ribs to meal."

He charged.

This time, he kept his axe tight to his chest, using his armored bulk to bulldoze me into the corner stakes. A classic street tactic: crowd the fencer, smother his reach, and crush him against the wood with brute weight.

I let him come.

I retreated two paces until my rear heel touched the rough oak planks of the pit wall. 

The spectators above shouted in triumph, believing the bout was over. "Cornered him! Squeeze him, Torvald!"

Torvald lunged with a straight overhead cleave, bringing eight pounds of iron straight down toward the crown of my kettle-helm.

In the final fraction of a heartbeat, I shifted my weight onto my left toe and dropped my center of gravity six inches. 

Refusing a direct block, I raised my buckler at a steep, forty-five-degree slant above my forehead.

The iron axe-head struck the sloped wooden face.

*SKRRR-THUNK.*

The steep angle deflected the blow cleanly, sending the eight-pound head sliding down the oiled hide, crashing harmlessly into the sawdust beside my left boot.

The downward momentum pulled Torvald forward, his entire chest exposed.

I exploded upward.

My left hip drove forward, my right arm extending in a straight, economic punch. 

The iron ring-pommel of my arming sword took Torvald squarely in the middle of his nose.

*CRUNCH.*

The sound of cartilage and nasal bone shattering was sweet as a lute chord. 

Torvald’s eyes rolled white. Blood erupted from both nostrils in a bright crimson spray. His axe slipped from his limp fingers, burying its blade in the dirt.

He teetered on his heels for a single, frozen second.

I stepped around him, planted my right boot against the small of his armored back, and gave a sharp shove.

Torvald crashed face-first into the sawdust, his massive arms splayed out, his shaven head plowing three inches into the muck. 

He lay motionless in the dirt. The brown stone-bone light at his neck sputtered and died, leaving only the dull grey of dead iron.

The cellar went utterly still.

Two hundred men leaned over the timber rails, mouths open, clay cups forgotten in their hands. 

The fat wool-merchant with the missing tooth stared down into the pit, his jaw slack, his ten silver marks sitting abandoned on the rail.

Deputy Brand lowered his cudgel, his scarred ear twitching. 

He walked to the edge of the pit, looked down at Torvald’s motionless bulk, and let out a long, low whistle.

"Bout is done," Brand announced, his gravelly voice echoing off the brick vaults. "Winner by knockout in the first round... Theron Marrow."

The cellar erupted into a pandemonium of curses, groans, and ecstatic shouts from the few gamblers who had wagered on the outsider. Coin bags slammed onto tables; slates were wiped clean with greasy sleeves.

I lowered my arming sword, sliding the clean steel back into its oiled scabbard with a crisp, dry snick.

My left shoulder throbbed with a dull, bruised ache where the axe haft had clipped me, and sweat ran down the small of my back, soaking my linen shirt. But my breathing was steady, my pulse calm.

Brand leaned over the railing, extending a heavy leather purse.

"Ten silver marks," Brand said, his grey eyes assessing me with sharp, professional respect. "Plus five from the house pot. Fifteen silver marks, lad. You just took down the best brawler in the Lower Ward without putting a single scratch on his mail."

I reached up with my right hand, catching the heavy leather purse. 

The weight was magnificent. Fifteen silver marks was three hundred copper crowns—enough to buy a trained gelding, a month of grain, and three sets of custom-fitted armor.

"You have clean footwork, boy," a dry, cultured voice spoke from the shadows of the lower gallery.

I turned my head.

Standing at the foot of the gallery stairs was a man in his late forties. He wore a long coat of dark green wool trimmed with black marten fur, a silver balance-scale pin fastened at his lapel. His fingers were clean, manicured, adorned with two gold signet rings. Behind him stood two heavy men-at-arms clad in matching grey tabards, carrying short-hafted bills.

Factor Vallow.

The boy Theron’s memories supplied the name immediately: one of the five merchant directors of the Inland Mining Syndicate, the syndicate that controlled the transport of iron and silver ore from the Grey Spires down to the southern river ports.

"I have seen hedge knights fight across three provinces," Factor Vallow said, stepping toward the chalk line, his dark eyes measuring the balance of my shoulders. "Most swing like drunken blacksmiths. You fight like an instructor from an imperial academy. You moved three inches and let his own weight break his nose."

"Distance is the soul of fencing," I said smoothly, resting my right hand on my pommel. "A man who does not understand distance belongs behind a plow."

Vallow smiled—a thin, shrewd merchant's smile.

"I have three wagons of refined iron ingots moving north to the Skittering Ridge outpost tomorrow at dawn," Vallow said, leaning closer. "The road passes through the Crags. The highland stalkers have grown bold since the first frost. The Grand Guild wants fifty silver marks for a five-man vanguard led by that golden-haired boy from the High Ward."

"Evander Montfort," I murmured, a faint smirk touching my lips.

"The very same," Vallow said with a quiet snort. "The boy wants twenty marks up front just to mount his white horse, and spends half the march posing for village girls along the road. I need steel on my rear wagons. Men who watch the tree line instead of their own shadows."

"What does it pay?" I asked.

"Six silver marks for the march," Vallow said. "Three marks upon departure, three upon safe delivery at the Skittering Ridge redoubt. You take command of four teamsters and two crossbowmen on the baggage train."

A rear-guard command. Independent authority over the supply wagons, six marks in ready coin, and a direct line of march along the same road Evander’s gilded party was escorting.

"Make it eight marks," I replied, holding his gaze without a flicker of hesitation. "And two jars of dried beef from your commissary."

Vallow’s eyebrows rose. He looked at the unconscious bulk of Torvald the Cleaver still snoring in the sawdust, then back to my steady grey eyes.

He reached into his fur-lined coat, pulled out a small leather voucher stamped with the syndicate’s scales, and handed it to me.

"Eight marks," Vallow agreed. "Report to the North Stables at fourth bell tomorrow. And bring that iron kettle-helm. The Crags are cold, and the wind has teeth."

"The wind and I are old friends," I said.

I pocketed the voucher, slung my buckler over my shoulder, and walked up the cellar stairs into the crisp autumn night.
