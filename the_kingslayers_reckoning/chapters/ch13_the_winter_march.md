# The Winter March

The northern mountain gale tasted of crushed ice, sulfur, and dead pine needles.

By the fourth day of the march into the Grey Spires, the mountain trail had ceased to be a road. It was a narrow, treacherous ledge of black basalt coated in an inch of glassy sleet, winding between sheer granite precipices that rose into swirling grey blizzards above and vanished into fog-choked ravines below. The temperature had plunged twenty degrees below freezing; every breath rasped in the throat like swallowed glass.

Yet the sovereign column moved with the relentless, unyielding cadence of an iron clockwork.

Four hundred heavy freight wagons, flanked by two thousand marcher conscripts and six hundred heavy pikes, advanced in three tight divisions. 

Every morning at third bell, teams of twelve pioneer sappers marched a hundred paces ahead of the lead wagon, scattering coarse crushed granite grit and wood-ash across the black ice to give the iron-shod hooves of the draught oxen purchase. Behind every four wagons walked a cart loaded with seasoned split birch logs, canvas snow-shovels, and iron picks.

To keep the wagon wheels turning on frozen ground, I had ordered every teamster to wrap their wheel rims in thick hemp rope soaked in hot pitch. 

The rope bit into the ice, preventing side-slips on the twenty-degree mountain grades. The hub boxes were packed every dawn with a mixture of mutton tallow and sulfur powder, which kept the axle grease from seizing into stone in the sub-zero chill. A broken wheel on a mountain ledge was a death sentence for five wagons behind it; my father Tywin had executed four baggage officers during the Reyne campaign for failing to grease their wagons, and the lesson had stayed with me through thirty years of war.

In the center of the column, four traveling soup-kitchens—heavy iron cauldrons mounted on wide-wheeled carts—bubbled continuously over slow charcoal embers. 

Every three miles, a division halted for ten minutes. Every soldier received a wooden bowl of boiling pease-porridge cooked with cured salt pork, wild mountain onion, and a ladle of hot spruce-needle tea to keep the scurvy from rotting their gums.

The men’s feet were dry. 

Before we left Barrow-Gate, I had used two hundred silver crowns from the commissary purse to buy three thousand tallow-soaked lambskin boot-wraps from the tanners. A soldier with wet feet was a cripple within three days; a soldier with dry wool and fat in his belly could march through the Seven Hells and ask for a second helping.

I rode at the head of the vanguard division on my shaggy mountain mule, wrapped in a heavy mantle of black wolf-skin over my steel brigandine, an iron nasal helm pulled low over my brow.

Beside my stirrup rode Master Kroll, his walrus mustache caked in white rime, an iron crowbar resting across his saddle bow.

"Look there, Captain," Kroll called out, pointing with a mittened thumb toward a rocky gully beside the trail.

I drew rein.

Half-buried in a drift of wind-blown snow lay the carcass of a fine southern thoroughbred charger. 

The horse had belonged to Evander’s temple retinue. Its crimson velvet barding was torn to shreds by sharp shale; its legs were stiff as pine posts, the golden varnish on its hooves cracked and peeled by the frost. The saddle had been roughly cut away, but the beast's swollen belly had already been scavenged by highland ravens.

Ten paces further up the gully sat a light two-wheeled baggage dray, its axle snapped in two, abandoned in the snow.

Inside the shattered bed lay three crates of southern spiced plum wine, frozen solid and burst into jagged purple ice shards, and two water-logged trunks of fine embroidered silk tunics ruined by the sleet.

"Two days ahead of us," Kroll muttered, spitting a dark plug of tobacco into the drift. "The idiot marched his men on light southern carts with ash-wood axles. One jolt on the basalt, and the hubs shatter like clay pots. He dumped the food to save his gilded trunks, then dumped the trunks when the horses starved."

"He didn't dump just trunks," I said, my eyes catching a shape beneath the overhang of a granite boulder thirty paces ahead.

I swung down from my mule, the frozen shale crunching under my iron-capped boots. 

Huddled beneath a frozen cedar branch, wrapped in a single tattered blanket of wet wool, were three people.

Two were teenage carters from the High Ward, their fingers black with third-degree frostbite, their lips blue and cracked. Between them sat a girl of eighteen, shivering so violently her teeth clicked like bone dice in a cup.

It was Maren, one of the kitchen maidens from the *Sun-Vanguard’s* high-town suite.

"Maren," I said, stepping into the shelter of the rock.

The girl raised her sunken, tear-streaked eyes. When she recognized my face under the kettle-helm, a ragged, choking sob tore from her throat. She tried to reach for my boots with hands wrapped in rags, but her limbs had no strength left.

"Theron..." she wept, her voice a dry, papery whisper against the howling gale. "He left us... he took our mules... Lord Evander said we were dead weight... he said anyone who couldn't keep pace with the chargers belonged in the snow..."

"How long ago?" I asked, kneeling beside her in the drift, pulling a flask of warm honeyed wine from inside my wolf-fur coat and pressing it to her frozen lips.

She swallowed with a desperate gasp, coughing as the warmth hit her chest. 

"Yesterday noon," Maren sobbed. "The sorceress... Lady Seraphina... she fell from her horse two leagues back. Her hands were shaking so bad she couldn't hold the reins. She was screaming for frost-drops, but the bottles broke when the dray tipped over. Evander slapped her... he cursed her for a useless drug-addict and tied her to his saddlebow."

"And Lyra?" I asked, my voice entirely flat.

Maren’s eyes flickered with bitter memory. "Lyra begged him to stop for the night. She begged him to build a fire because her toes were numb. Evander dragged her out of the tent by her hair and threw her into the baggage wagon. He said if she complained again, he would feed her to the frost-trolls."

Kroll stepped up behind me, cursing softly under his breath with raw, sulfurous frontier profanity.

"Pick them up, Kroll," I commanded quietly. "Carry them back to the third commissary cart. Have the chirurgeon wash their fingers with warm lard and wrap them in dry wool. Give them hot broth and spruce tea."

"Aye, Captain," Kroll nodded, signaling two stretcher-bearers with his arm. "Gods above... leaving your own camp-followers to freeze on a mountain pass. A swine wouldn't do that to his runts."

"A swine has natural instincts," I said, watching the bearers lift the shivering girl onto a wool litter. "A vain boy has only fear."

I climbed back onto the rocky ledge, looking up the twisting defile of the Grey Spires.

The wind had shifted. 

It was blowing from the northeast, carrying the distinct, unmistakable stink of old battlefields: the greasy, sweet reek of unburied carrion, the sour stench of spilled entrails, and the foul musk of mountain frost-fiends.

Half a league ahead, the mountain walls pinched together into the narrow defile known as the Black-Mist Gorge.

A wall of dense, freezing grey vapor hung over the entrance to the gorge, so thick the jagged basalt cliffs seemed to dissolve into milk.

A distant, echoing boom rattled through the gorge—the sound of a magical explosion, followed three seconds later by the thin, terrified scream of a dying warhorse.

Then another scream. High, shrill, human, cut short by the wet tearing of iron.

The vanguard Division Sergeant, a scarred marcher veteran named Kester, trotted his horse up beside me, his crossbow unlimbered across his saddlebow.

"Captain," Kester said, his voice tense and low. "That was temple magic in the fog. Evander hit the gorge."

I listened. The wind howled through the granite teeth of the canyon, carrying the sharp, whistling cries of dying horses and the crackle of firebolts dissipating in the freezing mist.

"Sound the halt," I ordered. "Close the files. Lock the perimeter."

"Halt the freight wagons at the gorge mouth," I ordered with crisp, absolute authority. "Form a double wagon wall across the neck. Dig the wheels six inches into the shale. Pikes in the front rank, kneeling with butt-spikes in the rock. Crossbowmen behind on the flatbeds. No man advances into the fog."

"And the temple host, Captain?" Kester asked, staring into the swirling grey mist. "If they're trapped in the throat—"

"The dead do not need rescue, Sergeant," I told him, drawing my arming sword and resting the flat across my pommel. "And the living will be running this way very soon."

I rode the perimeter of the wagon laager, watching sixty pioneer sappers swing their heavy iron pickaxes into the frozen shale, trenching a shallow ditch along the front rank. 

The sound of iron biting rock was honest and steady. 

Two hundred marcher pikemen dropped to one knee, planting the twelve-foot ash butts of their weapons against the stone revetment, their steel tips forming an unbroken hedge of sharpened iron pointing into the swirling mist. Behind them, eighty crossbowmen cranked their spanning levers, setting blackened iron bolts into their tracks, blowing on their fingers to keep their touch supple.

I dismounted, handed the mule’s reins to a teamster, and walked to the center of the line.

The air grew bitterly cold as twilight fell over the Grey Spires, turning the snow drifts the color of bruised slate. From deep within the fog of the gorge came the frantic, galloping rhythm of hooves slipping on black ice, accompanied by the desperate shouting of men who had thrown away their shields to run for their lives.

I adjusted the chin-strap of my kettle-helm, planted my boots in the gravel, and waited.
