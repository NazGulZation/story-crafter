# The Gilded Boar and the Right Hand

The red stones came down with the sound of a split mountain.

I remembered the weight first. Ten thousand tons of black-fused granite tumbling through the darkness beneath the Red Keep, crushing the vaulted cellar into choking dust. I remembered the press of Cersei’s ribs against mine, the wet scrape of her tears against my cheek, and the terror in her green eyes—eyes so like my own. *Nothing else matters. Only us.* That was what I told her while the world caved in. Then the ceiling gave way. An iron beam snapped, a slab of masonry took me across the spine, and the dark swallowed the last breath of Ser Jaime Lannister.

A boot took me in the side.

The impact drove the breath from my lungs with a raw, muddy wheeze. Cold water slapped my face. I gasped, coughing up foul, sulfurous sludge, my teeth grinding on grit. The air smelled of wet horse dung, rancid tallow, sour mash, and rotting cabbage. Cold rain needle-pricked my bare neck. Sleet clattered against timber eaves above.

"Get up, you useless ditch-dog," a woman barked.

My eyes cracked open against a grey, spitting sky. 

I was lying on my stomach in a churned wallow of brown mud and crushed straw. Above me rose the crooked timber walls of a three-story roadside tavern, its cedar shingles blackened by northern moss. A painted wooden sign creaked on rusty iron pivots in the autumn wind: a swine painted in peeling gold leaf, bearing a foaming tankard between its tusks. *The Gilded Boar.*

Memories that belonged to another man flooded my brain like cold water poured into a cracked skull.

The body was nineteen years old. His name was Theron Marrow. He was born in the sheep-folds of the southern lowlands, the son of a ditcher who died of the lung-rot. Three years ago, Theron took a rusted sickle and an iron hunting knife, left his village, and came to the frontier town of Barrow-Gate to make his fortune among the guild-hunts. 

Along the way, the fool had played the hero. 

He found three novice girls shivering in a drafty barn, broke his back hauling firewood to keep them warm, and pooled his meager copper bounty to buy them iron weapons. He taught them how to dress a deer, how to build a dry lean-to in the marsh, and how to hold a shield against timber-wolves. For two years they scraped by on fourth-tier bounties—clearing marsh-rats from grain cellars, driving goblins from turnip fields, counting every zinc bit by tallow candlelight.

Then came the gilded boy.

"Look at him," a voice drawled from the covered wooden porch five paces away. "He looks like a drowned badger."

I pushed myself up on my elbows, the mud sucking at my forearms. 

Standing on the porch flags, sheltered from the biting sleet, stood Evander Montfort. 

He was twenty, broad-shouldered as a plow horse, with curls of pale spun gold that fell across a forehead of smooth, unblemished alabaster. His jaw was square, his nose straight, his eyes a dazzling, theatrical violet. He wore a half-suit of mirror-polished plate armor inlaid with fine brass filigree, a white wool cloak pinned at his shoulder by a blazing sunburst badge. The badge pulsed with a faint, iridescent gold shimmer—the blessing of the Goddess Sol-Aethel, the radiant deity this realm’s simpletons worshiped like sheep bleating after a warm trough. 

Evander rested his right hand upon the wire-wrapped hilt of a six-foot greatsword sheathed in white leather. At his hip hung a heavy purse of tooled calfskin, fat with silver marks.

Beside him on the porch bench sat Seraphina Kestrel. 

She wore indigo robes trimmed with silver fox fur, her glossy black hair pinned with carved bone needles. Between her slender fingers she turned an amber wand, tapping it rhythmically against her knee. She smelled of distilled almond oil and burnt sulfur—the bitter residue of high-grade mana elixirs. Her lips were curled into a thin, aristocratic sneer.

"We wasted three weeks dragging him through the Grey Spires," Seraphina said, her voice smooth and cold as pond ice. "His blood holds no mana veins. When the frost-spiders breached the gorge, his blade merely bounced off their carapaces. Evander had to burn sixty units of solar aura to clear the path because our dear porter froze in terror. A third-tier party cannot harbor dead meat."

To her left stood Renata Fell, leaning against a timber support post. She wore boiled leather brigandine studded with square iron rivets, a recurve horn bow slung over her shoulder. She held an iron hunting knife, paring a red apple with brisk, aggressive strokes. With her boot, she nudged a stained canvas travel pack off the edge of the porch.

The pack tumbled into the horse trough below with a heavy splash, soaking the spare wool hose and tallow candles Theron had carefully saved.

"You never had the belly for blood, Theron," Renata said, popping a slice of apple into her mouth. Her chewing was loud and rhythmic. "You were good for skinning hares and tending the mule. But real steel demands weight. Evander splits a crag-bear with one sweep of his solar edge. You? You scratch bark. Clear out before the city watch sweeps the muck."

A circle of forty men and women stood on the wooden gallery and around the tavern yard—teamsters, mule-skinners, off-duty city watchmen in rusted mail, and cheap tavern doxies with wool shawls pulled tight against the sleet. They leaned over the timber rails, clutching clay mugs of sour ale, watching the spectacle with the dull, cruel relish common to men who own nothing and enjoy seeing another man stripped bare.

"Come now, ladies," Evander boomed. His chest swelled beneath his gilded plate, his voice carrying across the yard with the smooth resonance of a cathedral bell. "Let us show sacred charity. Theron did keep our camp clean. He boiled our oats. He held our horses in the rain."

He reached into his leather purse, his long, manicured fingers digging into the coin pouch. He pulled out three small, thin coins of grey zinc—the cheap, stamped scrap metal that common ditchers used to buy stale rye crusts. 

With a theatrical flick of his thumb, Evander sent the three coins spinning through the air.

They landed in the puddle six inches from my face, splashing dirty water across my cheek.

"Three zinc bits," Evander declared, offering the gallery a dazzling smile that showed two rows of straight, milk-white teeth. "Enough for a bowl of turnip water and a dry spot in the common hayloft. Go back south, Theron. Marry a farm girl who likes the smell of sheep fat. You have no place among the chosen of the Dawn."

The yard erupted into a chorus of rough braying laughter. A carter in a grease-stained smock slapped his thigh, spilling half his ale into the mud. 

The boy’s memories inside me screamed. 

A wave of suffocating, pathetic despair surged through Theron Marrow’s chest—a blind, choking agony born of shattered devotion and ruined dreams. He had given everything to these people. He had starved so they could eat meat; he had slept in wet ditches so they could take the dry side of the tent. 

The boy looked toward the third woman.

Lyra Cole stood on the porch steps, half-shielded behind Evander’s gilded shoulder.

She wore a gown of rich crimson wool, embroidered at the cuffs with golden wheat stalks, a silver chain resting against her collarbones. Her chestnut curls were piled high, her amber eyes bright, her skin flushed with the heat of the tavern hearth. She had grown up three doors down from Theron’s father’s hovel. When her mother died of the winter cough, Theron had chopped her wood and stolen salt pork from the bailiff’s storehouse to keep her from starving. He had loved her with the slow, dumb, stubborn loyalty of an ox.

Theron’s phantom voice fought for my throat, desperate to plead, desperate to ask *why*.

Lyra stepped down one wooden stair, her velvet-lined slippers stopping just short of the mud. Her delicate face, once so sweet and innocent in the meadow lanes of their childhood, hardened into an expression of vicious, triumphant spite.

"Stop staring at me like a whipped hound, Theron," Lyra said, her voice clear and sharp enough to pierce the wind. "It turns my stomach."

The yard fell into an expectant, cruel quiet. The spectators leaned forward over the railings.

"You think I owe you something because you shared a loaf of black bread three winters ago?" Lyra sneered, lifting her chin to show the gleaming silver chain at her throat. "Look at yourself. You are nineteen years old, and you own three copper coins, a notched iron pig-sticker, and a pair of boots with holes in the soles. You are small. You are weak. You will die in a ditch, just like your father did."

She laughed—a high, mocking chime that drew appreciative chuckles from the teamsters.

"You want to know why I chose Evander?" she asked, stepping close enough that the scent of her lavender soap cut through the reek of horse manure. "Evander is a man. Evander has the favor of the Goddess. Evander took me into his arms on our second march, while you were pacing outside in the frost playing the faithful sentry."

She leaned down slightly, her amber eyes glittering with venom, ensuring every drunkard on the veranda heard every syllable.

"Every night you froze out there in the dark, counting oat sacks and checking axle grease, I was naked in his furs," Lyra said, her lips parting in a wicked, triumphant smirk. "We romped by the brazier until the dawn light. He took me three times a night, screaming his name into the wool, while you shivered by the ash heap like a blind mule. We laughed at you, Theron. Every time we heard your boots crunching the gravel outside, we laughed until our ribs ached. He gave me silk. He gave me silver. What did you ever give me besides cold mud and blistered feet?"

A roar of raucous, filthy laughter shook the tavern porch. 

"Hear that, lad?" shouted an old pikeman with a missing ear. "She was taking the golden lance while you were minding the oats!"

"Three zinc bits is two too many for a gelding!" a mule-skinner bawled, wiping beer foam from his grey beard.

The phantom agony of Theron Marrow threatened to drown my senses. The boy’s heart was shattering, tearing itself apart in a convulsion of shame, weeping for a love that had never existed, begging the earth to open up and swallow him whole.

*Be quiet,* I told the boy.

I said it with the cold, unyielding authority of a Lord Commander of the Kingsguard. I said it with the voice that commanded five thousand lances at the Golden Tooth and stared down the Mad King beneath the iron gargoyles of King's Landing. 

The boy’s weeping stopped. His consciousness receded, slipping into the dark recesses of the mind, leaving the driver’s seat to me.

I lay in the mud for three slow heartbeats. 

Then, I looked down at my hands.

My left hand was whole, lean, pale, coated in brown mire.

I turned my right arm.

A gasp caught in my throat.

The arm ended in sound bone and pale, living skin. The scarred stump was gone; the heavy gilded brass clasp had vanished into the ash of King's Landing. Gone was the phantom ache of severed tendons, wiped clean along with the memory of Vargo Hoat’s rusty cleaver slamming into the tree trunk in the dark woods of the Riverlands.

I had a hand.

A real, living, flesh-and-blood right hand.

I spread the five fingers. I watched them flex against the grey daylight. I pressed the pads against the wet grit of the earth, feeling the sharp bite of every grain of stone. I closed my fist. The knuckles cracked cleanly; the flexor tendons in my forearm bunched with tight, unbroken spring-steel tension. I rolled the wrist. The joint turned with fluid, effortless precision.

Ten fingers. Two arms. A young spine. Clean lungs that drew cold air without the rattle of age or the crush of fallen masonry.

I was twenty years younger, possessing forty years of slaughter, combat instinct, and courtly deceit locked behind my eyes—and I had my sword hand back.

A sound started in my chest. 

It began as a low, dry vibration against my ribs, bubbling up through my throat. Before I could check it, it broke past my lips.

I started to laugh.

The laugh rang out like a gilded horn across the mud—the rich, insolent, aristocratic roar of Jaime Lannister, the golden lion who mocked Robert Baratheon’s scowl and spat at the feet of the high lords of Westeros. 

The yard’s braying laughter faltered.

The silence began on the porch. Evander’s triumphant smirk wavered, his violet eyes narrowing in confusion. Beside him, Seraphina lowered her amber wand, her brow furrowing as she stared down into the puddle. Renata stopped chewing her apple, her hand remaining still against her hip.

The drunken carters and mercenary pikemen slowly shut their mouths, glancing at one another with uneasy frowns.

The crowd expected tears from a cuckolded wretch in the mud. They expected groveling, curses, or a blind, weeping charge into the spears of the guards. They expected a broken boy.

Instead, my laughter cut through the rain with the cold, leisurely amusement of an emperor listening to a tavern jester tell a tired joke.

I pushed myself up from the mud. 

My movements were smooth, deliberate, and entirely free of haste. I stood tall, planting my boots firmly in the muck. Theron’s frame was lean, perhaps an inch shorter than my old Westerosi body, but his center of gravity was solid. His shoulders were wide enough, his hips narrow, his legs possessing the clean, springy reach of a natural runner. 

I wiped a smear of brown sludge from my cheek with the back of my right hand, feeling the warm friction of my own skin. 

*Gods,* I thought, a razor-thin smile touching my mouth. *Cersei would have hated this mud. But she would have loved the look on this boy's face.*

I stepped forward until I stood at the base of the porch stairs, looking up at Evander Montfort.

Up close, the hero was even more absurd. His gilded plate was polished to a high mirror shine, yet the elbow cops were set half an inch too high, restricting his inward parry. His greatsword was magnificent to look upon, but the wire wrap on the hilt was decorative silver, slick as grease once blood hit the steel. His weight rested squarely on his heels—the stance of a man who had never had to dodge a mace aimed at his teeth.

"You missed three coins in the mud, boy," Evander said. His voice was louder now, straining to recapture the easy, mocking command he held moments ago. "Pick them up. It is the last charity you will ever see from my hand."

I looked down at the three grey zinc bits resting in the brown pool. Then I looked back up at Evander’s smooth, handsome face.

"Keep them," I said.

My voice was quiet, but it carried across the damp yard like the ring of bare steel drawn from a scabbard. It held none of Theron’s rural cadence, none of the boy’s soft deference. It was the dry, haughty baritone of Casterly Rock.

"Buy yourself an extra yard of silk ribbon for your hair," I told him, letting my gaze drift lazily down his armor. "And pray to your radiant goddess that you never meet a man who knows how to hold an edge. You step with your heels together like a milkmaid crossing a gutter. A common hedge knight with a rusty boar spear would put three feet of ash wood through your throat before you cleared your pommel."

Evander’s jaw tightened. A dark, ugly flush crept up from his gorget, staining his porcelain cheeks crimson. His right hand clamped onto the hilt of his greatsword, the knuckles whitening. 

"You dare speak to me of steel, you ditch-rat?" Evander snarled, his voice dropping its melodic sheen. "I am the chosen blade of Sol-Aethel! I have slain crag-trolls with a single stroke! I could cleave you from collar to groin where you stand, and the Margrave’s bailiff would thank me for clearing the vermin!"

"You could try," I murmured, my eyes holding his with cold, relaxed amusement. "Draw it. Pull that six-foot iron spit out of its white scabbard. Let these good folk see how fast you swing against a moving blade instead of a wooden quintain."

Evander’s breath hitched. For a single heartbeat, pure fury flared in his eyes—and beneath the fury, a flicker of startled, instinctual hesitation. 

Animals know an apex predator when it looks them in the eye. A wolf does not care how brightly a peacock fans its feathers; it only sees meat and bone. In forty years of blood and steel, I had killed men far bigger, far stronger, and far braver than this gilded cockerel. The Smiling Knight had danced before me with an axe that split iron shields; Gregor Clegane had swung a six-foot greatsword with the weight of an ox. 

Evander Montfort was merely a handsome boy playing soldier in a mirror.

His hand stayed frozen on the wire-wrapped grip, his vanity paralyzed between his rage and an instinct he could not name.

I turned my eyes toward Lyra Cole.

The girl took a step back, her boot catching on the hem of her crimson wool gown. The triumphant spite on her face had curdled into a sudden, nervous tightness around her mouth. She clutched her silver necklace with both hands, her chest heaving against her bodice.

"You have a loud mouth, little bird," I said, my tone as mild as a spring morning in Lannisport. "And an even wider bedroll, it seems. You think you traded up from sheep wool to silk."

I let my gaze sweep over her gown, lingered for a fraction of a heartbeat on the grease stains near her hem, and laughed softly.

"When a peacock tires of his hen, he tosses her straight to the hounds," I told her. "Steer clear of my gate when that day comes. I keep no stray swine."

Lyra’s face turned white as bleached bone. She opened her mouth to spit another insult, but her jaw merely trembled, no sound escaping her throat.

Beside her, Seraphina rose from the bench, her amber wand raised, a spark of yellow flame crackling at the tip. "Impudent cur," the sorceress hissed. "You forget your place—"

"Quiet, witch," I said, without looking at her. "Your hand is shaking. You have taken too many frost-drops this morning. If you try to cast a firebolt with those trembling fingers, you will burn your own silk sleeves before you singe my tunic."

Seraphina froze. The spark at the tip of her wand sputtered and died. Her grey eyes went wide with sudden, sickening shock. Her drug habit was a secret she had guarded with her life; to have it spoken aloud in a crowded tavern yard by an unlettered porter stripped away her aristocratic composure like peeled bark.

Turning one's back on an armed man requires certainty of his cowardice. Evander remained rooted to the porch flags, his vanity paralyzed by the theft of his stage.

I walked through the puddle, my boots squelching in the brown muck. I reached the horse trough where Theron’s canvas pack floated in the grey water. 

I reached in with both hands. The icy water bit into my skin, clean and honest. I grabbed the coarse hemp straps, hoisted the dripping pack onto the rim of the trough, and unbuckled the iron clasp. 

At the bottom of the canvas, wrapped in greased sheepskin, lay Theron’s only weapon of value.

An arming sword. 

It was plain, blackened iron, three feet of straight double-edged steel, pitted near the fuller and notched near the tip from butchering wild pigs. The guard was a straight iron cross; the grip was cord-wrapped ash wood, darkened by the grease of Theron’s sweat. The pommel was a simple iron ring.

I pulled it from the sodden wool. 

I took the corded grip in my right hand.

The sensation rushed through my arm like liquid fire. My fingers wrapped around the ash wood, locking into place with the terrifying familiarity of an old lover’s touch. The weight was three pounds, slightly heavy in the blade, the balance point two inches past the guard—a crude tool, forged by a village smith for clearing brush and gutting sheep.

Yet in my right hand, it sang.

I drew the steel up before my eyes. I rolled my wrist. The tip carved a tight, invisible figure-eight through the freezing rain, stopping with absolute, vibrationless stillness six inches from my chin. 

*I am whole,* my heart whispered. *By the Seven, I am whole.*

I strapped the plain leather scabbard to my belt with my left hand, sliding the wet steel into the sheath with a dull, oiled snick. I left the ruined canvas pack and the soaked clothes where they lay. A soldier needs only three things: good boots, dry steel, and the will to use them.

The silence in the tavern yard was heavy as lead. Forty pairs of eyes watched me as I walked toward the open timber gate that led to the lower turnpike.

No one spoke. No one laughed. The carter with the spilled beer stared into his clay mug as though afraid to look up. 

Behind me on the porch, Evander found his voice at last, cracking with shrill, performative bravado.

"Run, then, coward!" the hero shouted into the wind. "Run to the ditches where you belong! If I ever see your face in Barrow-Gate again, I will have the bailiffs hang you for a vagrant!"

I kept walking, my gaze fixed on the road ahead. Replying to a dead boy in golden tin was a waste of breath.

I stepped out of the tavern yard and onto the muddy road. 

The wind howled down from the Grey Spires, carrying the bitter scent of pine resin, wet slate, and iron mines. Sleet stung my cheeks, but my blood was warm. My right hand rested comfortably upon the iron pommel at my hip, the fingers tapping a slow, rhythmic cadence against the grip.

Ahead, the grey limestone battlements of Barrow-Gate loomed against the stormy sky, its iron portcullis open to the trade wagons. 

I pulled the wet wool collar of my tunic tight against my neck and walked toward the gate.
