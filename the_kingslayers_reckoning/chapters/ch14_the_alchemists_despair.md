# The Alchemist's Despair

The ancient stormwater culvert beneath the High Ward was a black throat of carved basalt, slick with subterranean moss and smelling of stagnant rainwater, lime runoff, and wet rat dung.

Two hundred years ago, the founders of Barrow-Gate had built the culvert to vent flash-floods from the mountain peaks straight into the river canyon. The masonry was four feet thick, cold as tomb-iron, the water surging around our greased boots in a freezing brown torrent that rose halfway to our shins. Above our heads, through iron street grates spaced fifty paces apart, came the muffled howling of the gale and the occasional dull clatter of temple horse-patrols trotting across the paved limestone avenues.

I led the file, holding a small shuttered tin lantern that cast an eight-inch circle of yellow tallow light across the wet stone.

Behind me marched Sergeant Kester, carrying a heavy steel stirrup-crossbow cocked and loaded with an iron-capped quarrel. Behind him stepped Deputy Brand and four furnace veterans from the Smelter-Guild, each carrying a muffled iron pry-bar, coils of hemp rope, and broad skinning daggers tucked into their leather belts. 

Every man’s breath plumed white in the freezing draft. We moved without speaking, stepping into the hollows between water-worn stones where the current broke quietly against our leather leggings.

After four hundred paces of steady ascent, the culvert curved sharply to the north. 

The low ceiling rose into a vaulted brick junction where a secondary conduit branched upward. The air here lost the clean, muddy smell of mountain water, replaced by an acrid, biting stench that made my nostrils flare and my eyes water: sulfur, distilled vitriol, concentrated ammonia, and the sweet, nauseating stink of burnt phosphorus.

"The laboratory flume," Brand whispered into my ear, pointing an iron-clad finger toward an overhead vertical shaft fitted with a ladder of rusted iron rungs. "The runoff from the Academy of Light drains straight through this chute. The clerics pour their spent alchemical washes down the well."

I nodded, resting my back against the wet brickwork.

My right hand flexed against the pommel of my arming blade. The living tendons fired with clean, immediate torque. In my former life in Westeros, I had crawled through the sewer flumes of Casterly Rock as a boy of nine, racing Tyrion through the dark while our father held council with the lords of the West. Tyrion had known every pipe, every drain, every hidden sluice where water flowed. *A castle is only as strong as its drains, Jaime,* my brother had laughed, wiping gutter-muck from his crooked nose. *Lords build high towers to impress kings, but a scullery maid with a chamber pot knows the real way into the throne room.*

I reached up and grasped the bottom iron rung of the ladder. 

The iron was coated in a slippery, greasy film of chemical salt that stung the skin of my fingers, but the metal held firm in the stone mortar. 

I climbed smoothly, keeping my boots close to the wall to prevent the soles from scraping the brickwork. Twelve rungs brought me to an iron trapdoor set flush into the ceiling, secured by a heavy sliding latch on the underside—an emergency cleaning hatch installed for guild sweeps.

I drew a small brass oil-horn from my belt, trickled three drops of whale oil along the rusted latch pin, and waited ten heartbeats for the grease to soak the iron.

Then, bracing my feet on the third rung, I pushed upward with my shoulder.

The iron plate lifted with a faint, breathy scrape that was swallowed instantly by the roaring mountain wind outside.

A gust of warm, suffocating air washed over my face.

It smelled of shattered glassware, scorched sulfur, distilled ether, and dried blood.

I levered myself through the hatch, rolling flat onto a floor of polished slate tiles, my arming sword clearing its leather scabbard without a whisper. 

The room was vast, dark, and utterly desolate.

It was the Grand Laboratory of the High Academy—a soaring hall of ribbed sandstone arches, lined with mahogany workbenches sixty paces long, shelves of glass retorts, brass alembics, and lead-lined chemical sinks. But the scholarly tranquility of the guild was gone. 

Half the high leaded lancet windows had been shattered by the wind, admitting swirling sprays of freezing autumn sleet that rattled against broken alembics and soaked stacks of smoldering parchment strewn across the floor. In the center of the hall, three massive copper cauldrons—each the size of a brewing vat—lay overturned, their thick wooden legs chopped through with fire-axes. Dark, oily stains spread across the grey slate flags, hissing faintly where rainwater dripped from the ceiling beams.

Near the southern archway leading toward the inner cloister, two temple guards in white linen tabards lay sprawled on wooden campaign stools beside a charcoal brazier. 

They were not sleeping.

They had unbuckled their iron cuirasses and set their short-swords on the floor, their heads tilted back against the masonry as they passed an earthenware jug of southern brandy between their greasy gloves.

"Let the whelp whine," the elder guard spat, wiping liquor from his chin with the back of his thumb. "He wanted eighty barrels brewed before third bell. An apprentice cannot turn vitriol into sunfire in three days without blowing the roof off the citadel. The girl told him true."

"She told him true, and he gave her an iron fist across the mouth," the second guard chuckled, taking a long swallop from the jug. "Broke her pretty teeth. Stripped her velvet robes. Lord Montfort does not like being told no by a runaway magistrate's daughter. Once the Grand Inquisitor cleanses the Lower Ward tonight, she goes onto the pyre with the rest of the witches."

I stepped out of the shadow of the copper vat.

My boots made no sound against the wet slate.

The younger guard caught the movement in the edge of his vision. He turned, his mouth opening to shout a warning, reaching instinctively for the short-sword on the floor.

He never touched the hilt.

My passing step carried me across the six paces between us in two heartbeats. 

My left hand caught the bridge of his nose, clamping his head back against the stone wall with concussive force that cracked the mortar. 

Before the second guard could drop his brandy jug, my right hand drove the steel blade of my arming sword upward beneath his jaw, piercing through the soft floor of his mouth into the base of his skull.

The iron point grated against bone. 

The guard’s eyes rolled back; his fingers uncurled from the earthenware flagon. 

I caught the falling jug with my boot before it struck the slate, easing the dead man off my blade as his companion struggled feebly beneath my left hand, his windpipe crushed by my grip.

A single sharp twist of my wrist snapped the second man's neck with a muffled pop.

I lowered both bodies behind the charcoal brazier, wiped the red grease from my blade on the dead man's white tabard, and turned toward the arched alcove at the rear of the laboratory.

Sergeant Kester and Deputy Brand hauled themselves through the floor hatch, their crossbows raised, their eyes scanning the dark hall.

"Secure the hallway door," I whispered to Brand. "Kester, watch the clerestory stairs. If anyone comes down, shoot them through the throat before they reach the bells."

"Aye, Captain," Kester grunted, slipping into the shadow of a mahogany bookshelf.

I turned toward the rear alcove.

The alcove was closed off by a heavy iron grate—an isolation cell originally built to quarantine alchemists afflicted by chemical fevers. Inside, curled on a thin pile of damp straw beneath a dripping water pipe, lay a figure.

I stepped to the bars, lifting the small tin lantern.

The light fell across Lady Seraphina Kestrel.

The proud high elementalist of the *Sun-Vanguard* was broken.

Her fine azure silk robes had been ripped away, replaced by a filthy, oversized smock of unbleached hemp stained with sulfur soot and drying vomit. Her sleek black hair hung in matted, greasy clumps around a face that looked hollowed out by starvation and chemical fumes. Her right eye was swollen shut, the cheekbone beneath it purple and split to the bone. Her left hand was clamped tightly to her chest, her slender fingers blackened and blistered from contact with caustic acids. 

On the flagstones beside her cot lay the fragments of her prized amber-headed casting wand—snapped into three splintered pieces of ash-wood, the amber focus stone crushed to golden dust beneath an iron boot.

At the sound of my boots on the slate, Seraphina shrank back against the wet stones, a ragged, choking gasp breaking from her throat.

"Please," she whimpered, raising her blistered left arm to shield her face, her body trembling with violent, rhythmic shudders. "I... I gave you the formula... I cannot brew any more... the mana-burn is in my chest... I cannot cast..."

"Lady Kestrel," I said quietly.

The girl froze.

Slowly, her good eye opened, squinting through the greasy tangle of dark hair into the yellow glare of the lantern. 

Her breath stopped.

The blood drained from her pale lips until they were the color of cold tallow.

"You," she whispered, her voice cracking into a shrill, petrified squeak. "Theron... no... no, the inquisitors said you were dead... they said the Dawnguard cut you down in the river..."

"Inquisitors lie as easily as priests, my lady," I said, resting the point of my sword on the stone flags outside her cell. "And you should know by now that I am difficult to kill."

Seraphina collapsed forward onto her knees in the straw, pressing her forehead against the damp floorboards, sobbing with hysterical, gasping terror.

"Kill me then," she wept, her voice muffled by the hemp smock. "Go on! Run your sword through my ribs! I mocked you... I told Evander you were a useless beast of burden... I laughed when he tossed his clipped farthings into the tavern mud... I told the guild masters you were a cripple without a spark of divine mana in your blood! Kill me and have done with it!"

I looked down through the iron bars at the sobbing girl.

There was no joy in the sight. 

In Westeros, I had watched men and women beg for their lives beneath the swords of Tywin Lannister's vanguard after the battle of the Green Fork. Fear smelled the same whether it wore velvet or rags: sour sweat, urine, and the bitter stink of broken pride. Cruelty toward a helpless wretch was the mark of a butcher like Gregor Clegane, not a knight of the Kingsguard.

"Get up, Seraphina," I said, my voice cold and flat as an iron anvil.

She did not move, her shoulders heaving with ragged sobs.

"I said get up," I repeated, louder this time. "I did not crawl through four hundred paces of raw sewage to watch you ruin a perfectly good floor with snot."

Slowly, painfully, she pushed herself up onto her trembling knees. She looked at me through her swollen eye, her lips quivering, bewildered by the lack of fury in my face.

"Where is the counter-draught for the Sun-Spite venom?" I asked.

Seraphina blinked, wiping mucus from her chin with the back of her blistered wrist. "The... the counter-draught? For Margrave Carrow?"

"His heart is failing," I said. "He has less than six hours. Factor Vallow said the master vials are kept here."

"The third drawer," she stammered, pointing a trembling finger toward an iron apothecary cabinet set into the wall beside her cell door. "Behind the leaded jar of cinnabar... the silver-stoppered crystal flask... it is distilled white-root and moon-dew. It neutralizes the phosphorus in the bloodstream within forty heartbeats."

I turned to the cabinet. 

The brass pull was locked with a small padlock. 

I set the edge of my dagger against the iron hasp and struck the pommel with the heel of my palm. 

The small brass screw tore out of the dry pine timber with a sharp snap. 

I slid the drawer open. 

Inside, resting on a bed of blue velvet behind a heavy clay jar, lay a three-inch flask of cut crystal sealed with stamped silver wax. The liquid inside was a pale, milky opalescent fluid that glowed with a faint, cool luminescence in the gloom.

I lifted the flask, checked the silver wax for cracks, and tucked it securely into the inner pocket of my leather doublet.

"Now," I said, turning back to the iron grate. "The Liquid Sun."

A fresh shudder wracked Seraphina’s frame. "They... they took thirty barrels down to the lower cisterns three nights ago. Brother Dustin has them stacked in the dry brick chamber directly beneath the High Aqueduct flume."

"And the fuses?"

"Sulfur-soaked linen cords," she whispered, her voice shaking so violently her teeth clicked. "Treated with quicklime. Once Dustin drops the fire-pots into the tubs, the fuses will burn through the clay stoppers in less than five minutes. Evander... Evander ordered it himself."

"The hero of the realm," I murmured, a thin, razor-sharp smile touching my lips.

"He is mad, Theron," she sobbed, grasping the iron bars with her blistered fingers, her single open eye wide with frantic pleading. "He drank three vials of concentrated Solar Tears after you broke his jaw at the tourney. The divine fluid burned his mind... he hears voices from the sun... he believes the Goddess told him to burn the lower city to purge his shame! I told him the blast would fracture the mountain bedrock and drown the entire province in fire! He... he hit me with his gauntlet. He broke my wand across his knee. He told me an elementalist who couldn't burn a city was just another gutter-witch."

"He was right about one thing," I said, drawing an iron cold-chisel and a small mallet from my belt.

Seraphina flinched. "What?"

"You are a terrible judge of men," I replied.

I jammed the cold-chisel into the padlock holding the cell door and struck the head twice with the mallet.

*CLANG.*

The brass tumbler shattered. 

I kicked the iron door open. The rusted hinges shrieked as the gate swung wide.

Seraphina stared at the open doorway, her jaw slack, unable to comprehend the open space before her. "You... you are letting me go?"

"I don't have time to carry a prisoner through the sewers," I said, putting the chisel back into my pouch. "And I don't murder broken women in cages."

"Why?" she cried, her voice cracking with sudden, overwhelming confusion. "I treated you like dirt! I told Lyra to kick your pack into the ditch! I let Evander humiliate you in front of the entire tavern! Why don't you hate me?!"

I looked at her, standing in the doorway with my arming blade sheathed at my hip.

"Hating you would require me to care what you thought of me, Seraphina," I said calmly. "You were a silly, vain girl with a shiny stick who thought a handsome boy with a glowing sword made you a queen. Now your stick is kindling, your handsome boy is a mad dog, and you are shivering in a hemp sack."

I stepped past the doorway, gesturing toward the floor hatch where Deputy Brand was waiting.

"There is a dead sentry behind that brazier," I told her. "Strip his wool cloak, wrap yourself in it, and climb down that hatch. Go three hundred paces south until you reach the foundry sluice. Tell the smiths that Captain Marrow sent you, and sit by the coke-furnaces until the shooting stops."

Seraphina stared at me, tears cutting clean pale tracks through the soot on her bruised cheeks. 

She did not speak. 

She dropped to her hands and knees, crawled out of the iron cage, and scrambled toward the dead sentry's corpse with desperate, frantic haste.

I turned to Sergeant Kester, who stood watching from the mahogany workbench.

I reached into my doublet, pulled out the silver-sealed crystal flask of white-root elixir, and placed it firmly in Kester's callused palm.

"Take two men and go back through the culvert," I commanded him. "Deliver this into Lady Aurelia's hands. Tell her to pour half down the Margrave's throat immediately, and the other half after twenty heartbeats. Ensure Deputy Brand keeps twenty pikes on the foundry barricade."

"And you, Captain?" Kester asked, closing his fingers around the crystal vial. "What will you do?"

"Justinian expects thirty barrels to ignite at the fourth watch," I said, adjusting the straps of my iron kettle-helm. "I am going to pay Brother Dustin a visit in the cisterns."

I unbuckled my plain wooden buckler from my shoulder, slid my left arm through the leather braces, and headed down the vaulted hallway into the dark.
