# The Spoiled Escort

The road through the Iron Crags was an open wound in the rock.

A narrow, climbing ledge of crushed basalt and grey shale hugged the jagged cliffside, suspended four hundred paces above the foaming, slate-colored rapids of the Black Ditch. To the left rose sheer, wet granite walls dripping with rusty seepage; to the right lay the abyss, shrouded in swirling plumes of freezing mountain mist and the bitter, yellow stench of sulfur springs venting from deep cracks in the mountain roots.

The wind howled down from the Grey Spires with the shriek of an iron saw on bone, chilling the wool against my skin.

Our convoy consisted of seven heavy freight wagons. 

The first four wagons were high-walled freight drays loaded with casks of southern vintage, barrels of salted pork, and sacks of wheat flour for the border garrisons. The rear three wagons—my responsibility—were flatbed ore carts carrying ten tons of refined iron ingots from Factor Vallow’s smelters. Ten tons of cold, grey pig-iron lashed down with tarred hemp cables that creaked rhythmically with every jolt of the iron-shod wheels over the frozen shale.

I rode beside the lead ore cart on a sturdy mountain mule with a shaggy brown coat, a scarred left ear, and the stubborn temperament of a Northman. 

My plain arming sword hung at my left hip; my iron-rimmed buckler was strapped to the cart stanchion; a three-foot horn recurve bow and a quiver of twenty broadhead shafts, purchased from Deputy Brand's armory with my pit winnings, rested across the pommel of my saddle.

Fifty paces ahead of our wagons rode the vanguard.

The vanguard was a moving spectacle of gold leaf and silk.

At its head rode Evander Montfort atop a massive white charger whose hooves were painted with gold varnish. The stallion wore a quilted barding of crimson velvet embroidered with blazing sunbursts. Evander sat tall in his mirror-polished plate armor, a cloak of white fur billowing from his shoulders in the mountain gale. At his hip hung his six-foot greatsword, its white leather scabbard slapping rhythmically against the horse's flank.

Beside his stirrup rode a closed four-wheeled carriage of varnished ash wood, hung with heavy curtains of indigo wool. 

Through the parting curtains, Seraphina Kestrel could be seen reclining against silk cushions, her dark hair bound with silver pins, an ivory cup in her hands. The faint, sweet smell of burnt almond and distilled frost-drops drifted from the carriage window whenever the wind shifted rearward.

Behind the carriage rode Renata Fell, leading four mounted guild archers. She sat easily upon an agile grey mountain mare, an iron-bound recurve bow resting across her knees, her sharp eyes scanning the rocks above with aggressive, restless boredom.

And perched upon the carriage box, wrapped in a mantle of grey fox fur with silver clasps, sat Lyra Cole.

She held a fur-lined parasol to shield her face from the spitting mountain mist, her cheeks flushed pink from the cold, her lips painted with bright carmine grease. Every few miles, she would lean down to whisper something into Evander’s ear, prompting a booming, theatrical laugh from the hero that echoed off the basalt cliffs.

"Pompous young rooster," spat Master Kroll, my lead teamster, wiping tobacco spit from his grey walrus mustache. 

Kroll was fifty winters old, with hands like knotted oak roots and a squint born of forty years of mountain roads. He held the leather reins of four heavy draught mules with the calm, economic grip of a seasoned carter.

"Look at him prancing that white beast on loose shale," Kroll grumbled, nodding toward Evander's glittering figure. "One misplaced hoof on a patch of black ice, and that thousand-silver stallion will break its cannon bone and take the wagon with it. But try telling that to a boy with a holy badge. He thinks the mountain respects gold lace."

"The mountain respects gravity," I replied, checking the tension of the hemp cables securing the iron bars behind us. "And the mountain has plenty of patience."

At the midpoint of the afternoon, the convoy ground to a halt at a widening in the ledge known as the Cold Spring Cut.

The spring was a trickle of clear water spilling over a moss-grown shelf of black slate into a natural rock basin before plunging into the gorge. The teamsters took the opportunity to water the draught beasts and check the iron axle-pins.

I swung down from my mule. 

My boots struck the frozen gravel with a solid crunch. My left shoulder was stiff from the previous day's axe-blow in the pit, but a night of rest and a jar of hot bone broth had worked the worst of the ache from the meat. The flesh was sound.

I walked the length of my three ore carts, testing each hemp lashing with the toe of my boot. 

A loose cable on a twenty-degree mountain grade was an invitation to slaughter; if three tons of iron shifted during a climb, the wagon would overturn and drag the entire team over the cliffside.

"Check the linchpins on the middle dray, Kroll," I ordered quietly. "The left hub is weeping dark grease. Put a handful of fresh tallow in the box before we hit the upper grade."

"Aye, Captain," Kroll nodded, pulling an iron grease-horn from his tool chest. "You have the eye of an old wagon-master."

"I have the eye of a man who hates walking," I said.

A light, crunching step sounded behind me on the shale.

I kept my back turned, the scent announcing her before she spoke: sweet lavender soap, rosewater pomade, and the sharp musk of wet fox fur.

"Well, well," Lyra Cole’s voice chimed, dripping with theatrical pity. "Look at the great swordsman. Still checking wheel hubs and smelling of mule sweat."

I finished tying off a loose hemp knot on the cart rail. 

Then, with slow, unhurried deliberation, I turned around.

Lyra stood three paces away, holding her fox-fur mantle tight against the wind with gloved hands. Her amber eyes swept over my plain leather brigandine, my iron kettle-helm, and the scuffed toes of my riding boots. She tilted her chin, offering me the patronizing, superior smile of a village girl who had traded her homespun apron for a merchant's bed.

"I saw your name on the bailiff’s roster at the north gate," she said, her voice loud enough to carry to the nearby teamsters. "Factor Vallow hired you to watch the pig-iron for six marks. How quaint, Theron. While Evander negotiates with the Margrave’s council for a thousand silver sovereigns, you are still dragging wagon axles through the muck."

I rested my right hand casually upon the iron pommel at my hip. 

I looked at her with the cold, amused curiosity one might afford a performing ape at a harvest fair.

"The pig-iron does not whine when the wind blows, little bird," I said mildly. "Which makes it vastly superior company to the baggage in the front carriage."

Lyra’s smile faltered for an instant, a flash of irritation tightening the corners of her eyes. 

She took a step closer, lowering her voice to a venomous whisper, ensuring her words stung where she believed the boy Theron was most vulnerable.

"You are a fool, Theron," she hissed, her fingers twisting the silver chain at her neck. "You thought if you ignored me in the tavern yard, I would come crying to your door? Look at this fur. It cost forty silver marks in the High Ward. Evander bought it for me with a wave of his hand. He bought me silk shifts, pearl combs, and sweet wine from the eastern marches."

She leaned in, her amber eyes gleaming with malice.

"He takes me in his pavilion every night, Theron. On real goose-down mattresses, by the light of scented wax tapers. He holds my waist with hands that can split stone, and he tells me how lucky I was to escape a ditch-beggar like you. Do you hear me? You are nothing. You were born in the dirt, and you will die in the dirt."

I laughed softly.

The sound was pure, golden arrogance—the laughter of Jaime Lannister looking down from the walls of the Red Keep at the squabbling lords of the realm.

"You talk a great deal about another man's bed, little bird," I told her, letting my gaze drift lazily over her painted face. "A woman who is truly satisfied with her prize does not walk fifty yards through freezing mud just to remind an ex-porter of her good fortune."

Lyra froze. Her mouth opened, but no words came out.

"The truth is written across your face," I continued in a conversational, biting drawl. "You are terrified. You see that closed carriage up ahead? You see the magistrate’s daughter with the azure silk and the ivory pins? Evander has already spent two nights in her carriage while you slept on the floorboards with the baggage. You are a common village wench playing dress-up in borrowed furs. When this march reaches the highland citadel, the lords will offer Evander a real noble bride with land and titles. And where will that leave you?"

I leaned down slightly, meeting her wide, panicked eyes.

"Back in the dirt," I whispered. "Without the furs, without the silver, and without a single man living who would spit on you to put out a fire. Now get back to your carriage before you soil your pretty slippers. You are standing in my mule's trough."

Lyra stepped back as though she had been struck across the mouth with a wet leather strap. 

Her face turned the color of curdled milk. Pure, raw hatred contorted her features, but beneath the hatred was the cold, unmistakable terror of a woman whose deepest secret had been held up to the sunlight.

She spun on her heels, her velvet-lined skirts catching on the rocky scrub, and fled back toward the vanguard.

Master Kroll wiped his mustache, a wide grin splitting his leathery face. "Gods above, lad. I've seen men cleaved in two with a broadsword that didn't bleed as much as that girl just did."

"Words are cheaper than steel," I said, unbuckling the waterskin from my saddle. "And far cleaner."

Before I could take a drink, a commotion erupted at the head of the convoy.

Evander’s booming voice carried down the narrow defile, accompanied by the nervous whinny of his white stallion.

I climbed atop an outcropping of black basalt beside the road to see what the fool was doing.

Two hundred paces ahead, the road narrowed where a mountain spur jutted out over the gorge. A herd of highland goats had scattered across the shale, startled by the noise of the wagons. 

Among the boulders three hundred yards up the slope, a magnificent creature stood silhouetted against the grey sky.

It was an iron-antlered stag—a rare, territorial beast of the high crags, its massive rack of horn tipped with natural mineral deposits that gleamed like polished bronze in the mist. 

"Look at that beast!" Evander was shouting, his gilded gauntlet pointing excitedly toward the ridge. "Its antlers are worth fifty gold sovereigns at the Margrave’s court! Seraphina, Renata, hold the road! I will bring back its head before the wagons reach the ridge!"

"Evander, wait!" Renata called out from her saddle, her sharp eyes darting toward the black fir trees that lined the upper crag. "The wind is blowing from the north! If a stag is out on the open rock at midday, something drove it out of the timber!"

"Nonsense!" Evander roared with a confident, booming laugh. "The beast came out to admire my colors! Watch and learn, girl! This is how a champion of Sol-Aethel hunts!"

With a reckless dig of his gilded spurs, Evander sent his white warhorse galloping up the rocky scree, his greatsword drawn, a brilliant trail of golden solar aura flaring behind his blade.

I narrowed my eyes, looking past the stag toward the jagged shadows of the upper hemlocks.

The branches swayed against the wind with a rhythmic, creeping tremor. And from the dark gaps beneath the roots, three pairs of glowing red eyes peered down at the undefended rear of our convoy.

Crag-stalkers.

"Kroll!" I barked, dropping from the boulder and landing squarely in front of the ore wagons.

The teamster started at the sharp, iron authority in my tone. "Aye, Captain?"

"Draw the wagons together," I ordered with crisp, absolute clarity. "Pull the three ore carts into a tight wedge against the cliff wall. Put the draught mules in the center. Unlimber the crossbows. Now."

Kroll blinked, looking up at the empty road ahead. "Captain? The vanguard is still moving—"

"The vanguard is blind, and their leader is chasing a horned goat like an eight-year-old boy in a sheep pen," I said, stringing my recurve bow with a swift, fluid pull against my hip. "We have company coming from the rocks. Move!"
