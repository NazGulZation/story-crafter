# The Heretic's Summons

The bells of the Great Cathedral of Sol-Aethel tolled with a heavy, muffled cadence that set the crows wheeling in frantic circles above the snow-laden slate roofs of Barrow-Gate.

It was the *heretic’s clapper*—a dull, lead-weighted funeral toll reserved for sorcerers, oath-breakers, and traitors against the altar.

We had marched through the southern sally-port at mid-morning, bringing two thousand marcher conscripts, four hundred intact freight wagons, and three hundred pack beasts back from the heights of Ashen-Crag. The ancient frontier fortress had been secured without the loss of a single bushel of winter grain or an ounce of pig iron. Behind our lines marched the sixty-two surviving paladins of the Sun-Gilded Order, their dented breastplates strapped to the baggage drays, their heads bowed beneath coarse wool cloaks.

Yet there was no victory feast awaiting us in the lower plaza.

Instead, fifty temple guards in silvered scale-mail and sun-crested tabards stood in a double cordon across the avenue leading to Margrave Carrow’s palace. Their halberds were leveled at our lead wagons, their faces pale and rigid behind polished cheek-guards.

At their head stood a temple bailiff, a portly man in scarlet robes lined with ermine, holding an iron-tipped staff and a parchment scroll sealed with thick golden beeswax.

"Theron Marrow!" the bailiff proclaimed, his voice echoing off the timber facades of the merchant guildhalls. "By decree of His Holiness, High Prelate Malakor, and the Council of the Dawn, you are summoned to the High Dais of Sol-Aethel. You stand accused of black witchcraft, fiend-communion, and high treason against the realm!"

A murmur ran through the ranks of the marcher pikemen behind me. Sergeant Kester’s hand dropped to the butt of his loaded crossbow, his weathered knuckles white with fury.

"Hold your hands, Sergeant," I said quietly, reining my bay gelding to a halt before the bailiff’s staff. "A man who shouts treason in the street is usually trying to conceal a theft. Let us hear what the holy fathers have invented to cover their shame."

I dismounted, handing my reins to a carter. Margrave Carrow’s steward, an old clerk named Master Brand, stepped up beside me with two city magistrates and Factor Vallow, their wool cloaks pulled tight against the bitter river wind.

"The Margrave will be present, Bailiff," Brand said sharply, his ink-stained fingers tapping his cedar ledger case. "The civil charter of Barrow-Gate grants no prelate the right to try a captain of the Marcher Auxiliary in secret darkness."

"The doors of the temple stand wide to all believers," the bailiff sneered, gesturing with his staff toward the marble stairs of the cathedral. "Enter, if your champion dares face the light."

We climbed the thirty broad steps of white granite, our iron-shod boots scraping the frost from the stone.

The Great Cathedral of Sol-Aethel rose like a mountain of pale limestone into the slate-grey sky. Inside, the high barrel vaults soared eighty feet overhead, supported by ribbed piers of polished basalt. Thick clouds of burning frankincense and dried lavender rolled through the gloom, sweet and suffocating, masking the cold stink of wet slate and stagnant crypt air. Hundreds of tallow candles guttered in bronze candelabras along the nave, their yellow flames reflecting off three thousand citizens who packed the stone galleries.

At the far end of the nave, elevated upon a marble dais beneath a massive golden sunburst, sat High Prelate Malakor.

The prelate was a man carved of old tallow and cold ambition. He wore a ceremonial cope of heavy cloth-of-gold stiff with seed pearls, his withered fingers adorned with raw amber rings. On his lap rested the holy ledger of Sol-Aethel, bound in white calfskin with clasps of beaten electrum.

Beside the prelate’s high chair stood Evander Montfort.

The hero had attempted to restore his ruined splendour, but the cracks showed like soot on white linen. 

He wore a new tunic of ivory samite embroidered with golden lions—a petty mockery that brought a thin, quiet smile to my lips—and his electrum greatsword rested in an ivory scabbard at his hip. Yet his crooked, broken nose had healed into an ugly, misshapen lump across his pale face; his cheeks were sunken and sallow; and his golden curls were cut short to hide the scabs where frost-fiend claws had grazed his scalp. His right hand twitched beneath his linen cuff, trembling with a frantic, rhythmic tremor that ceased only when he gripped his scabbard.

Behind him stood the temple magistrates and sixty heavily armed templars with drawn broadswords.

"Step forward, Theron Marrow," Prelate Malakor intoned, his voice dry and hollow, carrying through the vaulted nave like the rustle of dead leaves. "You stand before the living gaze of the Dawn."

I walked to the base of the dais, stopping ten paces from the marble steps. I had not washed the road dust from my leather brigandine; the dark stains of frost-fiend blood still darkened the cuff of my right gauntlet; my steel arming sword hung plain and unadorned at my left hip. 

I gave the prelate the nod of a soldier acknowledging a clerk.

"I am here, Your Holiness," I said. "Speak your charge."

Evander took a step forward, his eyes burning with frantic, feverish hatred. He raised his left hand, pointing an ivory-ringed finger straight at my chest.

"He murdered my vanguard!" Evander shouted, his voice cracking with shrill hysteria that bounced off the limestone pillars. "He is an apostate! A hedge-wizard who sold his blood to the demons of the Grey Spires! When my knights charged the throat of Black-Mist Gorge, this creature invoked a shadow hex that quenched the holy radiance of my sword! He blinded my men with black mist, summoned a horde of two thousand frost-fiends, and barred his wagon wall against my retreat, leaving three hundred holy warriors to be torn to pieces by the beasts!"

The gallery of wealthy burghers and pious guild-matrons gasped, their heads turning in superstitious dread. 

Evander turned to the crowd, spreading his arms wide, his face adopting the grand, tragic mask of a wronged martyr.

"I fought them alone!" he declared, his voice ringing with theatrical passion. "I slew fifty of the mountain abominations with my own blade before the frost overwhelmed my steed! I rode through a storm of demon arrows to bring word to the city, while this coward sat by his fires, counting copper coins and laughing as the blood of the righteous drenched the shale! He is a heretic! A witch! He must be burned in the lower market!"

Prelate Malakor nodded slowly, his cold eyes fixed on me.

"A grave indictment," Malakor murmured. "The Church of Sol-Aethel cannot suffer a sorcerer to command the legions of the March. How do you answer this holy champion, Theron Marrow?"

I looked at Evander. 

I looked at the trembling fingers concealed in his cuff, the fevered sweat glistening on his forehead, the shallow, frantic rise and fall of his chest. I had seen dozens of men like him in the throne room of the Red Keep—spoiled lords whose lies collapsed the moment real iron touched their skin.

A quiet chuckle escaped my throat.

The sound was sharp, dry, and arrogant, cutting through the heavy incense of the cathedral like a clean razor through silk.

"You laugh before the holy tribunal?" Malakor snapped, his brow furrowing with sudden anger.

"I laugh at the arithmetic, Your Holiness," I replied, turning my back on the dais to address the assembled magistrates and guild-masters in the nave. "Lord Montfort claims he charged the throat of Black-Mist Gorge with five hundred knights. He claims I quenched his light with sorcery and summoned two thousand frost-fiends."

I signaled toward the cathedral doors.

"Sergeant Kester," I called out. "Bring forward the evidence."

The heavy oak doors creaked open.

Six marcher veterans entered the nave, carrying two long pine stretchers draped in coarse wool blankets. Behind them walked twelve temple paladins—the very men Evander claimed had been slaughtered by my hand—led by a young knight whose right hand was wrapped in thick linen bandages soaked in yellow tallow.

The assembly went completely silent. The only sound was the rhythmic tread of boots upon the limestone flags.

The veterans set the stretchers down at the foot of the dais.

With a flick of my wrist, I pulled back the first blanket.

Lying upon the rough pine boards was the frozen corpse of an eighty-pound pack mule, its flanks sliced open, its belly swollen with rot. Beside it lay three open wicker crates packed with fine velvet tunics, silver-plated perfume flagons, and sixteen sealed jars of southern peach preserves.

"This is the holy vanguard’s baggage," I told the council, my voice ringing through the vaulted nave with absolute clarity. "Three days before the storm struck, Lord Montfort ordered forty sacks of coarse salt and two hundred bushels of seed oats thrown into the ditch at High Crag to make room for his winter wardrobe and scented bath oils."

Evander’s face turned the color of curdled milk. "Lies! Slander from a common thief!"

"Master Brand," I said, looking toward the Margrave’s clerk. "Read the quartermaster’s manifest signed by Lord Montfort’s own hand at the garrison gate."

Master Brand stepped forward, unrolling a sheepskin scroll with the official seal of the Marcher Treasury.

"On the fourth day of the frost-moon," Brand read in a loud, flat voice that echoed through the galleries, "Lord Evander Montfort requisitioned sixteen drays for temple luxuries, certifying under penalty of fine that the vanguard carried three weeks of dried rations. The inspection logs at Ashen-Crag show the vanguard arrived with zero flour, zero salt, and thirty cases of spiced wine."

A low, angry murmur spread through the guild-masters. Factor Vallow stepped forward, his round face dark with contempt.

"My carters reported the same," Vallow barked toward the prelate. "The boy starved his own outriders to haul his vanity cases."

"And what of the slaughter in the gorge?" Prelate Malakor demanded, leaning forward in his chair, his knuckles white against his golden ledger. "Two hundred good men died in the pass!"

"They died because their captain is a coward who runs like a kicked cur the moment iron meets iron," I answered coldly.

I reached out and brought the wounded young paladin forward to the steps.

"Show His Holiness your hand, Brother Dustin."

The young knight raised his right arm. He unwrapped the grease-stained linen.

His four fingers were gone, severed clean at the middle knuckle, the stumps blackened with frost-burn and sutured with coarse hemp thread.

"Tell the council who took your fingers, Brother Dustin," I commanded.

The young paladin looked up at Evander. His voice trembled with bitter, unvarnished rage.

"It was Lord Montfort," the boy said, his words falling like lead stones upon the marble floor. "When the frost-fiends broke our line at the narrow pass, Lord Montfort did not draw his greatsword. He drew his skinning knife. I grasped his saddle-leather, begging him to rally the line, and he hacked my fingers off with his blade so his horse could run free down the mountain. He left three companies in the snow and rode away alone."

A collective roar of outrage erupted from the galleries. 

Nobles rose from their seats; guild-masters shook their fists; the surviving temple paladins in the rear began to beat their sword scabbards against their greaves in open rebellion against their former commander.

Evander stepped back toward the prelate’s chair, his face contorted in sheer panic, his chest heaving as if he were drowning in air.

"He lies!" Evander shrieked, his voice breaking into a wild, cracked scream. "They are all in his pay! The Margrave is in his pay! The Goddess chose me! I am the Sun! I cannot be judged by common ditch-scum!"

Prelate Malakor looked down at the evidence, then at the furious faces of the Margrave’s council. The old priest's lips thinned into a pale line. He saw the shift in power; he knew that if the church pressed the charge of sorcery against the man who had just saved the northern frontier, the city watch would seize the cathedral’s tithe-barns before nightfall.

"The charge of sorcery..." Malakor began cautiously, raising a placating hand toward the crowd, "...appears to require further theological examination by the high chapter."

"No!" Evander screamed.

The boy had broken completely. The fragile illusion of his divine majesty, built upon cheap flattery and stolen victories, had shattered into dust before the eyes of the entire province. 

He drew his six-foot electrum greatsword from its ivory sheath.

The golden steel flashed in the candlelight with a high, ringing hiss that shocked the entire cathedral into silence. Drawing steel upon the holy dais was an act of sacrilege punishable by death.

"I will not be mocked by a carter!" Evander howled, pointing the glowing gold blade at my heart. "You stole my glory! You ruined my retinue! You turned the city against me! I invoke the Ancient Covenant of the Dawn! I demand the Right of Trial by Combat!"

The nave fell dead still.

Trial by Combat was the ancient, inviolable law of the province. No magistrate, no margrave, no high prelate could deny a challenge of blood once the holy gage was cast upon the stones.

Prelate Malakor closed his eyes, exhaling a slow, resigned breath. "The Right is invoked," the prelate whispered. "Tomorrow at high noon, upon the Great Forum of the Cathedral. The sun shall judge between you."

Evander stood on the dais, chest heaving, his mouth open in a feral, panting sneer, convinced that with his sacred solar magic unleashed in open combat, no mere mortal could withstand him.

I looked at the boy.

I did not draw my sword. I did not shout.

I reached up with my right hand, unbuckled the iron cuff of my gauntlet, and pulled the heavy leather-and-plate glove from my fingers.

I stepped forward to the lowest marble tier.

With a flick of my wrist, I tossed the iron gauntlet onto the stone floor. It struck the marble flags with a heavy, ringing clang that resonated through the vaults like an executioner's bell.

"High noon," I said.

I turned my back on the dais, walked past the silent ranks of templars, and stepped out of the cathedral into the cold, clean air of the courtyard.

Sergeant Kester fell in beside me as we descended the granite stairs.

"The boy is mad, Captain," Kester muttered, checking the catch on his crossbow. "He'll drink every holy elixir in the temple vaults before he steps into that ring."

"Let him drink the sun itself," I replied, pulling my woolen mantle tight across my chest. "Go to the armory. Have the smith grind the nick out of my arming blade and fit a fresh strap to my buckler. We have work to do tomorrow."

We crossed the snowy plaza toward the garrison mess, the cold wind blowing the smell of pine smoke across the city.
