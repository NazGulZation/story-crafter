# The First Cleave

The counting house of the Inland Mining Syndicate sat on the river wharf of Barrow-Gate, built of heavy grey granite to withstand spring floods.

Inside, the air was warm, smelling of cedar sawdust, tallow candles, dried parchment, and the bitter, metallic scent of silver coinage stacked in neat cedar boxes. Rain drummed steadily against leaded glass windows, blurring the masts of the peat barges rocking on the grey current of the river below.

Factor Vallow sat behind an imposing desk of dark bog-oak, sliding eight heavy silver marks across the green felt cloth.

Beside the eight marks lay an additional small pouch of soft pigskin.

"Eight marks for the escort contract, as agreed," Vallow said, resting his ringed fingers on the edge of the desk. "And five marks from the Merchants' Guild bounty board for the three stalker skulls Master Kroll brought down from the ridge."

Thirteen silver marks. Combined with my previous pit winnings, I held over twenty-five silver marks—five hundred copper crowns. 

I scooped the coins into my belt pouch, the metal clinking with a solid, satisfying weight.

"The iron ingots were delivered without a missing bar," I said, leaning casually against the oak chair opposite him. "Your garrison commander at Skittering Ridge was pleased."

"He was more than pleased," Vallow replied with a dry chuckle, dipping his quill into a square stone inkpot. "He was astonished that a three-wagon iron train passed through the Crags without losing a single mule or carter. In three years, that road has swallowed forty men and twelve wagons. The Grand Guild usually arrives three days late, demanding double pay for 'monster hazard.' You brought my iron through on schedule and left three dead beasts on the road."

He sanded his parchment, blew away the fine grey dust, and looked up at me with sharp, assessing eyes.

"The Merchants' Council met this morning," Vallow said. "The southern harvest grain fleet arrives in Barrow-Gate next week—sixteen barges from the lower valleys, carrying four hundred tons of winter rye, dried peas, and salt pork. Lord Carrow wants the granaries filled before the first heavy snowfall blocks the passes."

"Grain is life on the frontier," I remarked. "And where there is grain, there are bandits."

"Precisely," Vallow nodded. "The High Guild put forward Lord Evander Montfort to command the river escort. They demanded eighty gold sovereigns—a king's ransom. But the merchants remember what happened on the Crags. We are businessmen, Captain Marrow. We prefer iron discipline over golden plumes."

He pulled a fresh parchment scroll from an oak pigeonhole, unrolling it across the felt.

"We offer you the sole commission as Master-at-Arms for the River Convoy," Vallow said, pointing with the tip of his quill to the blank signature line at the bottom. "Forty silver marks for three weeks of work. You recruit twenty independent sellswords, inspect their gear, and command the river barges from the Lower Ward to the high storehouses. The contract is direct with the Syndicate. No guild interference. No church oversight."

Forty silver marks. That was the wage of a royal captain. 

More importantly, it gave me independent command over twenty armed men and four hundred tons of sovereign provisions. In Westeros, my father Tywin always taught me that a lord who controlled the grain controlled the swords. A hero in gilded armor could dance in tourneys all day, but if the men holding the walls had empty bellies, the hero would find himself standing alone in the snow.

"I accept," I said.

I took the quill from his hand, dipped it in the dark ink, and signed the parchment in a firm, elegant script: *Theron Marrow, Captain.*

Vallow glanced at the signature, his eyebrows lifting slightly at the refined, aristocratic hand. A common sheep-boy could barely scratch an 'X' in the dirt; my letters possessed the sharp, disciplined slant of a scholar trained at the Citadel of Oldtown. But the merchant was wise enough to ask no questions.

"Report to the River Basin at second bell tomorrow," Vallow said, rolling the parchment and pressing his signet wax onto the ribbon. "The barges will be waiting."

I took my leave, stepping out of the counting house into the Lower Wharves.

The rain was turning to fine, stinging sleet. 

I pulled my wide-brimmed kettle-helm low over my brow and walked along Tanner’s Row toward the Lower Market, intending to buy three dozen iron crossbow bolts and two casks of wagon grease for the river fleet.

As I passed the arched stone colonnade that divided the Lower Ward from the High Ward, voices echoed from the covered arcade.

"I don't care what the merchant factor said!" a woman's voice hissed, sharp and shrill with fury. "The contract was ours! We are the *Sun-Vanguard*! We are third-tier heroes!"

I paused beneath the shadow of a stone buttress, leaning my back against the wet granite, listening.

Standing on the covered marble porch of the Guild Registry were Evander Montfort, Renata Fell, and Seraphina Kestrel. 

The scene was far less glorious than their departure two days prior.

Evander’s golden plate had been scrubbed clean of mountain mud, but the white fur on his cloak was matted and stained yellow from horse sweat. His handsome face was tight with suppressed rage, his jaw clenched, his violet eyes glaring at a parchment manifest in his hand.

"They canceled the river contract," Evander spat, crumbling the parchment in his fist. "The Merchants' Council assigned the barges to Theron. That ditch-dog! That useless pack-mule!"

Renata leaned against a fluted marble pillar, her arms crossed tightly over her boiled leather chest. Her face was sullen, her lips curled in disgust.

"You left the road to chase a horned goat, Evander," Renata said, her voice dry and biting. "I warned you the hemlocks were moving. You didn't listen. You wanted a trophy for the Margrave's wall, and while you were chasing it, Theron butchered three stalkers and saved the iron."

"He got lucky!" Evander shouted, turning on her with wild eyes. "The beasts were broken by my solar light! A common ditcher cannot kill three crag-stalkers with a piece of scrap iron!"

"He did kill them," Renata shot back, taking half a step toward him, her scarred brow twitching. "I saw the cuts, Evander. One thrust through the brisket, one down through the brainpan. Clean, professional butchery. No wasted swings. The teamsters saw it, Vallow saw it, and now every merchant in Barrow-Gate knows the boy who held the wagons while the 'Hero' was lost in the rocks."

She held out her callused palm, snapping her fingers sharply under his nose.

"Pay me my share," Renata demanded. "Vallow docked twenty marks from the contract, and you still owe me forty coppers for the arrows I burned in the pass. Where is my silver?"

"You will have your silver when the High Temple releases the holy subsidy!" Evander snarled, knocking her hand aside.

"The temple is not releasing anything," Seraphina broke in from the arcade bench.

The sorceress looked dreadful. 

Her azure robes were wrinkled, her ivory pins askew, her skin the color of damp tallow. Her hands shook violently in her lap, her amber wand resting forgotten on the stone flags beside her boots. The bitter scent of burnt almond rolled off her breath in waves.

"The High Prelate was summoned by the Margrave’s council this morning," Seraphina murmured, her voice hollow and trembling. "They are auditing the temple’s war funds. My apothecary told me the price of distilled frost-drops has doubled. I have only two vials left, Evander. If I run out of frost-drops before the winter campaign, my mana core will seize. I will not be able to cast a spark, let alone a lightning lance."

"Then drink water, you spoiled witch!" Evander snapped, his aristocratic veneer completely cracking under the strain. "All of you, stop whining! I am the favored blade of Sol-Aethel! The Margrave’s niece invited me to the palace salon tonight! Once I secure the Lady Aurelia’s favor, we will have gold, titles, and all the silver in the province!"

A small, quiet figure emerged from the Registry doorway behind them.

Lyra Cole stepped onto the porch, clutching a wooden basket of dried herbs.

She wore her crimson wool gown, but the hem was caked with dried mud that had never been washed out, and the fox-fur mantle she had flaunted so proudly on the mountain hung limp and damp around her shoulders. Her face was pale, her amber eyes red-rimmed from crying.

"Evander..." Lyra said, her voice small, timid, and hesitant. "The... the landlord at the High Ward inn came by. He says our suite is three weeks in arrears. He says if we don't pay six silver marks by sunset, he will lock our trunks and throw our bedding into the alley."

Evander turned on her like a cornered dog.

"Shut your mouth, girl!" he roared, stepping toward her with his fist clenched.

Lyra flinched, shrinking back against the doorframe, her hands coming up to shield her face. 

"You spend your days weeping and buying sugar plums while the guild falls apart around our ears!" Evander sneered, looking down his nose at her with utter contempt. "You think you can question me? You are a common village herbalist who crawled out of a sheep sty! If you don't like the inn, go sleep in the stables with the other livestock!"

He spun on his gilded heel, his cloak whipping in the freezing wind, and stormed down the marble stairs toward the High Ward.

Seraphina let out a cold, bitter scoff, picked up her wand with trembling fingers, and glided away into the fog without glancing at Lyra.

Renata lingered for two heartbeats, looking down at the weeping girl on the porch. The skirmisher shook her head in silent disgust, spat onto the marble, and walked away toward the tavern quarter.

Lyra was left standing alone in the rain.

She sank onto the cold stone steps, her face buried in her hands, her shoulders heaving as sobs wracked her small frame. Her wooden basket had tipped over, spilling dried mountain herbs across the wet flags, their fragrance crushed beneath passing boots.

I stood in the shadow of the buttress, watching the scene unfold with the cold, unblinking detachment of a gargoyle on the walls of King's Landing.

There was no joy in the sight, and certainly no pity. 

A woman who sold herself for stolen silks and mocked an honest man's devotion deserved every ounce of the bitter broth she was now forced to swallow. Evander Montfort had never cared for her. To a vainglorious peacock, a companion was merely an accessory—a mirror meant to reflect his own imagined brilliance until a brighter glass appeared.

I turned my back on the porch, stepped into the muddy lane of Tanner’s Row, and headed toward the river wharves.

The river barges were waiting, and there was iron to be moved.
