# Blood on the Pine Needles

The crag-stalkers hit the scree with the clatter of a falling rockslide.

Three beasts broke from the hemlock scrub, bounding across the shale on six jointed legs tipped with black, obsidian-hard talons. They were eight feet long, built low to the ground like hunting lizards, their hides covered in grey, overlapping scales that mirrored the color of the granite crags. Their triangular heads bore no visible eyes—only clusters of heat-sensing pits above wide, tooth-filled jaws dripping viscous, milky venom that hissed against the frozen gravel.

Behind them in the timber, four more shapes slithered through the underbrush.

"Hold your fire!" I roared, standing atop the tongue of the lead ore cart. 

My voice cut through the teamsters' panic like an iron whip. 

Master Kroll had slammed the three flatbeds together against the granite cliffside, forming an unbroken wedge of heavy oak planks and iron ingots. The four draught mules stood crowded inside the pocket, their ears pinned back, shivering in terror. 

Beside me on the ore bed, two hired carters knelt with heavy stirrup-crossbows, their hands shaking so violently the iron bolts clattered in the brass tracks.

"Fifty yards!" the younger carter whimpered, his teeth chattering. "Captain, let loose! They're coming—"

"You loose now, and your bolt skips off their neck scales into the gorge," I said, my tone flat, steady, carrying the cold authority of a commander who had held the shield-wall at Pyke. "Crossbows punch through scale only on a flat angle. Wait until they hit the gravel flat thirty paces out. Aim for the throat where the jaw meets the brisket. One shot, then drop the bow and take up your bills."

Fifty paces. Forty paces. Thirty paces.

The lead stalker hit the flat road, its talons tearing gouges in the frozen clay, its jaws opening with a high-pitched, whistling shriek.

"Loose!" I barked.

*THWACK-THWACK.*

Two steel cords slapped home. 

The younger carter’s bolt went wide, splintering against a basalt boulder. But Kroll’s bolt—shot with forty years of hunting experience—took the lead stalker dead in the soft flesh beneath the chin.

The beast shrieked, flipping end-over-end across the shale, its six legs thrashing in the dirt, black blood spraying across the cart wheels.

The other two pressed forward without hesitation. 

They vaulted over their dying pack-mate, leaping twelve feet through the air toward the low side of the ore wagons. 

I was moving before their claws cleared the dirt.

I jumped down from the wagon tongue, planting my boots in the gravel three paces in front of the teamsters. I held my iron-rimmed buckler high against my chest, my arming sword held point-forward in the low ward—*iron gate*, Arthur Dayne called it. 

The second stalker landed in front of me with an earth-shaking thud, its tail whipping like an iron chain. 

Its jaws snapped at my face.

I stepped to the left, pivoting on the ball of my rear foot. The beast’s snout struck the iron face of my buckler with the impact of a falling log, sending a jarring shudder through my arm. 

I took the blow on the angle, guiding the creature’s snout into the gravel, and stepped into its flank.

My right arm fired like a coiled spring.

The three feet of honed iron drove forward in a clean, economic thrust, punching three inches behind the beast's foreleg where the rib plates parted. 

The blade sank to the crossguard. 

Hot, foul-smelling grease gushed over my gauntlet. The stalker convulsel, its six talons churning the gravel into a brown cloud. 

With a sharp twist of my wrist, I wrenched the steel free, stepping back before its thrashing tail could catch my shins. 

The beast collapsed, twitching in its own black bile.

"Behind you!" Kroll shouted.

The third stalker had bypassed me entirely, leaping onto the flatbed of the rear cart, snapping at the draught mules. 

One of the carters screamed, dropping his weapon and scrambling under the wagon bed. 

The beast clamped its jaws on the flank of a grey pack-mule, the mule braying in agonizing terror, thrashing against its leather traces.

Instinct took over. 

I sprang onto the wagon wheel, grabbed the iron lashing cable, and hauled myself onto the bed. 

The stalker sensed the movement and released the mule, whipping its horned head toward me with a wet, whistling hiss. It raised its two fore-claws, obsidian talons poised to gut me from throat to navel.

I threw my buckler straight into its heat-pits.

The ten-inch wooden disc hit the beast between the nostrils with a dull, heavy thwack. 

Blinded and disoriented for half a heartbeat, the creature struck blindly at the empty air where my head had been.

I slipped beneath its outstretched talons. 

Gripping the ash-wood hilt of my sword with both hands, I drove the point downward with the entire weight of my falling body.

The iron tip punched through the top of the creature's skull, cracking the grey bone plate like an eggshell. 

The blade sank down to the iron ring-pommel.

The beast shuddered once, stiffened into a rigid arch, and collapsed onto the stack of iron ingots, dead before its snout touched the wood.

Silence fell over the defile, broken only by the whistling mountain wind, the laboured breathing of the teamsters, and the whimpering of the bleeding mule.

I stood atop the ore bed, my boots slick with black ichor. 

My breathing came in short, measured draughts. My forearms ached, the muscles burning with exertion, but the cuts had been clean. Three beasts down in less than two minutes.

From the rocks above, the remaining four stalkers hissed in frustration, turning their scaly bodies and slithering back into the deep timber of the Grey Spires. They were predators; they hunted easy meat. When a pack lost three hunters in ninety seconds without securing a meal, they broke.

"Kroll," I said, wiping the black gore from my blade with a handful of clean sheep's wool. "Treat the mule’s flank. Wash the wound with salt water and pack it with pine pitch before the venom sets."

"Aye... aye, Captain," Kroll breathed, staring up at me from the ground with wide, reverent eyes. He crossed his fingers over his chest in a rough mountain blessing. "By the Anvil... you took down two of them with three feet of plain iron. I've seen full guilds of silver-rank heroes scream like wenches when those things jump the road."

"A sword is a tool," I replied, sliding the clean steel into its scabbard. "Use it properly, and it cuts."

A distant clatter of hooves echoed down the turnpike from the north.

Two minutes later, Evander Montfort came galloping back down the trail atop his white charger.

His grand entrance was somewhat diminished by his appearance.

The stallion was lathered in white sweat, its crimson velvet barding torn by briars and spattered with grey clay. Evander’s gilded plate armor was streaked with soot and loose shale; two feather plumes had been sheared from his helm; and his greatsword was caked in dried peat. 

He held no stag's head across his saddlebow.

Behind him rode Seraphina’s carriage, the horses blowing hard, followed by Renata and the four guild archers.

Evander reined in his charger beside our wagons, his horse snorting and sidestepping nervously as it caught the stench of stalker blood.

The hero's eyes took in the scene: the three dead stalkers lying in pools of black ichor, the wagon wedge, the drawn crossbows, and me standing quietly on the ore cart with blood-stained wool in my hand.

Evander’s handsome face flushed an angry, mottled red.

"What is the meaning of this?" Evander demanded, his booming voice straining for authority. "Why are the supply wagons halted?"

Renata rode forward, her keen hunter’s eyes scanning the carcass on the road and the tracks on the upper bank. She whistled softly through her teeth. "Crag-stalkers. A seven-beast pack. They hit the rear while we were chasing that stag up the spur."

She turned her gaze toward me, her brow furrowing with genuine, grudging surprise. "You killed these, Theron? Without a spear line?"

"He didn't kill them alone," Evander broke in loudly, his chest swelling as he looked around at the teamsters. "The pack was already breaking! My charge up the ridge split their vanguard! The beasts were panicked by my solar flare—they merely ran into your wagons while trying to flee my blade!"

The teamsters stared at him in dead, disgusted silence.

Master Kroll stepped forward, holding a bloody knife from dressing the mule's wound. He spat a stream of dark tobacco juice within two inches of Evander’s golden stirrup.

"Your solar flare was half a mile up the mountain chasing a deer, Lord Hero," Kroll growled, his voice thick with frontier venom. "While you were playing hunter in the rocks, these things dropped out of the hemlocks to butcher our teams. If Captain Marrow hadn't formed the wedge and put steel through their skulls, you'd be hauling these iron bars on your own pretty shoulders."

"Mind your tongue, carter!" Seraphina snapped, thrusting her pale face from the carriage window. Her hands were trembling with the mana-shakes, her eyes bloodshot. "You speak to a chosen champion of the Radiant Dawn! His vanguard clears the road for this entire province!"

"His vanguard left ten tons of syndicate iron to be slaughtered," a cold, smooth voice spoke from the shadows behind the carriage.

Factor Vallow rode forward on a tall grey gelding, accompanied by his two house billmen.

The merchant factor’s face was hard as flint. He looked at Evander, then at the three dead stalkers, and finally up at me.

"The contract with the Grand Guild stipulated vanguard protection for the entire convoy," Vallow said, his voice quiet, measured, and dripping with legal ice. "Instead, Lord Montfort abandoned his post to hunt wild game for his private trophy room. The syndicate’s cargo was preserved solely through the competence of my rear-guard captain."

Evander stiffened in his saddle, his knuckles whitening on his reins. "Factor Vallow, you forget yourself. The Goddess herself watches over my lance—"

"The Goddess does not sign promissory notes, Lord Montfort," Vallow cut him off coldly. "And she does not pay for dead mules. I am docking twenty silver marks from your guild voucher for breach of escort discipline. If you have an objection, you may take it up with the High Assize in Barrow-Gate."

Evander looked as though he had been slapped with a dead fish. His mouth opened, closed, and his violet eyes flared with murderous rage. 

Beside the carriage, Lyra Cole sat frozen on the box, staring at me with wide, terrified eyes. She clutched her grey fox mantle tight around her throat, but the fur looked thin and ridiculous in the cold mountain rain.

I offered no reply.

I jumped down from the ore cart, landing lightly on the gravel. I checked the injured mule's bandage, tightened the girth strap on my own shaggy mount, and swung into the saddle.

"The road is clear, Factor Vallow," I said to the merchant. "Skittering Ridge redoubt is two leagues ahead. If we move now, we make camp before full dark."

"Lead on, Captain Marrow," Vallow said with a respectful bow of his head.

I touched my heels to my mule's flanks and rode to the front of the ore carts, leaving the golden hero choking on mountain dust.
