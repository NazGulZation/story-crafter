# The Tourney of the Pale March

The grand tourney arena of Barrow-Gate was an oval bowl carved into the southern limestone terraces, broad enough to hold five thousand spectators and two hundred horses.

From the cedar grandstands hung the blue and black banners of Margrave Carrow, fluttering beside the crimson sunburst pennants of the Church of the Radiant Dawn. The air smelled of roasting pork sausages, crushed wild thyme, sweet funnel-cakes fried in lard, horse sweat, fresh sawdust, and the sharp copper tang of drawn steel. Brass horns blared from the stone gates, echoing off the mountain cliffs as heralds shouted the brackets from parchment rolls.

In the royal pavilion draped in purple velvet sat Margrave Carrow himself—a grey-bearded marcher lord in blackened plate, wearing a heavy gold chain of office. 

Beside him sat his niece, Lady Aurelia, wrapped in ermine furs, sipping spiced wine from a silver goblet. Behind them sat the High Prelate of Sol-Aethel in white silk robes embroidered with golden thread, his soft hands folded smugly over his jeweled staff.

I stood in the staging yard beneath the southern grandstand, adjusting the leather straps of my kettle-helm.

My arms were bare beneath my oiled brigandine, my sleeves rolled to the elbows. In my right hand, I held my plain three-foot arming sword, its edge freshly honed on Master Hal’s slate. On my left arm was strapped my round wooden buckler.

Beside me stood Deputy Brand, holding an iron pail of clean water and a linen towel.

"Thirty-two swords entered the ring this morning, lad," Brand said, leaning against the timber partition, his scarred brow beaded with sweat. "Four rounds down. Only four men left in the lists. You, that hedge-captain from the eastern border, an iron-fist monk from the mountains... and Renata Fell."

"Renata drew the quarter-final bracket," I said, taking a sip of water from the ladle and spitting it into the sawdust.

"Aye," Brand nodded, his grey eyes narrowing toward the practice ring across the yard. "She put two swordsmen in the dirt already. Sliced the tendons behind their knees before they could even raise their shields. She has that cat-footed wind-blessing on her boots. She moves faster than a struck viper."

"Speed is nothing without distance," I replied. "A viper dies when an ox steps on its neck."

A brass trumpet blared from the herald's dais.

"Bout nine!" the herald’s voice bellowed through an iron horn. "Theron Marrow, Captain of the River Guard... versus Renata Fell, Vanguard of the Sun!"

The crowd in the grandstands roared with bloodthirsty relish. 

They loved a grudge match. Word had spread through every tavern in the Lower Ward: the discarded porter who saved the iron convoy was facing the scout who had kicked his pack into the mud three weeks ago.

I stepped through the timber sally-port and onto the raked white sand of the central ring.

The afternoon sun blazed down from a cloudless azure sky, heating the sand beneath my boots. Across the twenty-pace circle stood Renata Fell.

She had stripped down for maximum agility. 

She wore tight, supple calfskin breeches, soft leather moccasins that made no sound on the sand, and a sleeveless tunic of dark boiled leather reinforced with small horn scales over her ribs. At her left hip hung a recurve hunting bow of laminated horn and sinew; in her hands she held two twenty-inch curved hunting blades forged of blued steel, their edges ground thin as shaving razors.

At her ankles, two silver rings pulsed with a pale green light—the *Gale Step*, a low-tier wind-blessing that buoyed her footing across the loose sand and quickened her sudden bursts by half. Her crooked nose wrinkled in a cocky, predatory sneer.

"Well, well, Theron," Renata said, twirling her twin blades in tight, flashing figure-eights before her chest. "Look at you in your big boy boots. You got lucky on the Crags because the stalkers were focused on the mules. But here, there are no wagons to hide behind."

"You didn't fix your footwork, girl," I said, lowering my center of gravity into a balanced, relaxed stance. "You're still springing from the toe. I told you an iron boot would catch you."

Renata’s eyes narrowed to slits. "I'll carve the liver out of your ribs before you clear your second step, plow-boy."

Margrave Carrow leaned forward in the royal box, raising his iron truncheon.

The brass horn sounded.

*BLAAARE.*

Renata moved like a dropped stone.

The green light around her ankles flared with a sharp, whistling hiss. The sand exploded beneath her boots as she accelerated across the twenty paces in three blinding heartbeats, closing the distance with unnatural, fluid speed. 

She came in low, angling to my left side, her twin daggers held in a reverse ice-pick grip meant to hook behind my buckler and sever my neck cords.

A classic skirmisher's attack: use supernatural acceleration to overwhelm the eye, get inside the long blade's reach, and gut the heavy fighter before he can bring his point to bear.

My feet remained rooted in the sand. 

I stood rock-still, watching her center of mass.

When a fighter moves at high speed, their direction is locked two paces before their foot strikes the ground. Physics remains physics, even with wind magic. She had committed her weight forward, her head down, her eyes fixed on my exposed collarbone.

At four feet, she leaped into her strike, both blued blades plunging downward in a scissor-cut.

I dropped six inches, sinking my weight onto my rear heel.

My left arm came up, not to block, but to thrust the iron rim of my buckler straight into the bridge of her oncoming ribs.

*THWACK.*

The impact was brutal. 

Renata’s supernatural forward momentum worked entirely against her. Her ribs slammed into the iron-rimmed ash wood with the force of an ox charging an iron gate. The breath exploded from her lungs in a wet, ragged gasp; the scissor-cut with her daggers flew wild, the blades whistling through empty air above my kettle-helm.

Before she could bounce back, my right foot snapped forward.

My heavy iron-capped riding boot stamped down squarely onto the instep of her right foot, pinning her moccasin deep into the sand.

Renata shrieked in pain, trapped in place.

I stepped inside her guard, bringing the flat of my steel blade down across her right wrist.

*CRACK.*

The bone snapped with the sound of a dry branch underfoot. Her right dagger tumbled into the sand.

With my left hand, I seized the front of her boiled leather collar, yanking her forward until her face was three inches from mine. 

Her dark eyes were wide with blind, suffocating terror, her lips flecked with white spit as she struggled for air through shattered ribs.

"You said I never had the belly for blood, girl," I whispered into her ear, my voice cold, quiet, and completely devoid of mercy. 

I hooked my right hand behind her neck, spun her around, and drove my knee straight into the small of her back. 

Renata crashed facedown into the hot white sand, her remaining dagger flying ten paces across the ring. 

She reached frantically for the laminated hunting bow slung across her shoulders, her fingers clawing at the horn grip in a desperate attempt to bring up a weapon.

I planted my right boot on her shoulder blade, pinning her flat to the earth.

I reached down, took the laminated horn bow in both hands, placed the center across my raised knee, and heaved.

*SNAP.*

The hundred-pound recurve bow splintered in half, the horn cores popping with the sound of a pistol shot, the silk string whipping through the air like a broken rein. 

I dropped the shattered pieces onto her bleeding face.

"Three heartbeats," I told the crowd, resting the flat of my blade against the nape of her neck. "Take her out of the sand."

The five thousand spectators in the grandstands sat frozen in dead, breathless silence.

Not a single shout. Not a single cheer. 

A silver-rank vanguard scout—blessed with the speed of the wind, undefeated in five arena bouts—had crossed the sand in a blaze of green light, only to be dismantled, broken, and disarmed in less than four seconds by a man who barely moved two paces.

Up in the royal pavilion, Margrave Carrow rose slowly from his velvet chair. 

The old marcher lord stared down at the sand, his weathered jaw slack, his grey eyes wide with shock. Beside him, Lady Aurelia lowered her wine goblet, a slow, radiant smile curving her red lips. 

Across the pavilion, the High Prelate’s jeweled staff slipped from his soft fingers, clattering against the marble tiles.

The herald finally found his voice, his throat cracking as he shouted into the horn:

"Winner... by total disarm and submission... Captain Theron Marrow!"

The silence broke. 

A roar erupted from the lower grandstands—the teamsters, the carters, the miners, and the sellswords jumping atop the wooden benches, screaming my name till their voices cracked. 

"Marrow! Marrow! The Iron Captain!"

Two temple bailiffs scrambled into the ring with a canvas stretcher, dragging the weeping, broken form of Renata Fell out through the side gates. Her wind-rings flickered and died, leaving only streaks of blood and tears in the white sand.

I ignored the roar. 

I wiped a speck of sweat from my brow with the back of my gauntlet, turned my eyes toward the upper tunnel, and looked.

Standing in the shadow of the competitor's archway was Evander Montfort.

The hero had won his own semi-final bout an hour earlier, incinerating a hedge knight's shield with a theatrical solar blast. He stood clad in his mirror-polished gilded armor, his white cloak billowing behind him, his six-foot greatsword resting against his shoulder.

His face was the color of fresh ash. 

His violet eyes were fixed on the shattered pieces of Renata’s bow lying in the sand. His jaw worked, his teeth grinding together so hard the muscle at his temple twitched uncontrollably.

He had watched every beat. 

He had seen the economy of my footwork. He had seen how easily I turned supernatural speed into shattered bone. And for the first time since he had received the blessing of Sol-Aethel, the golden boy realized that he stood before an apex predator.

He was facing an executioner.

I lifted my sword toward him in a slow, elegant, mocking salute.

Then, I turned on my heel and walked into the cool shade of the sally-port to await the final call.
