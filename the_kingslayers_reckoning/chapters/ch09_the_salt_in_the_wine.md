# The Salt in the Wine

The armory quarter on Black Ditch smelled of quenching brine, charcoal soot, sulphur, and the rhythmic ring of heavy hammers striking red-hot iron.

It was two days before the Midsummer Tourney. 

Every anvil in Barrow-Gate was singing. Armorers' apprentices carried glowing billets from forge to trough; mercenary cutthroats bartered over second-hand brigandines; and tourney knights tested the temper of tournament lances against wooden butts set along the river wall.

I sat in the open lean-to of Master Hal’s smithy, resting on an overturned nail keg.

Master Hal was sixty winters old, with arms like twisted ash boughs and a blind white left eye taken by a flying iron splinter thirty years ago. He was an honest smith. He cared nothing for gilded filigree or temple crests; he judged steel by the ring of the blade and the grain of the carbon.

Across my knees lay my arming sword.

Beside my thigh on the bench sat a small terracotta oil jar and my slate whetstone.

A shadow fell across the dirt floor of the lean-to.

A boy of fourteen, dressed in the grease-stained smock of an apprentice leather-worker, stepped into the shed. He held a small stone jar sealed with yellow bees-wax. His fingers were nervous, his brown eyes darting between my face and the heavy iron tongs in Master Hal’s hands.

"Captain Marrow?" the boy asked, his voice squeaking slightly.

I looked up, my hand resting casually near my pommel. "Speak."

"A messenger came to the Saddler's Yard," the boy stammered, extending the small jar with both hands. "Said it was from Deputy Brand at the Bailiff’s Gate. Special whale-oil from the southern wharves, mixed with fine emery dust. Said it keeps steel sharp through twenty bouts without heating the blade. For the tourney."

I looked at the stone jar.

The yellow beeswax seal was stamped with a crude imitation of the town tower device. But the clay jar was high-fired porcelain, imported from the eastern cities—far too expensive for a border bailiff who chewed dried tobacco and drank four-copper ale.

"Set it on the bench," I said smoothly.

The boy set the jar down with trembling haste, bowed awkwardly, and bolted down the alleyway as though hounds were on his heels.

Master Hal paused in his hammering, pushing his leather apron aside with a grimy forearm. He squinted at the jar with his good eye. "Brand sending imported oil? That old skinflint wouldn't give grease to a dying hinge."

"Brand didn't send it," I said.

I picked up the porcelain jar. 

I broke the yellow wax seal with the tip of my hunting knife and lifted the cork stopper.

I brought the jar to within six inches of my nose and took a shallow, cautious breath.

The oil was dark, viscous, scented with sweet almond to mask its true nature. But beneath the almond lay the sharp, cold, metallic sting of boiled aconite root—monkshood. 

In Westeros, the alchemists called it *widow’s blood* or *wolf-bane*. Rubbed onto steel or soaked into leather gloves, the poison seeped through the pores of the skin within twenty heartbeats. It caused no immediate pain, no swelling, no discoloration. It merely paralyzed the motor nerves of the forearm, turning five strong fingers into useless, numb sausages that could not hold a wooden spoon, let alone an arming sword.

If I had used that oil on my whetstone, my sword hand would have been dead to the world the second my skin touched the hilt on tourney morning.

*Cersei used wolfsbane on her rivals' riding gloves,* I remembered with a quiet, icy smirk. *Evander thinks he invented court murder.*

I replaced the cork. 

I looked through the open slats of the lean-to into the narrow alley across the street.

Standing behind a stack of seasoned cedar firewood was a lean man in a ragged grey cloak, his hood pulled low over his eyes. He had been watching the smithy for half an hour, waiting to see if I opened the jar.

"Master Hal," I said, setting the jar into my belt pouch. "I will be back for the scabbard chape in half an hour."

"Aye, lad," the smith grunted, turning back to his bellows. "Mind the shadows in the Ditch. The tourney brings every cutpurse north of the pale."

I stepped out of the lean-to, turning left toward the river wharves.

I walked with a slow, heavy gait, letting my left shoulder droop slightly, playing the weary soldier carrying a heavy purse. 

Behind me, thirty paces back, the grey cloak followed.

The Black Ditch was a maze of narrow, winding lanes running between windowless tanneries and timber storehouses. The ground was slick with black mud and rotting offal; the river fog rolled in thick from the lower basins, muffling the sound of the hammers behind us.

I turned into the alley of the Salt Racks—a dead-end lane lined with wooden frames used for curing river fish.

I stopped at the far end, facing the blank granite wall of the town granary.

The footsteps behind me quickened. 

The man in the grey cloak rounded the corner, pulling an eight-inch bone-handled pig-sticker from his sleeve, moving with the quiet, predatory confidence of an alley cutthroat closing in on an easy mark.

"Turn around, ditch-boy," the cutpurse whispered, his yellow teeth bared in a sneer. "Hand over that purse, and maybe you live to see the lists—"

I turned.

I was not holding my purse. 

I was standing four feet from him, balanced on the balls of my feet, my arming sword resting point-forward in my right hand, steady as an iron bar.

The cutpurse’s eyes went wide. The sneer melted from his mouth.

He lunged with a desperate, frantic thrust at my throat.

He had speed, but zero form. He led with his chin, his weight falling forward onto his front toe.

I stepped to the right, sweeping my buckler across his forearm.

*SLAP.*

The iron rim struck his wrist, knocking the pig-sticker into the mud. 

Before he could pull his arm back, my left hand shot out like an iron clamp, seizing his wrist, twisting his arm upward behind his back in a brutal joint-lock.

*SNAP.*

The cutpurse shrieked, falling to his knees in the black filth, his elbow dislocated, his shoulder pinned against the damp granite stones of the wall.

"Quiet," I whispered into his ear, resting the cold flat of my blade against his jugular. "Scream again, and I take your ears off."

The man choked back his agony, sobbing into the muck, his forehead pressed against the wet stones. "Mercy... mercy, master... I was only paid... I was only hired..."

"Who gave you the porcelain jar?" I asked, my voice calm as falling snow.

"A paladin!" the cutpurse gasped, tears of pain cutting tracks through the soot on his cheeks. "Sir Kenneth... from the Temple of the Sun! He paid me three silver marks at the Red Lantern tavern! Said to give it to the boy to deliver to you! Said it was just a prank to make you drop your blade on the sands!"

"A very amusing prank," I murmured.

I reached into my belt pouch with my free hand, pulled out the stone jar of aconite oil, and held it before his terrified eyes.

"Sir Kenneth has horse-grooms, does he not?" I asked softly.

"Aye... aye, at the White Stallion livery..."

"Sir Kenneth rubs sweet oil on Lord Evander’s tournament lances and leather gauntlets every morning before the lists," I noted. "A good squire keeps his master’s gear supple."

I pulled a second small clay jar from my pouch—the ordinary mutton tallow I used for waterproofing my boots. 

I switched the wax seals with my thumb. 

I dropped the jar of wolfsbane oil into the cutpurse's ragged pocket.

"You will take this jar to Sir Kenneth’s stable tonight," I told him, pressing the steel an eighth of an inch deeper into his neck. "You will tell him the boy failed to find me, and that you stole it back so he could use it on Lord Montfort’s practice gauntlets instead. You will do it cleanly. If one drop touches your tongue, you will choke on your own spit before midnight."

"I'll do it! By the gods, I'll do it!" the man whimpered, trembling violently.

"If you flee the town, I will find you," I added, my tone devoid of heat, carrying the cold certainty of an absolute promise. "If you speak to the watch, I will find you. Now walk."

I released his arm.

The cutpurse collapsed into the mud, clutching his dislocated elbow to his chest with a whimpering gasp. He scrambled to his feet, slipping twice on the wet offal, and fled down the alley like a scalded cur.

I wiped a fleck of street mud from my leather brigandine with my knuckle.

A lion did not merely kill snakes; he turned their fangs back into their own tails. 

If Evander Montfort wanted poison on the lists, he was welcome to sample his own brew.

I turned on my heel and walked back to Master Hal’s smithy to finish my scabbard.
