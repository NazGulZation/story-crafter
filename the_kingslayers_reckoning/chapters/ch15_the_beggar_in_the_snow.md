# The Beggar in the Snow

The command pavilion was an island of iron, heat, and canvas in the middle of a frozen mountain wasteland.

Two sheet-iron stoves burned with dry birch logs, glowing dull cherry-red in the corners, venting white smoke through tin flues that poked through the heavy oiled canvas roof. The air inside smelled of boiled spruce tea, roasting salt pork, damp wool, weapon oil, and the sharp, coppery stink of blood drying on surgical saws. Outside, the blizzard howled like a pack of starved wolves, driving sleet against the tent walls in rattling volleys.

I sat on a wooden campaign stool beside the central map table, my leather brigandine unbuckled to let the heat reach my ribs.

On my left forearm, the camp chirurgeon had applied a poultice of comfrey and lard to the bruised bone where the alpha brute’s club had struck my buckler. In my right hand, I held an iron cup of mulled wine, the sweet steam warming my face.

Across the tent sat Sergeant Kester and Master Kroll, checking the casualty rolls by tallow candlelight.

"Eighteen conscripts killed in the breach," Kester reported, his voice low and steady. "Twenty-four wounded, mostly broken collarbones and claw-scratches. But the gorge is held, Captain. Not a single wagon was looted. The baggage train is secure."

"And the survivors of the temple vanguard?" I asked.

Kroll spat into an iron spittoon beside the stove. "Sixty-two of them. We disarmed them, stripped their horses for the meat wagons, and put them in the fourth tent line. The cowards are still weeping into their blankets."

The canvas flap of the tent was pulled back, admitting a blast of sub-zero wind and swirling snow.

Two marcher guards in kettle-helms entered, dragging a woman between them.

It was Lady Seraphina Kestrel.

The proud archmage of the High Academy was unrecognizable. 

Her indigo silk robes were torn to shreds, blackened by soot, and soaked in foul mountain mud. Her sleek dark hair hung in frozen, rat-tailed strands around a face that looked like an eighty-year-old crone’s—sunken, jaundiced, her lips cracked and bleeding. Her amber wand was gone, snapped in half when she fell from her horse.

She collapsed onto the frozen earth floor, shivering uncontrollably, her arms wrapped around her chest as violent tremors wracked her limbs. 

"Drops..." Seraphina whimpered, her voice a cracked, wheezing rattle. "Please... by the Light... give me the frost-drops... my core is burning... my veins are ice..."

"She has the mana-shakes, Captain," Kroll muttered, shaking his head. "Her body has burned out its channels. She will never cast a spark again. She's just a magistrate’s runaway brat who traded her wits for blue glass."

"Give her hot water and bread," I told the guards. "Put her in the medical dray with the other camp cripples. If she survives the night, she can scrape turnips for the stew pots."

The guards dragged the sobbing, shivering sorceress out into the storm.

Before the canvas flap could close, a third guard entered, pushing another figure through the doorway.

The woman stumbled forward and fell hard onto her hands and knees in the slush near my boots.

It was Lyra Cole.

The sight of her was an exhibition in the bitter comedy of the world.

Her expensive crimson wool gown was shredded at the knees, the fine golden wheat-stalk embroidery caked in brown horse manure and blackened mud. Her mantle of grey fox fur—the forty-silver-mark prize she had flaunted so proudly on the mountain ledge—was soaked in freezing water, matted with filth, smelling of wet dog and rotten lard. 

Her hands were bare, the skin of her fingers blackened by the first stages of frostbite, her fingernails cracked and bleeding from clawing at the frozen rocks when Evander’s baggage dray had overturned.

She lay in the slush for three long heartbeats, gasping for air, her shoulders heaving with ragged sobs.

Then, she raised her head.

Her eyes were red-rimmed, wild with hysterical terror, her face streaked with dirt and frozen tears. When she looked up and saw me sitting on the campaign stool—warm, whole, wearing the iron gorget of the Commissary-General, surrounded by veteran captains who jumped at my command—something broke inside her chest.

A high, strangulated sob tore from her throat.

Remaining on the frozen earth, she dragged herself forward on her knees.

Through the muddy slush of the tent floor, Lyra Cole crawled toward my boots.

"Theron..." she wept, her voice cracking into a desperate, shrill shriek that silenced the tent. "Theron... oh gods, Theron, look at me!"

She reached out with her trembling, frostbitten hands. 

Her blackened fingers clamped around the leather ankles of my riding boots, her forehead pressing down onto the iron-capped toes, her hot tears soaking into the mud on my leather.

"Theron, please!" she wailed, clutching my boots as though they were the last timbers of a sinking ship. "Forgive me! Please, by the gods, forgive me! I made a mistake... I was blind, I was foolish, I was bewitched!"

Sergeant Kester and Master Kroll watched from the table in dead, disgusted silence.

I kept my boots planted, sitting quietly, looking down at the weeping creature on the floor with the cold, aristocratic detachment of a lord looking down at a stray mongrel that had crawled under his banquet table.

"Bewitched, were you?" I murmured, taking a slow sip of my mulled wine.

"Yes! Yes!" Lyra screamed, looking up with a face contorted by desperate, frantic pleading. Snot and tears bubbled on her upper lip, freezing in the draft from the doorway. "Evander... he used magic on me! He forced me into his bed! He gave me foul potions that clouded my mind! I never wanted him, Theron! I swear it on my mother’s grave, I never wanted him!"

She gripped my knees, her shivering body pressing against my boots, desperate to find a single spark of the boy’s old, blind devotion in my eyes.

"You loved me, Theron!" she sobbed, her words tumbling over one another in a frantic torrent. "Remember our village? Remember the sheep meadows in the spring? Remember when you carried me across the brook when I twisted my ankle? You told me you would build me a house with an oak hearth! You told me you would protect me forever!"

Her fingers clawed at my leather breeches, trying to pull herself up toward my lap.

"Look at me, Theron! I am your Lyra! I am your little bird! Take me back... please, take me back! I will be your woman! I will sleep on the dirt by your bed! I will wash your feet with my hair! I will cook your porridge and mend your shirts! Just don't cast me out! Don't leave me in the snow!"

The phantom memories of Theron Marrow stirred faintly in the dark corners of my mind.

The boy remembered the brook. He remembered the small, freckle-faced girl who had shared his dry bread when their fathers were broke. He remembered the sweet, aching devotion of a nineteen-year-old heart that had believed in love, honor, and loyalty.

And then, I remembered the tavern yard of *The Gilded Boar*.

I remembered the freezing autumn downpour. I remembered the three clipped zinc farthings splashing into the brown puddle. 

I remembered her high, mocking chime of laughter echoing off the timber eaves before forty drunken mercenaries:

*While you were out there freezing in the dark, counting oat sacks and checking axle grease, I was naked in his furs. He took me three times a night, screaming his name into the wool, while you shivered by the ash heap like a blind mule. We laughed at you, Theron. We laughed until our ribs ached.*

The memory was clear as glass.

I reached down with my right hand.

My fingers—strong, whole, living fingers that had held the hilt of a knight’s sword through forty years of war—grasped her wrist.

I squeezed.

The pressure clamped down with the relentless weight of an iron vise.

Lyra’s breath hitched in her throat. The frantic torrent of lies stopped. She looked up into my face, her amber eyes wide with sudden, suffocating realization.

There was no boy behind these eyes.

There was no soft, wounded peasant who could be moved by tears, guilt, or childhood memories. 

Looking back at her was Ser Jaime Lannister—the golden lion who had looked into the mad eyes of King Aerys Targaryen while King's Landing burned, who had watched the high lords of Westeros grovel and scheme, who knew that the most vicious hypocrites in the world were the weak who traded loyalty for silk the moment a brighter master appeared.

"I am not Theron Marrow, little bird," I told her, my voice quiet, smooth, and colder than the mountain ice outside the canvas. "And even if I were, a lion does not rut with ditch-refuse."

Lyra’s jaw dropped. Her face turned the color of lead.

"You told the entire tavern yard how you took the golden lance while the boy froze on sentry duty," I said, letting every syllable drop into the silence of the tent like a falling hammer. "You laughed until your ribs ached. You boasted of his goose-down mattress, his scented tapers, and his forty-silver furs. You spat on his bread. You kicked his pack into the horse trough."

I leaned down slightly, my grey eyes burning into hers.

"You wanted silk, little bird. You wanted warm furs. You wanted a hero with gold lace in his hair. Well, your hero galloped down the south road alone, covered in his own filth, leaving five hundred men to die in the rocks."

I released her wrist. 

The girl slumped back into the slush, her hands limp in her lap, her body trembling with a cold that no fire could warm.

"Please..." she whispered, a broken, dry whimper escaping her trembling lips. "Please, Theron... what will become of me?"

"You will live," I said, leaning back on my campaign stool and taking another sip of my mulled wine. "Execution is far too clean for a traitor. You will live to remember every choice you made."

I looked over at Master Kroll.

"Kroll."

The old teamster rose from the table, his scarred face grim and unsparing. "Aye, Captain?"

"Put an iron shovel in her hands," I ordered. "Strip that ruined fox fur from her shoulders and give her a sackcloth smock. From this night forward, she belongs to the fourth baggage division."

Lyra’s head snapped up in horror. "No... no, please..."

"She will shovel mule dung from the wagon lines every morning at dawn," I continued, ignoring her whimpers. "She will scrub the grease from the axle boxes with sand and river water. She will sleep in the third flour cart with the other baggage scrubs, and she will eat the burnt porridge from the bottom of the pot. If she complains, withhold her bread. If she refuses to shovel, leave her on the road."

Kroll smiled—a tight, vicious grimace that showed his yellowed teeth. 

"With pleasure, Captain. We have forty mules with the belly-flux on the rear line. She won't lack for work."

Kroll stepped forward, seized the collar of Lyra’s ruined crimson gown with one massive, callused hand, and hauled her to her feet like a sack of wet flour.

"Get up, girl," Kroll grunted, dragging her toward the tent flap. "Your days of velvet and lavender soap are finished. Time to learn what real work smells like."

Lyra surrendered to the drag, offering no resistance, no scream. 

She let herself be dragged through the slush, her boots trailing limp in the dirt, her eyes hollow, staring at the floorboards in the catatonic stupor of a creature whose soul had been hollowed out by her own greed.

The canvas flap closed behind them, sealing the howling blizzard outside.

Silence returned to the command pavilion, save for the crackle of the birch logs in the iron stoves.

Sergeant Kester let out a low, appreciative whistle, pouring himself a cup of spruce beer from the kettle. 

"A hard judgment, Captain," Kester murmured. "Fair, but hard as iron."

"Call it an accounting, Sergeant," I replied, setting my empty wine cup on the oak table and picking up the army muster slate. "A Lannister pays his debts. And a traitor pays hers."

I finished calculating the grain rations, setting the chalk beside the tallow candle, and pulled my wolf-skin mantle tight across my shoulders.

I stepped out of the command pavilion into the bitter mountain dark.

The gale had died to a steady, whispering snowfall, laying a clean blanket of white over the blood-soaked shale of the defile. Along the wagon lines, fifty campfires flickered behind the iron revetment, the smell of roasting mule meat and pine smoke drifting through the freezing air.

Ten paces away, by the rear draught lines, Lyra Cole stood ankle-deep in frozen muck. 

The ruined crimson gown had been stripped away, replaced by a coarse sackcloth smock tied with rough hemp twine. In her blistered, blackened hands she clutched a heavy iron spade, shivering violently as she shoveled steaming dung into a wooden wheelbarrow under the watchful eye of a scarred teamster. When she paused to blow on her bleeding knuckles, the carter barked a curse, tapping the iron rim of his whip against the wagon rail.

She looked up. Through the falling snow, her hollow, tear-stained eyes caught mine across the firelight.

There was no pride left in her. No silk. No laughter. Only the crushing, inescapable reality of the life she had chosen.

I kept walking. 

I checked the scabbard at my hip, gave the midnight countersign to the picket sergeant at the perimeter trench, and turned my eyes toward the northern pass. 

Tomorrow, we would take Ashen-Crag.
