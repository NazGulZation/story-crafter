# The Siege of the High Bastion

The Grand Colonnade leading to the Margrave’s Keep was a killing ground of slick limestone flags, burning barricades, and drifting sleet.

High above the avenue, the bronze alarm bells of the North Watchtower clanged in frantic, discordant double-strokes, their clamor echoing off the basalt facades of the aristocratic manors. Freezing rain whipped through the stone arches in howling gusts, hissing against the burning pitch-barrels that the temple paladins had overturned across the roadway. The air tasted of wet soot, acrid sulfur smoke, iron filings, and the coppery stench of spilled blood cooling on freezing stone.

"Hold the shields!" Master Kroll’s roar cut through the din like an iron hammer. "Plant the spikes! Don't let the horses gather stride!"

At the mouth of the Colonnade, two hundred smelter-guild veterans had locked their four-foot iron-plated pavises together in a triple hedge of seasoned oak and steel. 

Behind them, eighty marcher pikemen braced twelve-foot ash-wood shafts at a thirty-degree angle, their heavy iron butt-spikes driven into the joints between the paving stones. The steel points bristled in the torchlight, waiting for the charge.

Down the broad basalt incline swept the pride of the Dawnguard.

Forty heavy cavalrymen on armored southern barded chargers, their silvered plate gleaming through the swirling smoke, their twelve-foot lances dipped toward our center. At their head rode Brother Dustin, the Inquest's captain of horse, screaming holy curses into the wind.

I stood on the raised pedestrian balustrade twenty paces behind the pike-wall, my plain arming sword resting point-down against the masonry, watching the cavalry gather speed.

"Wait for the dip," I said quietly to Sergeant Kester, who knelt beside me with six marksmen.

"They are at fifty paces, Captain," Kester warned, his knuckles white against his stirrup-crossbow.

"Wait," I commanded. 

In the Riverlands, twenty years ago, Robb Stark had baited my cavalry into a narrow defile between wooded ridges. I had charged straight into his trap, drunk on my own arrogance, believing golden armor and courage could shatter an iron line. My horse had died beneath me, five loyal Lannister cousins were butchered at my side, and I spent a year in chains eating crusts in the dark. A charge over uneven ground looked glorious from the saddle; from the ground, it was a butcher's arithmetic of balance, momentum, and iron.

At thirty paces, the lead chargers hit the caltrop line.

Two hours earlier, twelve apprentice smiths had scattered three hundred four-pronged iron caltrops across the depression where the road dipped beneath the aqueduct arch. 

The heavy iron points pierced through the frogs and soft horn of the chargers' hooves.

The front rank collapsed in an instant, violent heap.

Three great warhorses shrieked in agony, their front legs folding beneath them, skidding on their chests across the wet limestone flags with the momentum of twenty tons of armor and bone. Riders were catapulted through the air, their silvered plate shattering against the pavement; trailing horses tripped over the fallen mounts, piling into a screaming, thrashing mountain of broken limbs, bent lances, and dying beasts.

"Crossbows!" I roared. "Loose into the tangle!"

From the rooftops of the flanking merchant mansions, forty watchmen rose from behind the stone cornices.

Steel quarrels rained down into the stalled cavalry in a devastating, point-blank volley. 

Quarrels punched through shoulder joints, gorgets, and saddle-trees. The silver-plated horsemen, trapped in the narrow avenue between the stone walls, could neither advance nor wheel their blown mounts.

"Pikes advance!" I commanded, leaping down from the balustrade, my arming blade clearing its scabbard with a sharp, ringing hiss. "Step and thrust! Clear the avenue!"

The smelter-phalanx moved forward like an iron wall, the heavy pavises grinding across the stones with an unyielding roar. Pikes thrust in synchronized cadences into the writhing mass of cavalry, finishing the work with cold, mechanical efficiency.

Deputy Brand fought at my left, his heavy billhook hooking a dismounted knight by the neck-guard, dragging the man to the flagstones where a smelter's sledge caved his breastplate inward with a single strike.

I parried a desperate thrust from a temple sergeant, stepped inside his reach, and drove my steel crossguard into his nose. 

The nasal bone gave way with a wet crunch. 

As the man stumbled backward, I severed the artery in his right thigh with a clean, low passing cut, stepping past his falling body without breaking my stride.

"Captain!" Kroll shouted, spitting blood from a split lip as he kicked a dented shield aside. "The keep! The gatehouse is breached!"

I looked up toward the inner bailey of the Margrave’s Keep three hundred paces ahead.

The heavy iron portcullis had been raised. Smoke poured from the lower arched windows of the fortress, accompanied by the frantic, echoing screams of servants and the sound of iron blades clashing in the Great Hall.

Through the gatehouse galloped Evander Montfort.

He was dismounted now, surrounded by sixty elite Dawnguard infantry clad in full field plate, their tabards stained with blood. Evander’s electrum greatsword blazed with a sickly, sulfurous yellow halo, trailing smoke as he led his zealots straight toward the private family chambers where the paralyzed Margrave lay.

"Justinian sent him to finish the lord," Brand panted, wiping gore from his billhook. "If they slit the Margrave's throat, the lawful council dies with him!"

"Their design runs far deeper than assassination," I said, catching the sweet, chemical smell of burning turpentine and quicklime drifting on the gale.

The Liquid Sun.

Seraphina’s warning rang through my memory like a funeral bell: *Twelve barrels stacked in the dry cellar directly beneath the high foundation pillars. If the fuses burn through, the explosion will collapse the central keep into the valley.*

"Kroll! Take the phalanx and secure the courtyard!" I roared, pointing my blade toward the open gatehouse. "Bar the outer bailey! Do not let another temple bastard leave this fortress alive!"

"Aye, Captain!" Kroll bellowed, waving his bloody sledge to rally the pikemen.

I turned to Sergeant Kester and six picked smelter-hands armed with iron pry-bars and water buckets. "With me. The beer cellars."

We sprinted across the blood-slick cobbles of the outer ward, bypassing the clashing lines in the Great Hall, diving into the narrow stone staircase that spiraled downward beneath the northern buttresses.

The air in the stairwell grew rapidly hotter with every step.

By the second landing, the smell of damp stone was completely obliterated by the choking stench of burning quicklime and tar. A faint, dancing orange light flickered across the curved walls, accompanied by the rhythmic, spitting hiss of burning powder-fuses.

We hit the bottom landing and burst into the Great Undercroft.

The chamber was a massive subterranean crypt of twelve square granite pillars that held up the entire weight of the keep’s residential tower. 

In the center of the vault, stacked beneath the main load-bearing pillar, sat twelve heavy oak casks bound with blackened copper bands. 

Three braided hemp ropes, thick as a man’s wrist and coated in grey sulfur paste, trailed across the dry dirt floor from the barrels toward an iron brazier ten paces away. 

Kneeling beside the brazier was Brother Dustin, the Inquest's chief custodian, clutching an iron torch in trembling, soot-stained hands. Around him stood four fanatic temple acolytes armed with short-swords, their eyes wild with religious fervor.

"The holy fire cleanses all!" Dustin was screaming, raising the torch toward the braided sulfur fuse. "The light shall judge the wicked!"

"Shoot him!" I bellowed.

Sergeant Kester raised his stirrup-crossbow and squeezed the trigger.

The cord snapped. The steel quarrel took Brother Dustin dead through the throat.

The custodian’s shriek was cut short into a wet gargle. He dropped backward onto the dirt, the iron torch slipping from his fingers.

The torch landed dead across the center fuse.

*FZZZZT.*

A bright, crackling line of white-hot sparks erupted along the sulfur braid, hissing and spitting as it raced along the floor toward the copper-bound barrels.

"Kill the heretics!" the four acolytes shrieked, lunging across the crypt with raised short-swords to protect the fuse.

I did not stop to duel them.

"Take them!" I shouted to Brand and the smelter-hands.

I sprang forward, my boots kicking up clouds of dry dirt, clearing the six paces between the brazier and the burning fuse in two desperate strides.

An acolyte stepped into my path, thrusting with a broad hunting blade.

I did not parry with steel. 

I smashed my wooden buckler directly into his face, shattering his teeth and hurling him sideways into the stone pillar. 

The burning spark was three feet from the first barrel bung. 

The quicklime coating was already bubbling, releasing a sweet, toxic vapor that made my lungs burn as though filled with boiling oil.

I threw myself flat onto the dirt.

My left arm, protected by the heavy boiled leather of my vambrace, slammed down across the burning fuse, crushing the smoldering braid against the damp earth.

The heat was agonizing. 

The sulfur fire bit through the oiled leather in two heartbeats, scorching the wool of my sleeve and searing into the skin of my forearm with blinding pain. 

I did not lift my arm. 

With my right hand, I drew my skinning knife and drove the blade through the thick hemp braid two inches behind the spark, slicing through the powder core with a single savage saw of the honed steel.

With my bare fingers, I grabbed the burning head of the fuse, tore it away from the barrel, and plunged the glowing ember into the bucket of salt-mash that a smelter-hand dropped beside my knee.

*HSSSSSS.*

A cloud of foul, grey steam hissed from the bucket.

The spark was dead.

I lay in the dirt for three heartbeats, my chest heaving, listening to the drip of cold water and the heavy, ragged breathing of my men. 

Behind me, the last temple acolyte collapsed with a dull thud as Deputy Brand’s billhook ended the fight.

I sat up slowly, peeling the charred leather of my left vambrace away from my forearm. 

The skin beneath was blistered and raw, streaked with grey sulfur soot and red burns, but the muscle moved. The bone was intact. 

"Gods above," Sergeant Kester whispered, shining his lantern over the twelve copper-bound casks. "Ten heartbeats more, and this entire mountain would have come down on our heads."

"Roll the barrels away from the pillars," I commanded, getting to my feet, my voice raspy from the sulfur fumes. "Drench them in cold well water and set four guards on the hatch. No fire down here. Not even a pipe."

"Aye, Captain," Brand said, his eyes filled with grim reverence as he looked at my scorched arm.

From the spiraling stairwell above came a sudden, deafening roar of brass trumpets and cheering voices.

"The Margrave!" a watchman shouted from the upper landing. "The Margrave is on the wall!"

I climbed the stone stairs, using my right hand to wipe the soot from my eyes, emerging back into the wet, cold air of the inner courtyard.

The gale was blowing the smoke away over the battlements.

Standing upon the covered balcony of the Great Hall, clad in a full suit of dark steel armor and wrapped in a mantle of wolf-fur, stood Margrave Carrow.

Beside him stood Lady Aurelia, holding the green falcon banner of their house high above her head.

The Margrave was pale, his hands trembling slightly against the carved stone balustrade, but his eyes burned with fierce, unquestioned authority. 

"Soldiers of Barrow-Gate!" Margrave Carrow’s voice boomed across the courtyard, rolling off the stone walls with the weight of an avalanche. "The treason of the temple is broken! The holy venom has failed! By sovereign authority, the Church of Sol-Aethel is stripped of all civic and military privileges! Lay down your steel, or hang before the sun sets!"

In the courtyard below, forty surviving Dawnguard paladins dropped their broadswords onto the bloody flagstones. 

Men fell to their knees, ripping the sunburst badges from their tabards, raising their empty hands to the sky as the smelter-militia surrounded them with leveled pikes.

At the far end of the courtyard, near the southern carriage gate, a lone horseman wheeled his mount.

It was Evander Montfort.

His golden breastplate was dented and scorched by crossbow bolts; his electrum sword hung limp in his hand, its holy light sputtering and dying like an old lantern in the rain. Behind him, on a light mountain dray, sat High Prelate Malakor and Grand Inquisitor Justinian, their white robes spattered with mud, their faces masks of petrified despair.

Evander looked across the courtyard.

Through the drifting smoke, his wild, bloodshot eyes found mine.

I stood by the well-curb, my soot-blackened face framed by the iron rim of my kettle-helm, my burned left arm bound in a scrap of clean linen, my right hand resting steady on the hilt of my plain arming sword.

Evander’s swollen, wire-bound jaw worked convulsively, spit and blood flecking his chin.

"You witch!" he shrieked, his voice cracking into a shrill, hysterical wail that carried above the wind. "You cannot break the Sun! I am the Chosen! I am the Dawn!"

He drove his spurs savagely into his charger's flanks. 

The stallion bolted through the southern gate, accompanied by the clattering carriage of the prelate, fleeing down the paved causeway toward the massive marble dome of the Great Cathedral of Sol-Aethel.

A dozen watchmen raised their crossbows to shoot.

"Hold!" I shouted, raising my right hand.

The marksmen lowered their weapons, turning to look at me.

Margrave Carrow looked down from the balcony, his grey eyes locking onto mine with deep, unvoiced understanding.

"Let him run to his altar," I said, my voice carrying across the silent courtyard. "A trapped wolf dies in the open where the pack can watch."

I wiped the blood from my sword on my cloak, slid the blade into its plain scabbard with a crisp, final click, and turned toward the gate.
