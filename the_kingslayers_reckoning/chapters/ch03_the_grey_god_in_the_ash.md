# The Grey God in the Ash

Five silver marks weighed heavy in my palm.

They were broad, milled discs of solid southern silver, cold to the touch, stamped with the three towers of the Margraviate. They smelled of sweat, tallow, and the blood that had dripped from Horgan the Ox's split brow. 

The bout in the Bailiff’s Yard had lasted forty seconds. 

The giant had swung his four-foot iron-banded club with the roaring fury of an avalanche. He had expected me to raise my blade to block, which would have snapped Theron's thin arming sword like dry pine. Instead, I stepped inside his arc, slid my left shoulder against his ribs, and drove the heavy iron ring of my pommel straight into the hinge of his jaw. When he stumbled, roaring in pain, I hooked his lead ankle with the heel of my boot. Seven feet of muscle and bone crashed face-first into the churned sawdust. Before he could push his massive chest off the dirt, the cold edge of my steel was resting against his jugular.

Brand had stared in stunned silence for two full heartbeats before spitting his tobacco and calling the match.

"You're a wicked little bastard with that iron, boy," Brand had grunted, tossing the five silver marks onto the clerk's table. "No fancy foot-twirls. Just butcher's work. Come back next week. Lord Carrow’s wool-factors pay three marks a head for guards on the south turnpike."

I took the silver, dipped my chin in a polite nod, and walked out.

By midafternoon, I had spent one silver mark and six coppers in the market stalls along Tanner's Row.

I bought a stout sleeveless leather brigandine reinforced with riveted iron plates, oiled till the hide was supple as sheepskin. I bought a second-hand steel kettle-helm with a wide brim to turn rain, a clean pair of woolen hose, a pair of stiff riding boots with iron heel-caps, a leather waterskin, three pounds of dried beef cured in coarse rock salt, and a fine grey slate whetstone mined from the northern crags.

I left the reek of the Lower Ward behind, walking north along the old logging trail that climbed into the black pine forests of the foothills. 

The rain had cleared, leaving a sky the color of hammered lead. 

Mist curled through the fir trunks like white smoke from a smothered brush-fire. The earth beneath my boots was a springy carpet of fallen needles and damp moss. The air tasted clean—sharp with cold spruce sap, crushed fern, and the mineral bite of granite ledges weeping snowmelt. 

After half a league of steady climbing, the logging ruts faded into an overgrown deer path. 

Memories from Theron Marrow guided my feet. When the boy was fifteen, fleeing the wrath of a brutal sheep-master who had beaten him with a harness strap, he had hid in these deep woods for four days. He had stumbled upon an ancient place among the boulders—a place the village elders whispered about by winter hearths, warning children that the old lords of the stone slept beneath the pines.

I pushed through a thicket of tangled blackberry briars, the thorns catching harmlessly on my new leather brigandine.

The clearing opened before me in absolute silence.

It was a natural amphitheater of towering grey granite boulders, ringed by hemlocks thick as mill-wheels. In the center of the clearing stood a solitary monolith of dark basalt, twelve feet high, split down the middle by an ancient lightning strike. 

At the base of the monolith rested an iron anvil.

The iron anvil rose massive and archaic, far larger than any common forge block, pitted with deep scars from weapons that had broken against its horns centuries before the first town walls of Barrow-Gate were raised. The anvil stood embedded in a circular bed of grey wood-ash three inches deep, untouched by weeds or grass.

No birds sang in the hemlock branches. No wind stirred the ferns. The silence was thick, heavy, carrying the deep acoustic pressure of an ancient tomb.

I unslung my travel bag, set it beside a granite boulder, and walked toward the anvil.

"A strange altar," I murmured aloud, my voice flat in the dead quiet. "In the West, we built septs of crystal and gold. My father spent forty thousand gold pieces raising a gilded hall for the High Septon in Lannisport. The old gods of the North preferred weirwood trees with weeping red faces."

I rested my right hand on the cold iron horn of the anvil.

The frost in the metal bit into the flesh of my palm with the shock of mountain ice.

*The new gods are whores,* a voice replied.

The voice struck like an iron punch-press deep in the center of my chest, bypassing the ears entirely, vibrating through the bone of my sternum and rattling the teeth in my jaw.

The grey ash around the anvil stirred.

A plume of pale smoke rose from the bed of cinder, smelling of crushed charcoal, forge-slag, and dried blood. The air grew bitterly cold, freezing the moisture in my nostrils.

From the fissure in the basalt monolith, the shadow detached itself.

It was eight feet tall, shaped like a broad, heavy-shouldered man clad in overlapping plates of rusted iron and boar hide. His face was a featureless mask of weathered grey stone, save for two hollow sockets where white hearth-coals burned with slow, ancient embers. In his massive stone fist he held a four-foot iron forging hammer, its head black with grease and blood.

The hairs on the back of my neck stood up. 

In my life, I had faced kings, madmen, giants, and sellsword captains. I had seen wildfire burn armies to cinder at the Field of Fire in the old histories. 

This was something older than kings. This was the raw, brutal weight of the mountain itself—the memory of an era when men fought for their meat with sharpened flint and slept with axes beneath their heads.

"You bear a dead king’s pride," the stone god rumbled, the sound grinding like two millstones turning without grain. "And the soul of a slaughtered lion, poured into a vessel of common ditch-clay."

My boots held firm. I kept my chin high, my left hand resting casually on the iron pommel at my hip. 

"I was a lion in my own world," I said. "Here, your people seem to prefer peacocks in gilded tin."

A sound like cracking granite rumbled through the clearing. The god was laughing.

"The girl Sol-Aethel is a flighty child," the god said, lifting his heavy iron hammer and letting the head rest on the anvil face. The impact produced no ring, only a dull thud that shook the earth beneath my boots. "Two hundred summers ago, she crawled out of the morning mist, preening her golden feathers. The mortals saw her smooth skin, smelled her perfumed oils, and fell upon their knees like sheep before a salt lick."

The white embers in his eye sockets flared with cold disdain.

"She hands out her holy grace like a tavern wench tossing copper favors to drunken carters," the god spat. "She favors handsome faces. She favors broad shoulders and golden curls. She gives them light that cuts through stone and shields that turn steel, asking for nothing in return save songs of praise and silver incense in her marble halls."

"A cheap bargain," I noted, a cynical smirk touching my lips. "In Westeros, we had kings who gave lordship to fools who made them laugh at feast-time. It always ended in blood."

"It ends in rot," the god growled. "Her chosen champions do not bleed. They do not sweat. They swing heavy steel without knowing the weight of iron in their bones. When a real storm blows from the northern wastes, their gilded armor will crack like dried mud."

The stone colossus stepped forward, towering over me. The smell of cold ash and forge-hearth washed over my face.

"I am the Anvil of Ash," he rumbled. "The First Smith. The Judge of Iron and Bone. The mortals have forgotten my name, tearing down my shrines to carve sunbursts into soft plaster. But the law of the world remains iron. Bone breaks. Blood spills. Pride demands payment in the flesh."

He raised his free left hand. 

His stone palm opened, revealing a single token resting on the rock: a square nail of hand-forged black iron, four inches long, cold and pitted with hammer marks.

"You hate the peacock," the god said.

"I find him ridiculous," I corrected him smoothly. "A boy playing soldier in a mirror. But he insulted my dignity, stole the food from this boy's mouth, and laughed in my face. A Lannister pays his debts. The boy Evander owes me a reckoning."

"Then take the means to claim it," the god said. "I will not give you golden light. I will not grant you superhuman speed or fiery aura slashes to dazzle the simpletons on the tavern porch. Those are toys for children."

He tapped the iron nail with his hammerhead.

"I grant you the Sovereign Duel."

The words settled over the clearing with the weight of an executioner’s block.

"Speak the law," I said.

"When you face an enemy within the reach of your eye," the Anvil rumbled, "you may invoke the circle. For sixty heartbeats—one minute of mortal time—the world around you falls silent. Within that circle, every blessing is stripped away. Every spell is quenched. Every divine favor, every statutory enhancement, every aura shield and automated blade-guide vanishes into the dirt."

I felt my eyebrows rise. My pulse jumped against my ribs.

"Total nullification," I murmured.

"Total," the god confirmed, the white embers in his skull glowing brighter. "You and your foe will stand on bare ground. Your muscles against his muscles. Your bone against his bone. Your skill against his skill. For sixty seconds, the gods are blind. If you do not kill him or break him in those sixty heartbeats, the circle shatters, and his light returns."

My mind raced through the tactical reality with the cold precision of forty years of war.

"What are the limits?" I asked. "No gift comes without a cost."

"You learn quickly, lion," the god rumbled with grim approval. "First: the circle protects neither of you from the outside. If an archer stands beyond your circle and looses a shaft at your ribs, the arrow will bite your flesh. If a witch hurls lightning from the treeline, the fire will burn you both. You are vulnerable to the world."

"Fair enough," I said. "A battlefield is never clean."

"Second," the god continued, "the strain of stripping the divine realm tears at your mortal blood. Once the circle closes, your soul requires one full hour of recovery before you may invoke the duel again. Use it foolishly, and you will face your enemies with nothing save your own bare hands."

"One hour," I repeated, committing the number to memory. "And sixty seconds inside the ring."

"Sixty seconds," the god said. "Is that enough time for a knight of the Golden Lion to kill a boy in gilded tin?"

A slow, terrifying grin spread across my face—the same smile I wore when I cut down the outlaws of the Kingswood at fifteen.

"Give me sixty seconds without magic against any man who ever drew breath," I said, "and I will show him why they called me the Kingslayer."

The Anvil of Ash struck the four-inch iron nail with his massive hammer.

*CLANG.*

The sound was deafening. It split the air of the clearing like a thunderclap, vibrating through the granite boulders, sending flocks of black crows screeching from the pines. 

A bolt of grey light shot from the anvil straight into my chest.

The impact drove me to one knee. 

Pain flared beneath my breastbone—not the tearing agony of a weapon, but the searing heat of a brand burned into the soul. My veins felt as though they were running with liquid lead. The mark etched itself deep into my spirit: an ancient brand shaped like a crossed anvil and broken spear.

Then, the heat cooled. 

The heavy lead in my veins settled into an icy, coiled spring of supernatural power, resting just behind my heartbeat, waiting for my command.

I gasped, pushing myself back to my feet.

The clearing was empty.

The eight-foot stone colossus had vanished. The fissure in the basalt monolith was merely a crack in weathered stone. The white smoke had dispersed on the pine-scented wind. 

Only the massive iron anvil remained, resting in its circle of grey wood-ash. On the flat horn of the anvil lay the four-inch hand-forged iron nail, cold and real.

I stepped forward, picked up the nail, and turned it over in my fingers. 

The metal was rough, heavy, smelling of coal smoke and deep ore. I slipped it into the leather purse at my belt beside my four silver marks.

I walked back to the granite boulder where my pack lay. 

I unstrapped my water skin and took a long swallow of mountain water, washing the sulfurous taste from my tongue. Then I pulled out my new slate whetstone and a small scrap of oiled sheepskin.

I sat on the boulder, resting the blackened arming sword across my knees.

I poured three drops of clear oil along the fuller of the blade. 

With firm, circular strokes, I drew the whetstone along the bevel, from the crossguard to the notched tip. *Scrape. Scrape. Scrape.* The rhythmic sound of stone biting iron echoed softly through the hemlocks, steady as the ticking of a water clock.

Evander Montfort was down in the High Ward, no doubt. 

He was drinking spiced vintage in the Margrave’s hall, showing his milk-white teeth to noble ladies, basking in the glow of his radiant sun-crest, and bragging about how he had kicked a useless porter into the tavern mud. Lyra was beside him, stroking his gilded pauldrons, reveling in her stolen silks.

Let them laugh. Let them drink.

I tested the newly sharpened edge against the ball of my right thumb. 

The steel was cold. It bit the skin with razor-clean precision, drawing a single bead of bright crimson blood.

I wiped the blood on the flat of the blade, sheathed the sword, and stood up into the falling twilight.
