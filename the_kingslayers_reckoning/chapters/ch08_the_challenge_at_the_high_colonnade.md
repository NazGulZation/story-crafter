# The Challenge at the High Colonnade

The High Promenade was built of white mountain limestone that caught the pale autumn sun like polished bone.

Three tiers of marble terraces looked out over the Lower Ward of Barrow-Gate, separated from the filth of the tanneries and pig-pens by a fifty-foot curtain wall of dressed basalt. Up here, the air smelled of cedar shavings, roasted chestnuts from street braziers, imported clove wine, and the sweet lavender pomade worn by court functionaries. Gilded guards in crimson silk tabards leaned against their halberds at every arched gate, their breastplates polished to a blinding mirror sheen.

I walked along the upper colonnade, carrying a roll of stamped barge manifests under my left arm. 

The morning had been spent in the River Basin, inspecting the sixteen flat-bottomed grain barges that had arrived from the southern lowlands. Four hundred tons of winter rye, dried peas, and salt pork rested beneath tarred canvas covers, their holds guarded by the twenty veterans I had chosen from the Bailiff’s Yard. 

I had audited every vessel myself. 

I checked the bilge pumps, tested the tow-ropes for river rot, and replaced four drunken skippers with sober marcher carters. When a company commander failed to check his ropes, his wagons drowned; my father Tywin had taught me that before I was old enough to ride a destrier. A soldier marched on iron and meat, but an army starved when its barges rotted at the quay.

Behind me walked four of my chosen veterans—hard-faced marcher men in ringmail hauberks, carrying heavy boar spears with blackened ash hafts. 

Their boots struck the white limestone flags with a steady, rhythmic crunch that drew haughty stares from the courtiers strolling beneath the marble pergolas.

"Look at the mercenaries," a young lord in saffron velvet drawled, turning an amber pomander ball between perfumed fingers. "Factor Vallow brings ditch-fighters onto the sovereign terrace as though the High Ward were a cattle pen."

"Ditch-fighters who keep your winter bread dry, my lord," I said without slowing my stride, my grey eyes meeting his with the cold, effortless weight of Casterly Rock. 

The young noble blinked, stepping back against the carved balustrade, his scent of musk and almond powder suddenly thin against the mountain air.

Ahead of us, blocking the sunlit archway of the Great Portico, stood Evander Montfort.

He was accompanied by six temple paladins in silvered plate armor, their white silk capes billowing in the fresh breeze. 

Evander had dressed for the court with theatrical vanity. His armor was magnificent—a newly commissioned half-suit of fluted white steel trimmed with repoussé gold sunbursts, the steel buffed to a mirror gloss. At his neck hung a collar of heavy electrum links, holding a sun-crystal the size of a pigeon's egg that pulsed with a warm, arrogant golden glow. His golden curls were pomaded and parted in the eastern fashion; his violet eyes burned with imperious, wounded malice.

On the upper gallery overlooking the portico, a group of highborn ladies stood leaning over the gilded balustrade. 

Among them sat Lady Aurelia, the Margrave's niece—a striking woman of twenty-two with cold green eyes, wrapped in dark ermine furs. A small court of poets, falconers, and junior bailiffs hovered around her chair, watching the confrontation below with breathless amusement.

Evander had clearly planned his stage. 

He waited until my boots were five paces from the marble steps before stepping forward, resting his right hand upon the wire-wrapped pommel of his six-foot greatsword.

"Halt there, carter," Evander called out, his voice booming under the vaulted limestone arches with the trained resonance of an actor playing an emperor.

The courtiers and noble ladies taking the afternoon air paused, rustling their heavy silk skirts and whispering behind painted fans. Two city watchmen stepped back, reluctant to cross the favored champion of the Radiant Dawn.

My four spearmen slowed their pace, their fingers tightening around their ash hafts.

"Steady," I told them quietly, my tone level and calm. "Keep your ranks. A barking dog rarely bites."

I stopped four paces from Evander, resting my right hand lightly on the pommel of my plain arming sword.

"The promenade is wide, Lord Montfort," I said in a dry, measured baritone. "There is plenty of room for a peacock to spread his tail without blocking the trade way."

A ripple of nervous snickers broke out along the upper gallery. Lady Aurelia pressed a gloved finger to her lips, her green eyes glittering with sharp, aristocratic amusement.

Evander’s jaw set hard, a dark flush staining his fair cheeks beneath his golden curls.

"You have grown arrogant on carters' coppers, Theron," Evander sneered, stepping down one marble stair, his gilded spurs ringing against the stone. "Factor Vallow is an old skinflint who hoards pig-iron like a badger. But the river grain fleet belongs to the Grand Guild. I have already petitioned Margrave Carrow's High Seneschal to revoke your commission for gross incompetence."

"The Seneschal denied your petition an hour ago," I replied smoothly, tapping the parchment roll under my arm. "The Merchants' Guild pays the garrison's wages. Factor Vallow reminded the Seneschal that your vanguard spent their last march hunting wild goats while the freight wagons took three stalkers in the rear. The Seneschal preferred grain in his storehouses over gold feathers in his salon."

"You insolent gutter-rat!" a temple paladin snarled, stepping forward with his hand clamped onto the hilt of his broadsword. "You speak to a knight of the Holy Dawn—"

"Stand down, Kenneth," Evander commanded, holding out a gilded gauntlet. 

The hero fixed his gaze upon me, his lips curling into a smug, venomous grin that reeked of wounded vanity.

"You think you understand steel, Theron, because you butchered a wounded beast with an old spit. You think you can stand among real men."

He stepped closer, towering half a head above me, his broad chest swelling beneath his fluted white cuirass. The electrum sunburst at his collar pulsed with warm, golden light, radiating a faint halo of divine protection that rippled over his armor.

"You got lucky in the pine defile," Evander whispered, his tone dropping to a venomous purr meant for my ears alone. "Just like you got lucky in the lime-kiln. But out on the sand, there are no wagons to hide behind. There are no teamsters with crossbows. There is only blood and sun."

"You talk too much, boy," I told him, letting my gaze drift lazily down his magnificent armor. 

My eyes lingered on the gap between his fauld and his tassets, then dropped to his boots.

"Your left heel turns inward by three inches when you posture," I noted aloud, my voice carrying cleanly across the entire portico. "You drop your left shoulder every time you draw a deep breath to boast. And that six-foot spit at your hip is balanced so poorly that if a man parries you in the middle octave, your wrist will snap before you complete the recovery."

The watching guards stiffened. 

Two veteran sergeants on the portico exchanged startled, knowing glances. Fencers who knew their craft could see the truth in the assessment: Evander’s stance was built for tournament parades, wide and stiff, relying entirely on his divine strength to force his blade through parries.

Up on the gallery, Lady Aurelia leaned forward, whispering something to her master-at-arms, who nodded in quiet, professional agreement.

Evander’s eyes flared with murderous fury. The laughter of the court, the quiet nods of the veterans, the cool contempt in my eyes—it stripped away his rehearsed heroic posture like bark torn from a rotting log.

He unclasped his white leather glove with a vicious yank of his teeth. 

With a theatrical sweep of his arm, Evander struck me across the left pauldron with the leather gauntlet.

The white hide slapped against my oiled brigandine with a sharp, leather snap.

The colonnade went dead silent.

"The Midsummer Tourney begins in three days," Evander declared, his booming voice carrying across the entire promenade, addressing the gathered courtiers, watchmen, and the Margrave’s gallery. "The Grand List before Margrave Carrow and the High Council. Five thousand silver crowns to the champion. Open to all badged masters of the province."

He pointed a shaking, ringed finger at my chest.

"Enter the lists, Theron. Step onto the sands before the Margrave. Let the province see what happens when a common ditch-dog tries to stand against the chosen blade of the Sun."

He leaned forward, his violet eyes burning with absolute, fanatical certainty.

"I will cleave your plain iron blade in half," Evander whispered. "I will shatter your collarbone before the whole court. And when you are crawling in the sand, I will have the bailiffs drag you through the gates in chains."

I looked down at the white leather gauntlet resting on the marble flags between our boots.

Then, I looked back up into his handsome, empty face.

A smile touched my lips—the cold, golden smile that made the lords of the Eyrie tremble when I offered them trial by combat.

"Five thousand crowns," I murmured. "A very respectable purse."

I drew back my right foot and kicked the white glove over the balustrade.

The leather tumbled through the bright mountain sunlight, spinning forty paces down into the muddy drainage ditch of the Lower Ward.

"Sharpen your six-foot spit, boy," I told him, turning my back on his gilded retinue. "And pray the wind doesn't blow too hard on the lists. You might lose your feathers."

I walked through the colonnade, my four spearmen falling into disciplined step behind me, their iron boots ringing with rhythmic confidence against the white stone.
