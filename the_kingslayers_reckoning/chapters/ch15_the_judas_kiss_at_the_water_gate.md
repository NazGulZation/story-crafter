# The Judas Kiss at the Water Gate

The river wharves at the Lower Water Gate smelled of rotting marsh weeds, brine-soaked pine timber, and stagnant ditch-water.

Dense, cold fog rolled off the swollen Skell in thick grey blankets, muffling the distant tolling of the cathedral bells and clinging to the slippery timber pilings of the salt-warehouses like wet wool. Every few moments, the river current slapped against the hull of an empty coal dray moored to the stone quayside, the hemp hawser groaning with a low, mournful creak. High above the wharves, the iron portcullis of the Water Gate hung suspended over the sluice channel, its blackened spikes dripping with condensation in the autumn dark.

I stood in the deep shadow of an arched salt-shed, my back pressed against the salt-encrusted stone, an iron-capped kettle-helm pulled low over my brow. 

In my left hand, I held a small carved bone button.

It was an old thing, smoothed by years of handling, carved from the knuckle-bone of a sheep. Seven years ago, in the muddy potato fields of Alder-Hollow, the boy Theron Marrow had carved that button for a ten-year-old girl named Lyra Cole when the brass clasp on her winter cape broke in the sleet. Theron had frozen his knuckles to the bone whittling the marrow-groove with an old skinning knife, feeling like a prince when she gave him a shy smile and kissed his frostbitten cheek.

Two hours ago, an urchin from the dockside fish-gutters had pressed that exact bone button into my palm in the foundry cellar, along with a scrap of torn linen inscribed with charcoal:

> *Theron—*
> *They locked me in the vestry when the guards seized the keep. Evander is mad. He struck me when I wept. I stole the master keys to the High Ward armory and the Margrave’s bedchamber. Meet me at the third salt-shed by the Water Gate at the fifth watch. Alone. For the love of our village, save me.*
> *—Lyra.*

I rolled the bone button between my thumb and forefinger, feeling the cold smoothness of the polish.

A thin, razor-sharp smile touched the corner of my lips.

"A touching missive, Captain," Deputy Brand muttered from the darkness behind the brine barrels. The watch officer leaned on a heavy iron-headed billhook, chewing on a sprig of dried mint to keep the river stench from his teeth. "The little field-mouse discovers her golden cat has claws, and now she wants her country dog back."

"A field-mouse doesn't know how to write in High Imperial cursive, Deputy," I replied, tossing the bone button into the black river current. 

The small piece of bone hit the water with a faint *plink* and vanished into the fog. 

"Lyra Cole grew up in a turf hovel with five brothers who couldn't tell an ox-brand from a tax stamp," I said, checking the edge of my arming sword with a light scrape of my thumbnail. "She knows how to cure sheep-gut and boil dried pease. This letter was dictated by a clerk from the High Inquest, written with an ink-horn filled with imperial gall-nut ink. The phrasing smells of temple chancery."

"Then she is selling you to Justinian," Brand spat, tightening his grip on the billhook.

"She is selling me for whatever Evander promised her," I said. "A silver girdle, a velvet gown, or a cottage in the southern vineyards where the mud won't dirty her skirts. A girl who drops her skirts for gold will sell her own mother to keep the silk dry."

"Then we don't go," Brand said. "We bar the foundry doors and wait for the Margrave to wake."

"We go," I countered, looking Brand dead in his weathered face. "A man who turns away from an ambush leaves thirty enemies behind his back to butcher him when he sleeps. When a fool prepares a trap, the kindest thing you can do is walk into it... with forty sledgehammers behind your cloak."

I turned toward the interior of the salt-warehouse.

In the gloom between the sixty-pound sacks of coarse salt, forty furnace-hands from the Smelter-Guild and twenty veteran watchmen crouched in absolute silence. 

Every smith carried a heavy iron pavise—four feet of seasoned oak faced with sheet-iron—and a twelve-pound steel sledge. The watchmen were armed with short ash-wood boarding pikes and loaded steel crossbows. Master Kroll knelt by the side door, holding a coil of tarred rope and two heavy iron crossbars taken from the canal weirs.

"You have your orders, Kroll," I whispered.

"Aye, Captain," the master smith grunted, his walrus mustache twitching with grim anticipation. "We let the lady sing her song. The moment the temple dogs show steel from the loft, we drop the iron bars on the street doors and turn the warehouse into a slaughterhouse."

"No shouting," I commanded. "No heroic challenges. Break their knees, take their quarrels, and drive them into the river."

I left the salt-shed alone, stepping out onto the open wooden planks of the wharf.

The fifth watch was tolling from the high towers. The sound rolled through the fog like a muffled drumbeat: five slow, heavy strokes that set the gulls screaming in the mist.

Thirty paces ahead, where the timber wharf met the stone wall of the Water Gate, stood a solitary figure.

It was Lyra Cole.

The transformation since the tavern yard was unmistakable. The muddy homespun of the frontier had vanished, replaced by the stolen trappings of the high town, yet she had not escaped the storm. She wore a long traveling mantle of fine crimson wool lined with white ermine—a gift from Evander’s high-town suite—draped over a gown of embroidered sky-blue silk. But the sleet and river fog had ruined the finery. 

The hem of her silk gown was caked in black river mud and dead marsh grass; the white ermine fur was soaked and matted like the pelt of a drowned weasel; and her chestnut curls hung in limp, shivering strings around her pale shoulders.

She stood huddled against an iron mooring bollard, clutching a small leather pouch to her chest, her teeth chattering with cold.

"Theron?" she called out, her voice a thin, trembling whisper that barely carried above the slap of the water. "Theron... is that you?"

I walked forward with slow, measured strides, the iron caps of my riding boots echoing against the wet oak boards. 

"I am here, Lyra," I said, coming to a halt ten paces from her stirrup.

At the sound of my voice, a sob broke from her lips. 

She dropped the leather pouch onto the wet planks and rushed toward me, her arms outstretched, her wide amber eyes brimming with glistening tears. 

"Theron! Oh gods, Theron!" she cried, throwing herself against my chest.

Her arms wrapped around my neck; her face buried itself into the collar of my leather brigandine. The scent of her washed over me—expensive lavender water struggling against the sour, panicked sweat of a cornered beast. 

"I was so afraid," she wept, her small body trembling violently against mine. "The church... Evander... he went mad after the tourney! He smashed the mirrors in our rooms... he struck me when I asked about the prize silver... he called me a gutter-tramp! He told me he was going to take the Margrave's daughter for his lady and leave me to the temple guards! I couldn't stay there, Theron! I couldn't!"

She pressed her cheek against my chest, her voice rising in a frantic, honeyed crescendo of desperate affection:

"Take me back, Theron! Please! Remember the winter in Alder-Hollow? Remember how you kept my feet warm by the hearth? Remember how we promised we would buy a small farm by the river when you earned your silver? I was foolish... I was dazzled by the gold and the songs... but it was always you! It was always you, my lion!"

I did not move.

My arms remained resting loosely at my sides. My arming sword stayed in its scabbard. 

I looked down at the crown of her wet chestnut head with the cold, unyielding detachment of an executioner inspecting a condemned neck.

"A lovely speech, Lyra," I said, my voice smooth and quiet as silk sliding over an edge. "You almost sound as though you believe it."

Lyra’s breath hitched. 

Her sobbing stopped with unnatural abruptness. 

Her fingers, which had been clutching the back of my wool doublet, tightened suddenly into claws.

"Theron?" she whispered, lifting her tear-streaked face to look into my slate-grey eyes. "What... what are you saying?"

"I am saying that you are standing on my left flank," I told her, my right hand coming up with blinding speed to close around her slender right wrist. 

My fingers clamped down like an iron vise, squeezing the bone until her knuckles turned blue.

"Ow! Theron, you are hurting me!" she cried, struggling to pull away.

"You positioned yourself to turn my back toward the upper loft of the second warehouse," I said, my voice dropping into a razor-edged register that froze the blood in her veins. "You stepped into my reach to pin my sword arm, and your left hand has been tapping the rhythm of a temple signal against the iron ring of that bollard for the last twenty heartbeats."

The color vanished from Lyra’s face.

The wide, tearful innocence in her amber eyes dissolved instantly, replaced by a naked, feral terror.

"Now!" Lyra screamed at the top of her lungs, twisting frantically in my grasp. "Shoot him! Shoot the witch!"

From the dark, shuttered loft windows of the third salt-warehouse, twenty crossbow quarrels erupted into the fog.

They were aimed dead at the space where my chest had been.

But I was already moving.

The moment Lyra drew breath to scream, I stepped backward and swung her bodily across my front, using her momentum and the torque of my hips to pivot behind the heavy stone masonry of the Water Gate’s archway.

*THWACK. THWACK. THWACK.*

Six steel quarrels buried themselves into the heavy oak boards of the wharf where I had stood half a heartbeat before. Two more bolts struck the stone archway above my head, showering our hair in sharp limestone splinters. One quarrel sheared through the hem of Lyra’s red cloak, pinning the crimson wool straight into the wood at her feet.

Lyra shrieked in horror, staring at the iron quarrel vibrating between her boots.

"Inquisitors never care about the bait, Lyra," I muttered, releasing her bruised wrist with a contemptuous shove that sent her sprawling onto her hands and knees in the mud. "They shoot through the sheep to kill the wolf."

From the warehouse lofts came the shouting of men: "He stepped behind the gate! Advance! Cut him down before he reaches the water!"

The double doors of the second warehouse burst open.

Thirty elite paladins of the Dawnguard poured out onto the wharf in a wedge of silver-plated armor, brandishing broadswords and heavy poleaxes, their white surcoats billowing in the river gale. At their head ran an inquisitorial sub-prior, screaming holy slogans as he leveled a loaded repeating crossbow.

I did not draw my sword.

I lifted two fingers to my lips and blew a sharp, piercing whistle that cut through the fog like an arrow.

*BOOM.*

Behind the advancing paladins, the heavy iron-reinforced doors of the main salt-shed crashed open against the stone walls.

Fifty smelter-guild veterans surged onto the quayside in three tight ranks, their massive iron-clad pavises locking together with a thunderous clatter that shook the wharf. Behind the shield-wall stood twenty marcher watchmen, their heavy stirrup-crossbows already leveled at the backs of the temple paladins.

"Level quarrels!" Deputy Brand roared from the flank. "Loose!"

Twenty crossbow strings snapped in unison.

At twenty paces, heavy steel quarrels tore through silvered plate like parchment. 

Seven temple paladins collapsed instantly, pierced through kidneys and shoulder-blades, their screams choked off as they crashed into the wet timber. 

Before the inquisitorial squad could turn their formation to face the rear assault, Master Kroll led the smelter-hands in a devastating charge.

"Smelt the bastards!" Kroll bellowed, swinging a sixteen-pound steel sledge with both hands.

The iron head struck the sub-prior’s breastplate with the concussive force of a falling boulder. 

The silvered steel caved inward with a sickening crunch of ribs and sternum. The sub-prior flew backward through the air, crashing over the wharf railing and plunging into the churning black rapids of the Skell forty feet below.

The fight was short, brutal, and unsparing.

Trapped in the narrow, eight-foot corridor between the warehouse wall and the river, the Dawnguard’s long broadswords were useless against the interlocking pavises and short boarding pikes of the marcher veterans. Smelter-hands swung sledgehammers into greaves, shattering knees and shins; watchmen drove iron billhooks into gorgets and visors, dragging armored men to the boards and pinning them beneath heavy boots.

Within three minutes, the wharf was quiet.

Eighteen temple paladins lay dead in pools of dark, steaming blood that washed into the river through the gaps in the timber. The remaining twelve knelt in the mud, their dented swords discarded, their hands clasped behind their heads as watchmen bound their wrists with rough hemp rope.

I stepped out from beneath the stone archway of the Water Gate, walking slowly across the blood-slick planks.

My plain arming sword remained sheathed at my hip. My hands were clean.

Beside the iron mooring bollard, curled into a ball of wet silk, red wool, and foul river mud, lay Lyra Cole.

She was shivering so hard her teeth clicked like dice in a leather cup. Her costly ermine trim was ruined, soaked in fish scales, horse manure, and bilge water. Her fingers were clawed into the wet dirt, her face pressed against the slime as she wept—not with the theatrical crocodile tears of her entrance, but with the raw, suffocating, animal terror of a creature that knew its life was forfeit.

I stopped two paces from her head, looking down with cold, aristocratic boredom.

"Theron..." she choked out, looking up through her tangled, mud-caked hair. 

Her amber eyes were swollen, wide with frantic, begging desperation. She crawled forward on her elbows and knees, reaching out with trembling, slime-covered hands to grasp the toe of my iron-capped boot.

"Theron... have mercy... please... please!" she sobbed, pressing her forehead against the leather of my boot. "I was forced... Justinian threatened my family! He said they would burn my mother’s cottage in the valley! I didn't want to do it... I swear by the Light, I didn't want to!"

"Your mother died of the winter cough when you were six years old, Lyra," I said quietly.

Lyra’s breath caught in her throat. The lie withered on her lips.

"You traded me for three clipped farthings in the tavern yard when you thought Evander was a god," I said, my voice carrying across the silent wharf like the strike of cold iron. "You stood before fifty laughing teamsters and told them you were in his warm bed screaming his name while I froze on watch. And tonight, you traded my life for a purse of sovereigns and a promise of a silk gown."

"I was wrong!" she wailed, clutching my boot with both hands, wetting the leather with her tears and saliva. "I was blind! I love you, Theron! I have always loved you! Look at me! Look at your Lyra! I will do anything... I will be your servant... I will wash your feet... I will warm your bed every night... just don't kill me! Don't let them hang me from the gate!"

I looked down at her.

Thirty years ago, in the Red Keep, I had watched men and women crawl through the rushes before King Robert, begging for pardons, lands, and favor. Cersei had always smiled when men begged, savoring their humiliation like sweet wine. I had always found it pathetic. A traitor who crawled was still a traitor; groveling did not erase treason, it merely made it ugly.

I reached down, took the collar of her ruined ermine mantle in my left fist, and hauled her to her feet with a single, effortless pull.

She hung in my grip like a broken doll, her feet dangling inches above the mud, gasping for breath.

With my right hand, I reached to her throat.

My fingers caught the heavy silver chain resting beneath her collar—the five-mark necklace Evander had given her from the plundered merchant wagons.

I snapped the chain with a single sharp jerk of my wrist.

The silver links parted with a crisp, metallic snap.

"Theron... please..." she whispered, her eyes rolling in terror as she waited for the steel of my dagger.

I did not draw my dagger.

I tossed the broken silver chain into the hands of Sergeant Kester, who stood watching from the warehouse door.

"Give that to the widow of the watchman Justinian's men murdered at the South Bastion," I commanded.

"Aye, Captain," Kester said, pocketing the silver with a grim nod.

I dropped Lyra Cole back into the mud.

She collapsed into a heap of wet skirts, weeping uncontrollably, her hands clutching her bruised throat.

"Execution is far too clean for you, Lyra," I said, looking down at her with the cold detachment of a Kingsguard Lord Commander. "A corpse forgets its shame in an hour. You will live to remember every choice you made."

I turned to Master Kroll.

"Take her to the municipal debtors' yard at the Lower Foundry," I ordered. "Strip that velvet and ermine; burn it in the slag-furnace. Issue her a coarse hemp tunic, an iron shovel, and a wooden scraper. She will scrape mule dung from the supply wagons and haul coke to the smelting pits until the city magistrates convene court."

"No!" Lyra screamed, clawing at the muddy planks as two burly smelter-hands seized her by the arms, hauling her backward toward the cart. "Theron! No! Not the foundries! I am a lady! Evander promised I would be a lady! Theron!"

Her shrieks echoed across the foggy river, fading slowly into the dark as the smelter-hands dragged her away through the salt-sheds.

Deputy Brand wiped the blood from his billhook with a wool rag, stepping up beside me on the quayside.

"The Water Gate is secure, Captain," Brand said. "The boats are manned, and Justinian's ambush squad is broken. What are your orders?"

I looked up through the swirling river fog toward the upper basalt terrace of Barrow-Gate, where the torches of the Margrave’s Keep flickered against the storm.

From the east came the faint, unmistakable sound of iron horns blowing from the municipal watchtowers. 

The signal of the Falcon.

"The Margrave has taken the counter-draught," I said, adjusting my kettle-helm and settling my sword belt against my hip. "He is awake. The city watch is assembling at the Great Colonnade."

I drew my arming blade, checked the clean line of the steel in the torchlight, and turned toward the upper causeway.

"Let us go relieve the Lord Protector of his burden."
