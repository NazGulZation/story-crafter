# The Shadow of the Sunburst

The subterranean foundries beneath the Lower Ward smelled of sulfur, red-hot coke, and damp limestone dust.

Deep within the bedrock beneath Barrow-Gate, the Smelter-Guild had hollowed out a cavernous labyrinth of brick-lined tunnels, iron casting pits, and roaring blast furnaces that had fed the frontier with steel for three hundred years. Water from the mountain streams rushed through vaulted basalt sluices overhead, driving heavy wooden trip-hammers that pounded against iron anvils with a steady, deafening cadence. In the red glare of the tap-holes, soot-stained smiths moved stripped to the waist, their callused arms glistening with sweat as they raked slag into gravel channels.

In the center of the Great Vault, beneath an arched ceiling blackened by generations of bituminous smoke, sat our makeshift command post.

Three heavy iron anvils supported a rough table of split pine planks covered in sheepskin city maps, municipal tax rolls, and iron tallow lamps. On a cot of straw and sheepskins in the corner lay Margrave Carrow.

His breathing was a shallow, rattling gasp. His grey skin looked like wet clay in the orange furnace glow, his lips stained purple where dried blood crusted his teeth. Beside him sat Lady Aurelia, her green velvet sleeves rolled to her elbows, pressing a clean cloth soaked in cold well water to her father's fevered forehead. 

"The charcoal slurry slowed the bile," Master Kroll muttered, tossing an iron stirrup-bar onto the table with a dull clang. The master smelter smelled of stale tobacco and scorched leather, his walrus mustache singed at the corners. "His heart still beats, but it beats weak. The poison is eating him from inside, Captain. If we don't get the counter-draught into his throat before the second dawn, his lungs will turn to water."

"What is the antidote?" I asked, looking up from the map where the city's three rings of limestone walls were drawn in black ink.

"White-root elixir and distilled moon-dew," Factor Vallow answered from across the table, his thin fingers trembling as he adjusted his iron-rimmed spectacles. The merchant factor had lost his velvet mantle during the scramble through the buttery, but he still clutched his brass-bound cash box against his ribs like a shield. "Only the High Academy brews it. High Prelate Malakor keeps the master vials locked within the inner sanctum of the Sunburst Cloister."

"Then we take the cloister," I said.

"Take the cloister?" Vallow squawked, his voice cracking against the rhythmic boom of the forge hammers. "Captain, the Dawnguard holds every street from the Iron Market to the Upper Colonnade! Have you heard the heralds?"

"I heard them," I replied.

An hour before midnight, church criers had marched through the main thoroughfares accompanied by squads of twenty armored paladins. 

From the stone platforms of the market squares, they had read the decree of the Holy Council: Margrave Carrow was declared an apostate stricken down by divine judgment; Captain Theron Marrow was named a sorcerer of the black ash, condemned to burn upon an iron grate; and a bounty of ten thousand silver crowns was promised to any freeman or bondsman who delivered my severed right hand to the steps of Sol-Aethel.

The five gates of Barrow-Gate were barred with iron portcullises and chained oak balks. No cart of rye entered; no messenger crossed the moat. 

Above the basalt towers of the High Ward, the golden sunburst banner of the Radiant Dawn fluttered in the autumn gale where the green falcon of House Carrow had flown for two centuries.

"They have four hundred temple paladins," Vallow pleaded, pointing a trembling finger at the upper city sectors. "Three companies of southern mercenaries under contract, sixty light horsemen, and the entire high arsenal. If they march down the basalt causeway with their holy firebolts, these foundries will become our tombs!"

"They will not march down the causeway," I said calmly, tracing the line of the main avenue with the tip of my dagger.

"And why in the seven hells wouldn't they?" Kroll grunted.

"Because they are cowards who rely on bells and gilded armor to scare peasants," I said, looking Kroll dead in the eye. "A real commander attacks while his enemy is scrambling in the dark. Evander Montfort is nursing a broken knee and three shattered teeth in a silk bed, whining to his priests. Justinian is a clerk who thinks parchment decrees fight wars. They believe the ten-thousand-silver bounty will do their butcher's work for them. They think hunger and terror will drive the guilds to drag me to the cathedral in chains."

I turned to Deputy Brand, who stood by the forge archway with four city watchmen in rusted kettle-helms. "How many men can the watch muster?"

"Eighty loyal spears in the Lower Ward," Brand replied, spitting tobacco into a slag trough. "The rest are barricaded in the South Bastion, waiting to see which way the wind blows. But the guildsmen are angry, Captain. Justinian's inquisitors broke into the cloth-hall an hour ago, seized forty bales of wool to pay for temple supplies, and strung up an apprentice weaver who spit on their robes. The Lower Ward is a powder keg waiting for an iron spark."

"Then we give them the spark," I said. "Master Kroll, how many men can you put in the line?"

Kroll wiped his sooty brow with a hairy forearm, a grim grin stretching his mustache. "Two hundred furnace hands and mold-boys who know how to swing a twelve-pound sledge. We have fifty iron crowbars, forty pig-stickers, and two tons of scrap axle-iron we can turn into crossbow bolts before cockcrow."

"Do it," I commanded. "Barricade the three street mouths leading from the upper avenue into the Foundry Quarter. Lay down spiked hurdles and iron caltrops in the shadows. If their silver horsemen ride down the flags, unseat them with grappling hooks and butcher them in the mud."

Before Kroll could answer, the iron door at the end of the drainage tunnel creaked open.

Sergeant Kester emerged from the darkness, accompanied by six smelter-boys carrying iron clubs. Behind them, two apprentice carters dragged a captured hand-dray covered in a damp canvas tarp.

"Found this crossing the canal bridge near the Old Tanneries, Captain," Kester grunted, his breath misting in the cavern air. "Four temple lay-brothers with a mule team. They tried to draw knives, but the boys cracked their crowns with hickory pick-handles."

"What was in the dray?" I asked, stepping toward the cart.

Kester reached out and yanked the heavy canvas tarp away.

Resting in a bed of dry sawdust lay six squat barrels made of thick, river-seasoned oak, bound with bands of blackened copper. Each barrel was stamped with the sunburst seal of Sol-Aethel, surrounded by three warning runes etched in scarlet cinnabar: *CAUTION: RADIANT NAPHTHA.*

Lady Aurelia rose from her father's cot, her face turning pale as bone in the torchlight. "Liquid Sun."

"What is it?" I asked, leaning down to inspect the bung-seal of the nearest cask. The wood was cold to the touch, but a faint, oily vapor seeped from the seam—smelling of distilled turpentine, sulfur, and sweet almonds. 

"Alchemical fire," Aurelia whispered, her hands balling into fists at her sides. "The High Academy developed it three winters ago for siege defense. It is distilled from the concentrated bile of sulfur-crawlers and refined solar oils. Once ignited, water will not extinguish it. It burns with white-hot heat that melts bronze, cracks granite, and consumes flesh in seconds."

"Why would four temple brothers be hauling siege fuel through the tannery alleys in the middle of a storm?" Factor Vallow asked, adjusting his spectacles as he leaned over the barrels.

Kester spat on the floor. "We took an iron ledger from the lead brother's pouch. Written in the Grand Inquisitor's own hand."

He handed me a folded sheet of vellum.

I opened the sheet beside an iron tallow lamp. The handwriting was thin, precise, and venomous—the script of an inquisitor accounting for slaughter:

> *To Brother Dustin, Custodian of the Sluices:*
> *Thirty casks of the Sacred Light have been transferred to the cistern tunnels beneath the Lower Market. Upon the signal bells of the Fourth Watch, the sluice-bungs shall be breached into the municipal reservoirs and ignited.*
> *The fire of Sol-Aethel shall cleanse the disease of heresy from the lower quarters. The false captain and his apostate accomplices shall burn in their own foundries. Let the ashes of the wicked testify to the eternal sovereignty of the Sun.*
> *Signed: Justinian, Grand Inquisitor.*

The cavern fell into a dead, suffocating silence.

The rhythmic pounding of the forge hammers seemed to fade into a hollow echo. Factor Vallow dropped his brass cash box onto the dirt, staring at the vellum in sheer, unadulterated horror.

"Gods above," Vallow stammered, his lips trembling. "Thirty casks in the cisterns. If they ignite that volume in the aqueduct beneath the market... the blast will collapse the street vaults. The fire will boil through the sewers, into the basements, into the tenements... fifty thousand people live in the Lower Ward."

"They don't want to conquer the city," Lady Aurelia said, her voice shaking with cold fury. "They want to exterminate it. If they cannot rule Barrow-Gate, they will turn it into an ash heap and blame the disaster on my father's supposed witchcraft."

I stared at the black vellum, a cold, familiar sensation settling behind my breastbone.

*Burn them all.*

I had heard those words before. 

Thirty years ago, in a cavernous throne room of black marble beneath the Red Keep, an old madman with filthy silver fingernails and sores across his mouth had screamed the exact same madness. King Aerys Targaryen had buried thousands of jars of green wildfire beneath the Great Sept of Baelor, beneath Flea Bottom, beneath the Dragonpit, beneath the City Gates. *Let Robert be king over charred bones and cooked meat,* the Mad King had laughed.

I remembered the smell of the pyromancer's oiled robes as my sword took him through the belly. I remembered the look in Aerys's eyes when he realized his Kingsguard knight had turned his back on sacred oaths to save a city of half a million souls who would never know his name. 

They had called me Kingslayer. They had spat upon my golden armor and whispered coward behind my back in every tavern from Sunspear to the Wall.

I looked down at the six copper-bound casks in the cart.

Then I looked across the table at the dying Margrave, at the terrified merchant factor, at the stubborn master smith, and at Lady Aurelia, whose green eyes were fixed upon me with desperate, unvoiced hope.

I closed the vellum slip and folded it into my tunic.

"Sergeant Kester," I said, my voice cutting through the damp cellar air like a cold blade. "Where are the prisoners?"

"Tied to the iron posts by the coke-kilns, Captain," Kester answered instantly.

"Bring the lead brother here," I commanded. "We need to know the exact chamber where the remaining twenty-four casks are resting. And Deputy Brand—gather twelve of your best scouts who know the drainage tunnels beneath the Sunburst Cloister."

"What are you planning, Captain?" Lady Aurelia asked, stepping toward me, her eyes searching my face.

"Your father needs the antidote before dawn," I said, checking the balance of my arming blade and sliding the steel into its plain leather scabbard. "And this city has five hours before Justinian lights the fuse. We are going to pay a visit to the High Academy."

I picked up my iron kettle-helm, settled it over my dark hair, and walked toward the coke-kilns.
