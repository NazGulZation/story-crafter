# Chapter 3: The Autopsy of a Martyr

For four exhausting hours through the quiet watches of the night, the silver-haired Time Lord had cross-examined him over refilled mugs of bitter chicory tea. 

The Doctor had interrogated him with the ruthless, surgical persistence of an imperial magistrate—wringing out every campaign, every contract, every coin spent on pine splints and poultices, and every scar etched into Kazuki's flesh across five years in the Eastern Marches. Kazuki had recited the party's formation until his throat was raspy: the offensive pyromancy of Lady Reiko, the silent scouting ambushes of Kaede, the flashy dueling strikes of Takumi, and the sixty-two major sorties where an ash-wood tower shield was the only barrier between survival and the charnel house.

Now, the chalk screeched against the slate with a sound sharp enough to set Kazuki's teeth on edge.

The Doctor stood before a tall, double-sided wooden chalkboard he had wheeled from the shadows of the library gallery, waving a thick stick of white calcium chalk with the aggressive precision of a dueling master. White dust settled across the crimson silk cuffs of his coat and powdered the shoulders of his dark velvet waistcoat. He had translated Kazuki's painful recollections into a manic, sprawling web of vector arrows, kinetic impact formulas, structural load curves, and jagged timelines that looked like the teeth of a saw.

"Look at this," the Doctor barked, tapping the slate with the chalk until the stick snapped in half. He tossed the broken nub over his shoulder without looking and immediately pulled a fresh one from his coat pocket. "Look at the numbers. Don't look at me with that pathetic, beaten-hound expression. Look at the board. What do you see?"

Kazuki stood five paces back, his hands resting awkwardly at his sides. He wore a clean, dark woolen tunic the Doctor had pulled from a cedar chest three corridors deep in the TARDIS interior. It was warm, dry, and free of mud, but Kazuki felt hollow inside it, as though the absence of his dented breastplate had stripped away his skin.

"I see... runes," Kazuki murmured, his voice hesitant. "Foreign equations. High-circle arithmetic."

"I see an idiot," the Doctor corrected flatly, spinning around and glaring through his bushy brows. "An absolute, certified, gold-plated municipal idiot. Five years! Sixty-two major subterranean sorties. Three hundred and fourteen defensive engagements. In eighty-seven percent of those encounters, your party's offensive spellcaster—this Reiko person with the grand titles and the flammable ego—began incantations requiring a minimum preparation window of one hundred and twenty seconds."

The Doctor jabbed the chalk into a cluster of jagged lines.

"One hundred and twenty seconds is an eternity on a battlefield, boy! In two minutes, a pack of charging iron-tusk boars can cross four furlongs, trample the supply wagons, and turn a choir of squawking mages into strawberry jam. Who stopped the boars?"

"I did," Kazuki said quietly. "I planted the shield."

"And while you were planting the shield," the Doctor continued, pacing across the grating with rapid, jerky strides, "where was the dashing young swordsman? The golden boy with the clean teeth and the expensive boots?"

"Takumi was... in the flanking position," Kazuki replied, his eyes dropping to the iron deck. "He was waiting for the beast's armor plates to crack. He said his rapier was forged of star-silver and could not be risked against blunt bone."

"Star-silver," the Doctor repeated witheringly, his rolling northern vowels biting into the air like acid. "Translation: he stood thirty yards away, looking terribly heroic in the torchlight, waiting for you to shatter your collarbone so he could step forward, poke the dying monster in the eye, and take bows for the guild scribes. And when the contract was paid, who received the bounty shares?"

"The guild charter dictates equal distribution after administrative costs," Kazuki said.

"Except you paid for your own shield repairs!" The Doctor slammed his chalk against the slate. "You paid the blacksmith's coal tax! You paid the apothecary for the leeches to drain the venom from your knees! You absorbed ninety-two percent of the party's physical structural wear, and when the bill came due, they treated your medical decay as a moral failure!"

Kazuki flinched, the words striking him with greater force than any ogre's club. He clenched his fists, the fingernails biting into his palms.

"I took an oath," Kazuki said, a tremor of defensive anger surfacing in his throat. "When we left Tanizaki... when Saki and I had nothing but two stale millet cakes and the dust on our sandals... I swore I would protect her. I swore that whatever horrors the guild sent our way, they would hit me first. That is what a vanguard does. That is what a man does."

The Doctor stopped pacing. 

He lowered his hand, the chalk hanging loosely between his bony fingers. He stepped toward Kazuki, his movements slow, deliberate, and stripped of all theatrical frenzy. The console behind them hummed its steady, deep note, washing the Doctor's silver hair in emerald chronal light. 

"You think that makes you noble, don't you?" the Doctor asked softly. The mockery was gone from his voice, replaced by an ancient, terrible sorrow that weighed heavier than the stone walls of Mizunara Keep. "You think suffering for people who don't care about you makes you holy. You think that if you just bleed enough, if you break enough bones, if you make yourself small enough and miserable enough, they will finally love you."

Kazuki stared at him, his breath catching in his throat.

"I've seen your kind across three dozen galaxies, Kazuki," the Doctor said, his pale blue-grey eyes fixed on the young man's face. "The martyr. The quiet boy who stands in the doorway while the house burns down around him, convinced that his ashes will keep the floor warm for the people inside. Do you know what happens to martyrs?"

Kazuki remained silent, his jaw clamped shut.

"They get walked on," the Doctor stated coldly. "They get used until their springs snap, and then they get tossed into the nearest ditch so the people who broke them don't have to look at the rust. They didn't discard you because you were weak, Kazuki. Understand that. Burn it into your skull. They discarded you because every single scar on your body was an indictment of their cowardice."

The Doctor pointed a sharp finger toward the blue door leading back to the muddy reality of the frontier.

"Every time Takumi looked at your crushed knee, he was reminded that his gold medal was bought with your torn ligaments. Every time that fire-witch saw your scorched forearms, she was reminded that her three-minute poetry recitals only worked because you were choking on wyvern smoke thirty feet ahead of her. You were the mirror that showed them who they really were: parasites living on credit drawn from your flesh."

"And Saki?" Kazuki's voice broke, the name tearing out of him like jagged glass. "What about Saki? We grew up in the same gutter! I took the bailiff's cane for her! I starved for three winters so she could have the clean flour! How could she look at me lying in that mud and laugh?"

The Doctor sighed, a long, weary exhalation that seemed to carry the dust of dead worlds. He turned back to the chalkboard, picked up a felt rag, and began wiping away the frantic equations with slow, heavy strokes.

"Unpayable debt," the Doctor said quietly, his back to Kazuki. "The most toxic poison in the human heart."

"Debt?"

"You saved her life, Kazuki. Not once, but a hundred times. You gave her food when she was starving. You gave her clothes when she was shivering. You gave her the very boots on her feet." The Doctor tossed the rag onto the chalk tray and turned back around, crossing his arms over his chest. "To a decent person, that breeds lifelong loyalty and humble gratitude. But Saki is not a decent person. Saki is a vain, terrified, small-minded climber who desperately wants to forget that she ever had mud between her toes."

The Doctor tapped his own temple.

"Every time she looked at you, she didn't see a protector. She saw Tanizaki. She saw the bitter millet, the tenant hovel, the smell of burnt straw, and the dirty beggar girl she used to be. You were the living anchor dragging her back into the poverty she hated. She didn't leave you for Takumi because Takumi is brave or kind; she left you for Takumi because Takumi has clean linen, a coat of arms, and no memory of her shame."

Kazuki leaned his back against the curved railing of the walkway. His knees felt steady, healed by the miraculous machinery of the vessel, but the hollow inside his chest seemed to widen into an abyss.

"So all of it was for nothing," Kazuki whispered. The words tasted like cold ashes. "Five years of broken bones. Five years of poison. All of it... just a joke."

"No," the Doctor said firmly, his sharp, rolling cadence snapping like a whip. He stepped directly into Kazuki's space, close enough that Kazuki could smell the dry tea and cedar on his breath. "The suffering was real. The courage was real. You stood between teeth and flesh, and you didn't run. That is not a joke. That is the rarest thing in any universe."

The Doctor reached out and tapped the center of Kazuki's chest with a rigid forefinger.

"The mistake was the client. You gave a king's ransom of courage to four thieves who couldn't tell the difference between iron and dirt. Now, you have a choice."

"What choice?" Kazuki asked, looking into those fierce, storm-grey eyes.

"You can curl up in the corner of my library, drown yourself in self-pity, and let them win," the Doctor said, his voice dropping to a low, challenging murmur. "Or you can stand up, drink your tea, and let me teach you what a shield is actually for."

The Doctor didn't wait for an answer. He turned on his heel, his dark velvet coat flaring around his boots, and walked toward the central console. He pulled a heavy brass lever, sending the central time rotor sliding upward into its glass housing with a deep, resonant chime.

Kazuki stood by the railing. He looked at his unscarred hands, then at the wiped chalkboard where faint white smears still traced the mathematics of his survival. He reached for the tin mug on the rail, gripped the warm metal, and drank.
