# Chapter 6: The Ghost in the Lower Valley

The autumn mist lay thick across the marshes of the Lower Valley, pooling in the reeds like grey wool and muffling the rushing roar of the Mizunara River three furlongs to the west. The air smelled of rotting sedge grass, wet river clay, and the sour yeast of damp grain sacks stacked high under oiled hemp tarpaulins.

A heavy four-wheeled freight wagon stood mired up to its hubs in the black silt of the river road, its rear axle snapped cleanly through seasoned ash. Two shaggy marsh ponies stood shivering in their harness, their breath puffing white in the freezing dusk.

Beside the broken wheel, an elderly carter named Hideo was frantically hauling sacks of coarse barley out of the slurry, his patched sleeves soaked with icy water. His daughter, a girl of sixteen with hollow cheeks and dirt-smudged knuckles, stood guard by the horses with a rusted hayfork gripped in both hands.

"Leave the sacks, Father!" the girl cried out, her voice thin with mounting panic. "Listen to the reeds! They’ve caught the scent of the flour!"

From the dense thicket of black willows along the river bank came the heavy, wet crunch of snapping branches.

A low, guttural snort vibrated through the marsh fog, followed by the clatter of bone against stone. Three shadows emerged from the gloom, their broad, bristled backs glistening with black river muck. They were river marsh boars—colossal, razor-backed beasts the size of draught oxen, their snouts crowned by curved plates of calcified bone and flanked by twin iron-hard tusks long enough to impale a horse through the breastplate. Their yellow eyes burned with territorial hunger, locked onto the leaking barley sacks.

Two hired guild guards in rusted iron helmets—novices hired for three coppers a day from the guild steps—took one look at the beasts, dropped their pikes into the mud, and bolted toward the safety of the fortress road without looking back.

"Cowards!" the girl screamed, backing against the shivering ponies, raising her hayfork with trembling arms.

The lead boar lowered its wedge-shaped skull, snorting hot vapor into the frost, and charged.

The ground shuddered under three tons of muscle and bone. Mud erupted in twin rooster-tails behind its splayed hooves as the beast closed the forty paces across the track in three heartbeats, hurtling straight toward the wagon's tongue.

A figure stepped out of the reeds.

Kazuki moved without haste. He wore a dark, hooded cloak over an unadorned leather brigandine, his left arm bearing the matte-grey kite shield of meteoritic iron. His center of gravity was low and balanced; his breath was steady in his chest.

Two weeks earlier, his instinct would have demanded that he plant both heels deep into the silt, lock his knees into rigid iron braces, lower his brow behind the rim, and let the boar's skull shatter against his breastbone, praying that his calcified ribs didn't puncture his lungs.

The Doctor’s voice echoed sharply through his memory: *A human being is not an iron stake. Defense is geometry.*

Kazuki didn't brace. 

As the colossal boar reached the three-pace mark, its curved tusks gleaming like butcher hooks, Kazuki stepped smoothly to his right—a shift of two inches, turning his hips forty-five degrees off the beast's forward vector. He presented the curved bevel of the meteor-iron shield not as an unyielding wall, but as an inclined ramp.

The collision was a sharp, ringing chime that cut through the fog like a bell.

The boar’s blunt snout struck the angled plate. Instead of slamming into a dead stop, the beast's immense kinetic force slid along the slick, dense alloy. Kazuki dropped his weight onto his rear leg, guiding the impact across his forearm with a fluid sweep of his wrist.

The boar’s own momentum carried it forward out of control. Its splayed hooves lost their grip on the wet clay, skidding sideways across the road. Unable to arrest its three-ton rush, the beast smashed headlong into a massive granite milestone planted beside the drainage ditch.

A sickening crack of splintering bone split the marsh air.

The lead boar collapsed into the ditch, its calcified skull fractured against the granite, its rear legs twitching once before going still.

Kazuki stood on the centerline of the road. His boots had slid barely half a foot in the silt. His ribs felt whole; his shoulders remained loose; his breath flowed through his throat without a single trace of venomous cough.

The two remaining boars skidded to a halt in the reeds, snorting in bewildered fury as they looked at the carcass of their pack leader.

The second boar lunged, swerving low to hook Kazuki’s thigh with an upward sweep of its left tusk.

Kazuki didn't retreat. He pivoted on the ball of his left foot, letting the tusk whistle through empty air an inch from his knee. As the beast overextended, Kazuki brought the bottom point of the kite shield down like an executioner’s axe, driving the heavy iron tip directly into the nerve cluster behind the boar’s shoulder blade.

A dull concussive shockwave rippled through the beast's spine. The boar shrieked, its front legs buckling beneath its chest, sliding ten feet through the mud before scrambling upright with a limp and fleeing squealing into the deep willows.

The third boar took one look at the lone figure standing over the dead leader, turned its broad flank, and plunged into the river rapids, vanishing into the grey mist.

Silence settled over the marsh road, broken only by the panting of the ponies and the heavy drip of water from the broken wagon bed.

Hideo collapsed to his knees in the mud, his hands clasped together in frantic prayer. "Mercy... divine mercy... you killed an elder tusker with a single blow! Who... who are you, master vanguard?"

Kazuki lowered the dark shield, sliding his arm from the ox-hide strap. He felt the cold river wind against his face, clean and sweet, carrying no smell of rot or bile.

"I am nobody," Kazuki said, his voice quiet, steady, and low. "Just a traveler passing along the ditch."

"Please, my lord!" The girl ran forward, tears cutting clean streaks through the grime on her cheeks. She reached into the wagon seat and hauled out a worn leather pouch, thrusting it toward Kazuki with trembling hands. "There are forty coppers in here, and two silver pieces from our harvest sale! Take it! It is all we have, but you saved our lives and our winter grain!"

Kazuki looked at the pouch. He saw the frayed stitches, the salt stains on the pigskin, and the desperate, hollow eyes of the farmer who had worked four seasons in the mud to fill it. 

He thought of Takumi pocketing five hundred gold royals from the guild master, sneering at commoners who couldn't afford horse-flesh. He thought of Saki demanding fifty silvers for a simple purification prayer over a sick child.

Kazuki gently pushed the girl's hands back toward her chest.

"Keep your coin," Kazuki said. "You will need it to pay the wheelwright in the lower district."

"We cannot let you walk away with nothing!" Hideo protested, scrambling to his feet and bowing until his forehead touched his knees. "The gods will curse us if we turn an iron hero away without recompense!"

"I am not an iron hero," Kazuki replied, his mouth tightening into a wry, slightly sour line that carried an uncanny reflection of a certain silver-haired traveler. "And gods are remarkably poor accountants. If you must give me something, give me three of those dried persimmons hanging from the wagon bows, and a ladle of clean well-water."

The old carter blinked in astonished silence. Hurrying to the wagon, he cut down three large, sugar-frosted persimmons strung on hemp twine and filled a wooden cup from his earthenware cask.

Kazuki took the persimmons, tucking two into his coat pocket and biting into the third. The fruit was dense, chewy, and intensely sweet, coating his tongue with the rich flavor of autumn sun. He drank the water in three long gulps, wiped his mouth with his sleeve, and set the wooden cup on the wagon tailboard.

He stepped over to the broken rear corner of the wagon bed. Squatting down, he wedged his shoulder beneath the heavy timber frame, found his footing in the clay, and straightened his legs.

The four-ton wagon rose three inches out of the mud with a groan of timber.

"Hideo," Kazuki said calmly, holding the massive frame steady on his back. "Roll that cedar log beneath the axle bed so your horses don't pull against the ditch."

The carter and his daughter scrambled into motion, shoving the log into place. When Kazuki eased his weight off, the broken wagon rested securely above the muck, ready for a replacement wheel.

"May the ancestors watch your path," Hideo whispered, staring at Kazuki with reverent awe. "What name shall we speak at the temple altar?"

Kazuki pulled his wool hood over his head, resting the meteor-iron shield against his left hip.

"Speak no name," Kazuki said. "Just tell the people in the lower district that the road is open."

He turned and walked down the mist-shrouded road toward the western treeline.

Two hundred paces up the ridge, concealed behind a cluster of wild sumac bushes, a young guild scout in the yellow livery of the apprentice corps lowered a brass spyglass. His hands were shaking so hard the lenses rattled against his teeth.

He had watched the entire encounter—the fluid, impossible deflection of an elder river tusker, the effortless dispatch of the pack, the lifting of a four-ton grain wagon, and the strange, hooded warrior who took three dried persimmons instead of silver.

"That wasn't a novice," the boy breathed, his heart hammering against his ribs. "That wasn't any vanguard on the registry."

Slipping the spyglass into his satchel, the boy turned and ran at full sprint along the ridge toward the limestone gates of Mizunara Keep.
