# Chapter 1: The Cold Ditch of Mizunara

Rain fell in grey sheets across the lower ditch of Mizunara Keep, turning the refuse of the butcher stalls and the overflow of the latrines into a freezing, knee-deep sludge. The water carried the stench of rotten cabbage, stale ale, and the rancid mutton tallow greasing the axles of the freight wagons parked along the timber palisade. Through the fog, twenty feet above the ditch wall, the stained-glass lanterns of the Gilded Tankard tavern glowed like molten amber, casting long streaks of red and gold across the slick surface of the mud.

Kazuki lay wedged between two rotted hemlock posts, half-submerged in the drainage muck. Every shallow draw of air sent a knife of white agony through his right ribs, where three separate fractures—calcified poorly over three seasons of relentless expeditions—ground against his chest wall. His right knee was a swollen mass of torn ligaments and dead cartilage, crushed two weeks prior by the iron cudgel of an ogre warlord in the subterranean vaults beneath the Red Ridge. His breath wheezed through lips cracked and blackened by the lingering trace of wyvern bile, tasting of sour copper and dying lung tissue.

Inside the tavern, someone dropped a heavy firkin of spiced cherry wine onto a trestle table, followed by a roar of laughter that cut straight through the drone of the autumn sleet.

"To the vanguard's glorious end!" a voice rang out, bright, ringing, and theatrical. It belonged to Takumi of House Kaneshiro. The vowels carried that effortless, musical cadence reserved for the sons of capital magistrates who had never spent a single winter night sleeping on wet straw. "A noble sacrifice for the registry! The guild clerk stamped the promotion parchment without a second glance. Gold rank, my friends! Five hundred crowns per sovereign bounty, full exemptions from the frontier levies, and private quarters in the Upper Bastion!"

A chorus of pewter mugs crashed together. Metal clattered against seasoned oak; boots stomped in rhythm across the dry, rushes-strewn floorboards above.

Kazuki turned his cheek slightly, pressing his burning temple against the cold slime of the ditch bank. Mud filled the hollow of his ear, dulling the sound of the rain to a thick, aquatic thrum. His fingers twitched within the shredded remnants of his pigskin gauntlets. The leather was stiff with dried salt and old boar gore, stiffened by days of exposure. His tower shield—the three-foot slab of layered ash and folded charcoal iron that had stopped eighteen hundred lethal blows across five years of mercenary contracts—was gone. Takumi had taken the iron fittings and the embossed guild plate to pawn for tavern credit, leaving only the splintered wooden core floating face-down in the privy ditch fifty paces down the wall.

"You speak too loudly, Takumi," a woman's voice drifted through an open casement shutter, accompanied by the dry rattle of bone dice in a leather cup. It was Saki. 

The sound of her voice produced a dull, throbbing knot behind Kazuki's breastbone, more persistent than the broken ribs. They had left the scorched millet fields of Tanizaki together when they were fourteen years old, barefoot and coughing from the seasonal soot of the landlord's burnings. He had stolen salt fish from the bailiff's pantry to keep her belly full; he had taken six lashes from a magistrate's cane when they were caught sleeping under the granary eaves; he had pawned his mother's iron hair-pin to buy her first bleached-linen acolyte vestment so the temple elders would teach her the prayers of purification.

"Let them hear," Takumi replied, his tone laced with easy, arrogant laughter. A wet, smacking sound followed—a careless kiss planted on the nape of her neck. "The whole lower district ought to know who keeps their bellies fed. Kazuki served his turn. He took the hits, as an anvil must. When an anvil cracks, you discard it and purchase an iron ingot from a proper capital smith. You cannot expect highborn duelists to carry dead weight into the royal hunting grounds."

"I told him three months ago that his lung was failing," Saki said. Her tone was smooth, flat, devoid of tremor or hesitation. "He slowed our advance by four furlongs a day through the volcanic tunnels. Every time he coughed, the smell of burnt bile made my eyes water. It was becoming embarrassing before the senior inspectors. A divine acolyte cannot be seen healing a beggar who limps like an old mule. It compromises our standing."

"Besides," added Reiko, her aristocratic diction clipped and frigid as an icicle, "his defensive stance was obsolete. Three seconds to plant a baseline pivot! My fire incantations require four hundred yards of clean field, and he could barely hold fifty before collapsing into the grit. Good riddance. Now we can hire a proper royal dragoon from the capital escort."

"The guild share split five ways was mathematically inefficient anyway," Kaede murmured from the shadows of the tavern corner, her voice accompanied by the rhythmic scrape of a whetstone across an oiled dagger. "Four shares yield twenty-five percent higher net return per contract. The math is simple. He is gone. The books are balanced."

Laughter flared again, warm and buoyant, accompanied by the bright strumming of a lute and the clatter of roasted capon bones tossed onto tavern trenchers.

Kazuki closed his eyes. 

Cold rainwater pooled in the hollows of his collarbones, soaking through the threadbare linen of his tunic. The numbness was spreading upward now, creeping past his crushed knees into his pelvis, chilling his stomach until the nausea gave way to a heavy, leaden weight. He had known this moment was coming for six months. He had seen the way Takumi looked at his rusted armor; he had seen the subtle, disgusted flinch in Saki's eyes whenever his scarred, blackened fingers reached out to pass her a water-skin. He had told himself that if he simply held the line a little longer, took one more manticore spike, shielded one more fireblast, they would remember where they came from.

They had not remembered. They had simply learned that an anvil does not complain when you strike it, until the day it splits in two.

*So this is the end of the contract,* he thought, staring at the grey underbelly of the clouds. His breath came in ragged, shallow puffs of vapor that vanished instantly into the downpour. *Just mud. A ditch behind the privy. A copper coin's worth of earth.*

Then, beneath the drumming of the sleet, a sound tore through the lower ditch.

It was unlike anything Kazuki had ever heard in the frontier marches. It was neither the roar of a subterranean beast nor the crack of lightning across the parapets. It was a rhythmic, wheezing, grinding groan—a mechanical respiration that seemed to shudder through the very bedrock beneath his spine. 

*Vworp. Vworp. Vworp.*

The acoustic pulse vibrated through the mud, rattling the loose hemlock posts and sending ripples across the stagnant puddles. The puddle three inches from Kazuki's nose flattened into concentric silver rings, shuddering in time with the deep, discordant rasp. The air grew sharp with the smell of scorched amber and ozone, cutting through the reek of sewer filth with the clean, biting chill of high-altitude frost.

*Vworp. Vworp. Vworp.*

A patch of empty air beside the hemlock wall began to warp, bending the grey light of the rain into strange, angular refractions. Out of that distorted haze, solid form coalesced with the suddenness of a dropped hammer. 

It was a box. 

A tall, rectangular wooden cabinet of deep, faded blue paint, its surface weathered by unimaginable weather, studded with white-framed window panes near the roof and capped by a small, pulsing lamp that flickered with white fire. On its door hung a small white placard bearing foreign, sharply stamped lettering that Kazuki's fever-glazed eyes could not decipher.

The wheezing died away with a long, metallic sigh, like the settling of a colossal iron boiler.

For two heartbeats, the only sound was the rain slapping against the blue timber.

Then, the right-hand door unlatched with a sharp brass click.

The door swung inward by half a foot. A face peered out into the sodden gloom.

It was an older man, gaunt and severe, his silver hair swept back like storm clouds against his pale forehead. He had striking, terrifyingly sharp eyebrows that looked capable of cutting glass, framing two pale blue-grey eyes that swept across the muddy ditch with the speed of an inquisitor's ledger. He wore a dark velvet coat lined with deep crimson silk, a white collared shirt open at the throat, and an expression of utter, profound disgust.

The man looked up at the grey sky, feeling a raindrop strike the bridge of his nose. He sniffed, his mouth twisting into a sour, puckered grimace.

"Scotland in November," the man muttered in a sharp, dry, rolling cadence with hard consonants that clipped through the rain like shears. "Or a very badly maintained laundry house in Manchester. Why does it always have to be damp? I asked for the rings of Akhaten, and the navigation computer gives me a drainage ditch behind a third-rate public house."

To Kazuki’s fading consciousness, the strange words—*Scotland*, *Manchester*—sounded like the bizarre, untranslatable syllables of some unknown continent across the outer oceans.

He adjusted his dark velvet lapels, muttering under his breath, before his pale eyes dropped to the mud below the TARDIS threshold.

He stopped.

The eyebrows lowered, drawing together in an intense, analytical glare. The silver-haired man leaned forward, his long fingers gripping the wooden doorframe.

He saw the broken hemlock posts. He saw the black rot of wyvern venom spreading through the veins of Kazuki's exposed neck. He saw the pulverized right knee, the splintered bone pressing against the bruised purple skin, and the shallow, failing flutter of the boy's chest. Above them, through the illuminated tavern window, the drunken shouts and the clinking of pewter tankards continued unabated.

The man's mouth tightened into a hard, razor-thin line. 

"Well, now," the stranger said, his voice dropping from cranky annoyance into something cold, steady, and dangerously quiet. "That is remarkably poor party management."

He pushed the door fully open and stepped directly out into the ankle-deep filth, the fine leather of his boots sinking into the sewage without a single flicker of hesitation. He crouched down beside the hemlock post, his dark velvet coat dragging in the muck, and reached out with two long, bony fingers to press them against the carotid artery beneath Kazuki's jaw.

His touch was dry and shocking in its warmth.

"Listen to me, boy," the silver-haired man said, his pale eyes boring straight through Kazuki's glazed, dying gaze. "You're doing that thing humans do when they think the universe owes them a dramatic exit. You're giving up. Stop it. It's thoroughly untidy and it makes a terrible mess of the carpet."

Kazuki tried to draw breath to speak, but only a wet, bloody bubble surfaced between his lips.

"Don't talk," the man snapped, sliding one hand beneath Kazuki's shoulder and the other behind his shattered knee with an astonishing, iron-hard strength that belied his slender frame. "And whatever you do, don't die on my doorframe. Clara took three months to get the sulfur out of the woodwork, and I have no intention of repeating the exercise."

With a sharp grunt of effort, the old man lifted Kazuki clean out of the freezing mud, cradling the battered bulwark against his crimson velvet coat, and turned back toward the open blue door.
