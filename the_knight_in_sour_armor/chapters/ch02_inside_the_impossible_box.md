# Chapter 2: Inside the Impossible Box

The threshold was a boundary between two incompatible realities.

One moment, Kazuki's face was pressed into the freezing sewage of the ditch, the stench of rotted offal and stagnant ditch water filling his throat; the next, he was swept through an opening four feet wide into an immense, vaulted cathedral of warm amber light, humming machinery, and dry, conditioned air. 

The scent changed instantly. The damp chill of the autumn downpour gave way to the sharp, electric tang of ionized oxygen, warm brass gears, beeswax polish, and thousands of centuries of dry, yellowed paper. High above, suspended within a soaring lattice of geometric arches, great roundels glowed with soft, pearlescent radiance, illuminating concentric iron walkways, sweeping balustrades of darkened timber, and towering bookcases crammed with leather-bound folios. In the center of the vast chamber stood a massive, hexagonal console of polished steel and brass, crowned by a translucent glass column that rose and fell in a slow, hypnotic rhythm, glowing with emerald chronal light.

*Hum-hum-thrum.*

The deep vibration resonance sang through the iron grille underfoot, steady and rhythmic like the pulse of a sleeping titan.

"Right," the silver-haired man grunted, maneuvering Kazuki's dead weight past the hexagonal console with surprising balance. "Mind the grating. If you drop bile into the tertiary cooling exhaust, the thermal regulators throw a sulk and we spend the next three decades smelling like a boiled cabbage patch."

Kazuki hung in the man's arms, his head spinning with violent vertigo. His eyes widened, darting from the blue wooden door behind them—a simple pair of timber panels barely wide enough for two men—to the colossal, echoing expanse of the hall stretching fifty paces in every direction.

"It... it is..." Kazuki rasped, his cracked throat catching on the words. "The box... it is..."

"Don't," the man interrupted, his sharp, rolling northern vowels ringing off the vaulted ceiling. He dropped Kazuki onto a wide, leather-upholstered reclining bench set against a curved brass bulkhead. "Don't say it. Please. Two thousand years of wandering about, and everybody always says the exact same thing. It is mathematically tedious. Yes, it's bigger on the inside. No, it's not a pocket dimension created by an astral glyph. It is dimensionally transcendental, which is simply a very precise way of saying the interior and exterior exist in entirely different spatial planes. Now lie back and keep your mouth shut."

Kazuki collapsed onto the leather, gasping as his crushed ribs shifted. The leather beneath his back was supple, warm, and smelled faintly of lavender oil and beeswax. His mud-caked boots left thick, greasy smears across the buffed floor, but the silver-haired man ignored the filth, already striding toward a bank of circular toggle switches on the central console.

"Let's see the damage," the silver-haired man muttered, pulling a slender cylindrical device of polished chrome and dark carbon-fiber from his inside coat pocket. He clicked the emitter head with his thumb.

A shrill, buzzing whine filled the chamber as the tip of the rod flared with a brilliant, pulsing emerald light. 

The man swept the buzzing tool over Kazuki's body, starting at the crown of his skull and moving down across his collarbones, his ribs, and his pulverized knee. The light flickered rapidly, casting jittery green shadows across the man's severe, hawkish face. Tiny digital glyphs, green lines of script, and complex geometric waveforms floated momentarily in the beam, reflecting in the pale irises beneath his heavy brows.

"Fascinating," the man murmured, his brows drawing into a deep, furious v-shape. "A biological cocktail of neurotoxins, necrotizing enzymes, and calcified micro-fractures. Three shattered ribs held together by scar tissue and sheer stubborn idiocy. Right patella crushed into seven fragments; anterior cruciate ligament entirely dissolved by acidic bile. And your lungs... good grief, boy, you've been breathing through a sponge soaked in sulfuric sludge for at least eight months."

He turned off the buzzing device with a flick of his wrist and slid it back into his velvet coat. He leaned over the bench, staring down at Kazuki with a cold, piercing intensity that made the young bulwark flinch.

"Who did this to you?" the man demanded.

"The... the beasts," Kazuki choked out, his vision swimming. "The subterranean burrowers... the ogres in the deep faults... I had to... I had to hold the line."

"I didn't ask about the monsters, boy," the silver-haired man snapped, his tone dry as tinder. "Monsters bite because they're hungry or territorial or have very poorly designed nervous systems. That's just biology. I'm asking about the people who let you keep walking on a leg that should have been put in a cast half a year ago. Who stood behind you while you took those hits?"

Kazuki swallowed, tasting the bitter copper of his own blood. "My party. The Silver... no, they are Gold rank now. Takumi of House Kaneshiro. And Saki. She is... she is an acolyte of the White Dawn."

"An acolyte," the man repeated, the word tasting sour in his mouth. He began pacing back and forth across the iron grating, his hands clasped firmly behind the small of his back, coat tails swishing against his calves. "A healer, then? One of those self-righteous practitioners who waves a branch and mumbles about divine purity? She had the capability to mend this tissue, and she left it to fester?"

"She said... my body was too scarred," Kazuki whispered, his voice sinking into the hum of the engine. "She said the divine grace does not flow cleanly through blackened flesh. She said every time she cast a mending prayer on my knee, it exhausted her spiritual reserves for Takumi's sword enchantments. She said... I was holding them back."

The older man stopped dead in his tracks. 

The silence that descended upon the console room was absolute, heavy with an ancient, terrifying gravity. The man's pale eyes narrowed to slits. His long fingers flexed once, twice, before settling back against his spine.

"Spiritual reserves," the man said softly. Every syllable was etched with razor-sharp contempt. "Human beings. You build a cathedral out of mud, call it an empire, and immediately start looking for someone to kick into the foundation stones so the roof doesn't leak on your silk slippers."

He turned sharply on his heel, striding to a curved brass alcove near the secondary walkway. He pulled down a polished copper lever with a heavy clatter. A brass tray slid out from the wall, bearing a pressurized glass cylinder filled with a shimmering, opalescent mist and a pair of flexible acoustic cups.

"Right," the silver-haired man said, returning to the bench. "This is going to feel peculiar. It will tickle, then it will burn like very hot nettles, and then your cellular structure will decide to stop behaving like a sack of crushed gravel."

He placed the acoustic cups directly against Kazuki's swollen knee and pressed the valve on the cylinder.

A high-pitched, harmonic chord rang through the chamber. Kazuki gasped as a wave of intense, prickling heat flooded through his right leg. It felt as though a thousand tiny needles were stitching together the severed tendons beneath his skin, knitting the pulverized fragments of his kneecap into a solid, seamless dome. He clamped his teeth together to stifle a cry, the muscles in his neck standing out like cords.

"Breathe," the traveler ordered calmly, leaning over him with his hands resting on his hips. "Deep breaths through the diaphragm. Stop holding your breath. It's a dreadful habit. People hold their breath when they're terrified, which deprives the brain of oxygen, which makes them stupider, which makes them more terrified. Stop being stupid."

Kazuki forced his lungs open. 

The air entering his chest felt cool, clean, and utterly devoid of the damp rot of the ditch. The opalescent mist seeped through his skin, chasing the black venom from his veins. Across his forearms, the angry purple tracks of wyvern bile faded, receding like spilled ink washed away by fresh spring water. The grinding agony in his ribs smoothed out, the shattered bone edges fusing under the gentle, acoustic pressure of the Gallifreyan field.

Within three minutes, the burning subsided into a deep, soothing warmth that radiated through his bones.

Kazuki lay staring at the soaring ceiling, his chest rising and falling in smooth, effortless rhythm. For the first time in five years, there was no sharp catching in his throat, no hot pulse of poison behind his eyes, no agonizing throb in his joints.

He reached down with trembling fingers, pressing his hand against his right kneecap. It was whole. The skin was smooth, pale, and firm.

"How..." Kazuki whispered, tears stinging his eyes against his will. "What kind of high-tier magic is this? Did you use an elixir of the royal vault? A sacred relic?"

"Magic," the older man scoffed, waving his hand dismissively as he strode back to the console to adjust a brass dial. "There's no such thing as magic. It's just applied physics with an inflated sense of self-importance. Cellular regeneration triggered by targeted resonance waves and sub-molecular tissue scaffolding. Any decent field medic on the fourth moon of Jagrafess could do it with a spare battery and a rusty spanner."

The silver-haired stranger pulled a small iron kettle from a brass heating plate beside the steering mechanism and poured dark, steaming liquid into two heavy tin mugs. He carried one over and thrust it into Kazuki's hands.

"Drink that," he commanded.

Kazuki took the mug with both hands. The metal was hot against his palms, grounding him in the physical reality of the chamber. The liquid was dark, bitter, and tasted strongly of toasted chicory, dried orange peel, and something sharp and peaty that warmed the back of his throat like hearth fire.

"What is this?" Kazuki asked, taking a cautious sip.

"Tea," the stranger replied, leaning against the curved rail of the console with his own mug raised. "Proper tea. None of that dried grass and elderberry nonsense your local alchemists peddle. Now sit up. Try the leg. Slowly, mind you. The cellular bonding has settled, but your balance center needs a moment to catch up with the fact that you aren't lopsided anymore."

Kazuki swung his legs over the edge of the leather bench. 

His boots touched the iron grille. He hesitated, his knuckles white around the tin mug, bracing himself for the familiar, nauseating agony that had greeted every step for three long years.

He stood.

There was no pain. His right knee bore his full weight with solid, effortless firmness. His spine straightened; his shoulders squared; his chest expanded without the slightest twinge of bone grinding on bone. He took one step forward, then another, his boots ringing crisply against the metal floor.

He looked down at his boots, then around the vast, warm, humming chamber, and finally back at the silver-haired man sipping tea from a tin cup.

"I can walk," Kazuki whispered, pressing both hands against his mended knee, feeling the solid miracle of whole tendon and smooth bone under his palms. "The venom... the splintered patella... it's gone. Who are you? Are you an arch-mage from the High Spires? A royal physician?"

The stranger lowered his tin mug, peering down over the rim with dry, razor-sharp severity, his heavy attack eyebrows arched high.

"I am the Doctor," he said simply, each word clipped off with the clean, rolling authority of an ancient northern law. "Not an arch-mage, certainly not a royal physician—dreadful patients, always whining about their gout—and decidedly not an amateur cobbler. I am a Time Lord. And this," he tapped the brass console rail with a bony knuckle, "is the TARDIS. Now drink your tea before it behaves like cold sludge. We have a great deal of work to do, and I have very little patience for self-pity."
