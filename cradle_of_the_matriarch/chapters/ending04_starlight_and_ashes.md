# Ending 4: Starlight and Ashes

The morning wind that blew through the shattered clerestory of the cathedral was cold, carrying the clean, sharp scent of melting snow from the high peaks of the Astraean mountains. 

It was the first clean air that had touched the heart of Landosol in three years. It ruffled the torn silk of Yui’s sleeves, stirred the soot-blackened feathers of the swallows nesting in the high eaves, and carried away the lingering stench of burned copper and sulfur. 

Down upon the marble flags of the altar dais, the light was pale gold, slanting through the jagged gap in the roof to pool across my chest.

My head rested in Yui’s lap. Her tears fell steadily, warm and heavy, splashing against my cheek and running down into my collar to mingle with the dried salt and blood on my throat. Her small, trembling hands were pressed flat against my breastplate, desperately trying to feel for a pulse that had slowed to a ragged, irregular crawl.

"Stay with us, Yuuki..." she whispered, her voice cracking into breathless, ragged sobs. "Please... you promised you'd walk home with us. The sky is open. Look up... the stars are still there, and the sun is coming up over the mountains. You can see it... you just have to keep your eyes open..."

Beside her, Hiyori sat on her heels, her broad shoulders hunched forward, her face buried in her bare, split hands. The iron plates of her gauntlets lay discarded on the bloody stones. Her fingers were trembling violently, her chest heaving with silent, shuddering gasps that shook her entire frame. She had fought every beast and war-machine Kaiser had hurled at the district without taking a single step backward, but now, faced with the stillness spreading through my limbs, she looked like a lost child abandoned in an empty field.

"Why him?" Hiyori choked out, her voice muffled against her bloodied palms. "We were supposed to protect him. We swore we'd take the blows... we swore we'd be the shield..."

Rei knelt at my right side, holding my right hand tight between both of hers. Her gloves were off. Her fingers were calloused from ten thousand hours of sword practice, rough and firm, gripping my limp wrist as if she could physically hold my soul inside my veins by sheer force of will. Her sharp blue eyes were dry, but her jaw was locked so hard the muscles jumped beneath her pale skin, and her lower lip bled where her teeth had pierced it.

"He chose it, Hiyori," Rei said, though her own voice broke on the final word, sounding small and hoarse. "He took the burden because he knew our vessels couldn't survive the fire. He gave us the sky."

I managed to turn my head a fraction of an inch, my gaze drifting across their faces.

The pain had receded into a vast, numb quiet. The sapphire crystal that had encased my left arm and shoulder was losing its solid cohesion; tiny flecks of golden light were beginning to detach from the mineral surface, dissolving into sparkling grains of stardust that drifted upward into the morning sunbeams like motes of pollen. 

I lifted my right hand, my fingers heavy as lead, and touched Yui’s wet cheek.

"Don't cry, Yui," I whispered. My voice was barely a dry rasp, but in the echoing stillness of the ruined church, every syllable was clear.

"How can I not cry?" she wept, leaning down until her forehead pressed against mine, her breath warm against my cooling skin. "You took all my poison... you took the nightmare I made and drank it down... you're dying because of my stupid wish..."

"Your wish was beautiful," I said, a faint smile touching the corner of my lips as my thumb brushed away a tear from the corner of her eye. "It was... a wish for smiles. Look outside, Yui. Listen to the city."

Through the collapsed bronze gates of the cathedral, the first sounds of a waking world drifted across the square.

It was not the mechanical drone of Kaiser’s converters, nor the rhythmic clatter of porcelain boots. It was the sound of a woman crying out in sudden, joyful disbelief. It was the sound of window shutters being thrown wide against weathered sills. Down in the avenue where the ash had settled, footsteps were running—hundreds of footsteps, thousands of footsteps. 

People were walking out into the morning light. 

Men who had spent three years staring blankly at loom shuttles were looking up at the blue sky, blinking against the unaccustomed brightness of the sun, tears cutting clean tracks through the factory grime on their cheeks. Mothers were carrying their sleeping children out onto balcony railings, holding them up toward the warm gold rays that touched the tiled roofs of Astraea. The nightmare had shattered. The inverted frequency had evaporated, leaving behind eight hundred thousand souls who had awakened from the numbness to find their minds clear, their memories intact, and their lives their own again.

A distant bell began to ring. 

It was the great bronze bell of the municipal clocktower, swinging free in its damaged belfry as three elderly carters hauled on the frayed hemp rope together. Its deep, resonant peal rolled across the central basin, washing over the rooftops like the heartbeat of a resurrected kingdom.

"They're awake," Hiyori whispered, lifting her head from her hands. She listened to the distant cheers and weeping, her amber eyes wide and shining in the dawn light. "Everyone... they're really awake."

"You did that, Yuuki," Rei said softly, leaning down to press her forehead against the back of my hand. "You saved them all."

"We did it," I corrected her, my breath growing shallower, each word requiring an immense, deliberate effort of will. "Twinkle Wish... gave them back their morning."

I looked up at Yui one last time. 

Her amber eyes held a lifetime of shared memories—every bowl of warm soup, every walk home through the twilight streets of Landosol, every clumsy sword swing she had cheered from the guildhall porch. I wanted to tell her to bake the clover bread again. I wanted to tell Hiyori to keep her fists high, and Rei to never let her blade grow cold. 

The words faded in my throat, but she understood. She nodded through her tears, squeezing my fingers with gentle, fierce devotion.

"I'll smile, Yuuki," Yui whispered, her voice trembling as she forced her lips into the small, radiant curve I had loved since the day we met. "I promise. Every single morning, when the sun comes up over the mountains... I will look at the sky, and I will smile for you."

"Good," I breathed.

The last beat of my heart fluttered against my ribs and went still.

My left arm dissolved completely into a fountain of warm, golden stardust. The sparkling grains of light rose from the altar dais, swirling in a gentle, luminous spiral through the shattered roof, dancing on the mountain breeze before scattering across the waking streets of Landosol. 

Rei closed her eyes, bowing her head in silence as a single tear escaped her lashes, tracing down her pale cheek to fall upon the cold marble. Hiyori reached out, pulling my lifeless right hand against her chest, weeping openly as the morning bells filled the sky. And Yui held me close against her heart, her face turned upward toward the rising sun, her smile shining through her tears like starlight through the rain.

***

Six months later, the Grand Plaza of Astraea was filled with the clamor of rebuilding.

The three feet of violet sludge had long since been scraped from the flagstones, washed away into the river channels by the heavy spring rains. Where the blackened trenches of dead roses had stood along the royal avenue, new shoots of green were pushing up through the freshly tilled soil, their tender leaves catching the autumn sunlight. In the market stalls, timber awnings were being raised by carpenters from Sector 4; carters from the southern farms were unloading crates of yellow squash and barrels of fresh mountain cider; and the aroma of roasted chestnuts drifted through the cool, brisk air.

At the center of the square, where the cracked lily fountain had once bled Kaiser’s poison, a new monument stood upon the restored marble pavement.

It was a simple, circular basin of white Astraean granite, filled with clear mountain water that mirrored the blue sky above. In the center of the pool rose a square plinth of polished quartz, bearing no likeness or statue. 

Upon the face of the stone, carved in deep, crisp letters by the guild of masons, were four lines of verse:

*He gave his heart to the storm,*
*That we might walk in the dawn.*
*Look to the morning star,*
*And remember his name.*

Beside the inscription, four stars had been chiseled into the stone, their points linked together by a single delicate ribbon of carved silver.

The midday bells finished their chime, and three figures walked across the gravel path toward the fountain.

Yui wore her familiar pink robe, though its hem was clean and newly mended with fine linen thread. Her hair was brushed until it shone like chestnut silk, held back by a silver hairpin shaped like a four-petaled clover. In her arms she carried a flat wicker basket covered with a clean white napkin.

Beside her, Hiyori wore a new red tunic, her forearms bare and crisscrossed with faded, pale scars. Her solar gauntlets hung from her hips, polished to a bright, golden sheen that caught the afternoon sun. She walked with an easy, confident stride, waving cheerfully to the fruit sellers who called out greetings from their stalls.

Rei walked on Yui’s left, her dark hair gathered in a neat queue, her blue cloak draped over her shoulder. Her silver rapier rested in a new leather scabbard at her hip, its crossguard polished until it gleamed like ice.

The three girls stepped up to the edge of the granite basin.

Around them, the people of the market paused in their business. A carter hauling a load of kiln bricks tipped his cap; two women carrying baskets of washed wool stopped on the path, bowing their heads with quiet reverence before moving on. The people of Landosol knew who had brought down the converters, and they knew the price the three girls had paid to walk among them.

Yui set the wicker basket down upon the granite rim of the fountain. She lifted the cloth, revealing three thick slices of freshly baked clover bread, golden-brown and steaming with the fragrance of sweet wild honey and toasted wheat.

Beside the bread, she placed a fourth slice upon a small blue porcelain plate.

"The oven worked well today, Yuuki," Yui said softly, her voice carrying a calm, serene warmth that had no bitterness left in it. "The flour from the southern valley was sweet, just like you remembered. The baker down the street gave us two jars of clover honey. He said his little girl walks to school every morning without looking over her shoulder now."

Hiyori leaned against the granite rim, resting her chin on her forearms, looking down at the clear water where small golden leaves drifted across the surface.

"I started training the new city watch yesterday," Hiyori said with a wry, crooked grin. "A bunch of clumsy farm boys from Sector 3. None of them know how to throw a proper straight punch. I made them run the perimeter walls until their tongues were hanging out. You would have laughed your head off watching them trip over their own scabbards."

She reached down, dipping her fingertips into the cold water, watching the ripples spread across the reflection of the sky.

"We miss you, partner," Hiyori murmured, her voice softening. "Every single day. When the guildhall gets quiet at night... it feels too big. But we're keeping the fires lit. We're keeping the table set."

Rei stepped forward, placing her gloved hand flat against the polished quartz of the plinth.

"The royal guard has begun clearing the northern passes," Rei said, her sharp features softening as she looked at the chiseled stars. "The remaining puppet garrisons are retreating toward the imperial borders. The land is healing, Yuuki. The roads are open, and travelers are moving between the towns without fear. We took up a request this morning to escort a caravan of medical supplies to the southern clinics. We leave at first light tomorrow."

Rei pulled a sprig of wild crimson thorn-rose from her belt—the first bloom of the new season, cut that morning from the avenue gardens. She laid the blossom gently upon the quartz plinth, beside the blue porcelain plate.

"We are carrying the covenant forward," Rei whispered. "Your blade will never be forgotten."

A gentle gust of wind swept across the plaza, rustling the green boughs of the newly planted linden trees lining the esplanade. The breeze carried the scent of baking bread, river water, and wild roses, swirling around the three girls like a warm, comforting embrace.

Yui looked up toward the sky.

The leaden clouds of the cradle were gone forever. Above the rooftops of Landosol, the autumn heavens stretched wide and deep, a pure, brilliant expanse of azure blue that reached all the way to the distant sea. And high in the east, faint against the midday brightness, the morning star shone with steady, undying starlight.

Yui took a deep breath of the clean mountain air. 

She turned to Hiyori and Rei, reaching out with both hands to take theirs. Hiyori gripped her fingers with warm, fierce strength; Rei held her hand with steady, protective devotion.

Yui smiled. 

It was the smile she had promised him—bright, clear, and filled with the unyielding dawn of a tomorrow he had bought with his life.

"Let's go," Yui said softly, stepping back from the fountain. "We have a caravan to guide."

The three of them turned together, walking side by side down the busy avenue, their figures blending into the vibrant, laughing crowd of the free city.

Behind them, the clear water of the fountain rippled in the sunlight. Upon the white quartz plinth, the crimson rose rested beside the bread, bathed in the eternal light of the sky.
