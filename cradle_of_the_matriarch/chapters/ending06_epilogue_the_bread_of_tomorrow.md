# Ending 6: Epilogue — The Bread of Tomorrow

The first thing I smell every morning is the sourdough yeast rising in the big stone trough down in the cellar.

It is a sharp, clean smell, full of life, bubbling with the wild wheat flour the millers cart down from the northern terraces at five o'clock while the stars are still pale in the dawn. Next comes the hickory woodsmoke as Kokkoro stirs the ash in the hearth, followed by the rich, savory sizzle of salt pork hitting the hot iron skillet.

Whenever I open my bedroom shutters on the second floor of the tavern, the morning breeze sweeps in from the bay. It ruffles my hair, cools my forehead, and carries the sound of Landosol waking up.

Below my window, the stone avenues of the artisan quarter are already humming with life. Carters shout cheerful greetings to the water-haulers; blacksmiths ring their anvils with steady, musical cadence; and children from the neighborhood run past with wicker hampers, their wooden clogs clattering against the cobblestones as they race to buy the first crusty rolls from our ovens.

Five years ago, this street was three feet deep in frozen slush and sulfur smog, patrolled by porcelain paladins whose blank glass faces reflected only cold obedience. People walked with their heads down, their eyes dull, clutching their ration tokens with white knuckles while Kaiser's neural towers hummed in their skulls.

Today, the sky overhead is a vast, breathless bowl of brilliant cerulean blue. 

The sun climbs over the royal mountains in waves of liquid gold, painting the terracotta roofs of the city in warm shades of peach and apricot. There are no leaden clouds. There are no humming converters. There are only swallows swooping between the eaves and the sweet, clean wind that blows all the way from the southern sea.

My parents wanted me to take the throne.

When the machinery of the Tower of Sol dissolved into starlight and the King and Queen held me in the Great Throne Room, weeping into my hair until my golden armor was soaked with their tears, they offered me the crown. They called me Eustiana. They said the realm belonged to the daughter who had broken the false matriarch.

I kissed my mother's cheek, hugged my father until his ribs creaked, and gently placed the golden circlet back upon the altar.

A crown is a heavy, cold thing. It keeps you locked behind tall marble walls, surrounded by legal parchment and ceremonial daggers. But I am a girl who loves the smell of roasted onions, the sound of laughing friends, and the sight of hungry people cleaning their plates until the wood shines. 

I told them Landosol needed protectors, not another monarch sitting above the clouds. 

So my parents govern the halls of state with an open council of elder citizens and district delegates, while I run the kitchen down in the artisan quarter. 

My tavern is called **The Fourth Hearth**.

It is built from thick mountain oak and whitewashed river stone, with broad bay windows looking out over the municipal plum orchards. Above the stout double doors hangs our wooden sign, carved with four linked rings and our guild’s sacred motto: *None Who Hunger Shall Turn Away.*

"Lady Eustiana!" Kokkoro’s gentle voice floats up the stairwell, soft and clear as a silver chime. "The second batch of clover bread has finished baking, and the carters from the southern valley have arrived with the fresh river trout."

"Coming, Kokkoro!" I call back, tying my saffron linen apron securely around my waist.

I run down the wooden stairs two at a time, landing in the kitchen with a cheerful thud that sets the copper ladles rattling on their hooks.

The kitchen is a glorious, fragrant chaos.

Four massive brick ovens are roaring with beechwood embers, radiating a steady, comforting heat that warms the stone flags. Long pine worktables are covered with mounds of golden dough, bowls of chopped wild leeks, wheels of yellow goat's cheese, and tubs of sweet butter churned yesterday in the high pastures. 

Kokkoro stands at the prep counter, her slender frame enveloped in an apron twice her size. 

Her pink eyes shine with calm, patient serenity as she inspects a basket of wild mountain herbs brought down from the cliffs. Her small fingers move with practiced grace, sorting sprigs of wild thyme, sweet marjoram, and dried mint into neat bundles. When she looks up and sees me, her face breaks into that quiet, devoted smile that has anchored my heart across every dark hour we ever endured.

"Good morning, Lady Eustiana," Kokkoro says, bowing with formal grace before handing me a wooden cup of hot chicory tea. "You slept half an hour past the bells. I took the liberty of kneading the second trough."

"You're an angel, Kokkoro!" I beam, taking a huge, scalding gulp of the tea and letting out a happy sigh. "What would I do without my wonderful guide? I'd probably bake the rolling pins by mistake!"

"That did occur during our second autumn in the old guildhall," Kokkoro reminds me with a tiny, fond tilt of her head. "Master Yuuki was forced to carve two new spatulas from the firelogs."

The mention of his name settles into the warm kitchen like a familiar, comforting presence. It brings no sharp ache anymore. Only a deep, tender warmth, like stepping into a room where someone has left a candle burning by the window.

A sharp clatter of stoneware echoes from the swinging pantry doors.

Karyl walks into the scullery carrying a heavy oak ledger under her arm and a bundle of dried salt cod in her hand. Her black cat ears are twitching with vigorous, theatrical annoyance, and her twin tails swish like twin whips behind her dark skirts.

"Pecorine!" Karyl shouts, slamming the ledger down on the flour table with enough force to kick up a white cloud of dust. "Explain to me why the ledger shows three extra sacks of milled barley delivered to the weavers' guild in Sector Four without an invoice!"

"Because their loom-master’s wife just had twins, Karyl!" I say, bounding over to wrap my flour-dusted arms around her shoulders in a huge, rib-squeezing hug. "Twins! Two tiny little girls with bright red cheeks! They need porridge! Lots and lots of porridge so they can grow big and strong!"

"Let go of me, you giant idiot!" Karyl squawks, her face turning as crimson as an Astraean tomato as she flails against my chest. "You're getting flour all over my good cardigan! And stop giving away our inventory! We run a tavern, not an imperial relief depot! If we keep giving away barley, we won't have enough coin to pay the cooper for the new cider barrels!"

"We have plenty of coin, Lady Karyl," Kokkoro interjects softly, setting down a ceramic bowl filled with silver and copper pieces. "The merchants from the Royal Citadel paid triple for the honeyed ham this morning. They said Captain Jun recommended our table above all others in the province."

Karyl grumbles under her breath, straightening her cuffs with sharp, jerky tugs, though the little twitch at the corner of her lips gives away her secret satisfaction. She sits down on her tall wooden stool by the accounts desk, dips her steel nib into black squid ink, and begins writing with brisk, precise strokes.

"Fine," Karyl mutters, glaring affectionately at the ledger. "I'll write it off under charitable municipal reserves. But if you give away the smoked venison to the street dogs again, Pecorine, I'm locking the larder doors and swallowing the key."

"You'd never lock the larder, Karyl," I laugh, leaning over her shoulder to boop her nose with a floury finger. "You'd get hungry at midnight and have to pick the lock with a hairpin like you did last Tuesday!"

"That was for guild business!" Karyl shrieks, swatting my hand away with a scarlet blush.

Kokkoro chuckles quietly behind her apron, and the three of us share a burst of warm, honest laughter that rings off the ceiling beams.

This is our life. This is the family he bought for us with his blood.

Around ten o'clock, the deliveries from the other guilds begin to arrive, turning our delivery yard into a colorful crossroads of the new Landosol.

First comes Caon.

A two-horse farm wagon rumbles up to the loading dock, laden with wicker crates of sweet dew-melons, bushels of purple forest berries, and jars of wild clover honey that smells of deep cedar glades. 

Sitting on the wagon seat with reins clutched in her scarred hands is Makoto. 

She wears a simple deerskin tunic, her greatsword slung across her back in a scabbard wrapped in blue mountain linen. Beside her sits a young wolf-kin boy, laughing as Makoto lets him hold the leather leads. When she sees me standing on the platform, Makoto grins, showing her sharp canine teeth, and vaults down from the wagon with effortless, athletic grace.

"Hey, Princess!" Makoto calls out, clapping me on the shoulder with a grip that would buckle an ordinary soldier's knees. "Brought the melon harvest from the southern groves. Maho cast a sweet-dew blessing on the vines three nights ago. Cut one open and your teeth will hurt from the sugar."

"Makoto!" I cheer, catching her in a hearty bear hug that lifts her boots clean off the gravel. "You're the best! Stay for lunch! I made roasted squash stew with sage dumplings!"

"You don't have to ask me twice," Makoto laughs, leaning against the timber rail. Her expression softens, her amber eyes turning toward the southern hills where the ancient trees rise green and unbroken against the horizon. "Maho opened the new herbal school in the Animal Quarter yesterday. Forty kids showed up. Wolf-kin, lynx-folk, even some human orphans from the lower ward. No harvesters. No collars. Just children learning how to talk to the forest spirits under the sun."

"How is Kaori's patrol?" I ask.

"Bored out of her mind," Makoto chuckles, shaking her wolf ears. "She spent two hours yesterday chasing a stray sheep out of the berry bushes. Kasumi set up a permanent trade outpost at the river ford to oversee the timber exchange. The people are happy, Pecorine. We breathe the air, we eat our bread, and nobody looks over their shoulder anymore."

Before Makoto can sample a fresh clover bun, the sound of rhythmic, polished footsteps echoes from the avenue.

A patrol of four Royal Guards marches past the tavern, their breastplates of mountain steel gleaming like mirrors in the morning sun. 

Leading them is Captain Jun.

She carries her heavy halberd across her shoulder, but her terrifying, faceless black visor is gone. She walks with her face bare to the light, her dark hair woven into a practical braid, her grey eyes calm and approachable. When she reaches our gate, she raises her gauntleted hand, bringing the patrol to a halt.

"Good morning, Eustiana," Jun says, her voice steady and warm. "The eastern trade road is clear. The caravans from the border provinces crossed the passes without incident."

"Captain Jun!" I wave my wooden spoon at her. "Take a break! Come sit down and have a trencher of hot pork!"

"Duty calls on the watch, Princess," Jun smiles, though she gratefully accepts a wrapped linen parcel of warm bread that Kokkoro brings to the fence. "Christina is already waiting for me at the training grounds. She took on thirty new recruits this morning. She told them that if any of them could touch her doublet with a practice rapier before sundown, she'd buy them a flagon of cider."

"And did any of them touch her?" Makoto asks with a smirk.

"None," Jun replies with a dry chuckle. "Tomo and Matsuri had to carry five of them to the infirmary with bruised ribs. But Christina was laughing the entire time. She teaches them with patience now. There is no bloodlust in her blade anymore. She fences for the joy of the dance."

Jun inclines her head toward the upper terrace of our tavern, her gaze drifting toward the quiet bay window on the corner. 

"The cenotaph at the mountain pass stood clear this morning," Jun says softly. "The children left fresh mountain lavender on the stone. We remember him, Eustiana. Every guard who straps on an iron breastplate remembers whose sacrifice gave us our honor back."

"Thank you, Jun," I whisper, my hand pressing against my chest where my heart beats strong and steady.

As Jun’s patrol continues down the street, three more figures appear around the corner from the Grand Plaza.

It is Twinkle Wish.

Yui walks between Hiyori and Rei, carrying a flat wicker basket covered with an embroidered napkin. She wears her soft pink robes, her amber eyes bright and untroubled, her cheeks flushed with the healthy color of morning sun. 

Beside her, Hiyori carries a bundle of newly cut ash-wood poles for the community garden trellises, her broad fists swinging cheerfully at her sides. Her red tunic is neat, her solar gauntlets polished until they shine like molten brass. Rei walks on the left, her dark hair tied back with a blue silk ribbon, her silver rapier resting peacefully in its scabbard at her hip.

"Pecorine!" Yui calls out, her voice bright and full of melody.

She hurries up the tavern steps, handing me the basket. When I lift the napkin, the aroma of sweet clover and baked honey fills the air—twelve round loaves of clover bread, baked in the restored communal ovens of the Central Plaza.

"The flour from the southern farms came in yesterday," Yui says, her smile radiating that pure, boundless warmth that Kaiser tried so desperately to extinguish. "I baked these for your lunch crowd. A gift from the plaza."

"Yui! You're an absolute darling!" I cry, taking a bite of the crust right then and there. 

It is sweet, warm, crisp on the outside and pillowy soft within, tasting of sunshine and wild mountain blossoms. 

"Yummy!" I cheer, closing my eyes in pure bliss. "The best batch yet! How is the central clinic?"

"Full of people who just want tea and conversation," Yui laughs softly. "Rei escorted the merchant wagons through the high pass yesterday without drawing her sword once. And Hiyori spent the morning helping the stone masons lift the archstones for the new schoolhouse."

"Those masons are weaklings!" Hiyori grins, flexing her arms proudly. "I had to carry three lintels myself! But look at the plaza, Pecorine! The crimson roses are blooming along the entire avenue! Thousands of them! The people walk through the flowers every evening, holding hands, listening to the street fiddlers. My fists feel light, Pecorine. I don't have to break monsters anymore. I get to build things with my hands."

"We are heading to the library this afternoon," Rei adds, her sharp features relaxing into a gentle, dignified smile. "Akira and Labyrista’s scholars have finished translating the first three volumes of the ancient architectural codices from the subterranean archives. Shizuru and Rino are hosting a study circle in the park by the river. Shizuru made berry tarts, and Rino has been coaching the neighborhood boys in target archery."

"Tell Shizuru I'll trade her three meat pies for two berry tarts!" I shout after them as they turn back toward the avenue with cheerful waves.

By midday, the tavern common room is packed to the rafters.

Eighty people crowd the long trestle tables. Carters, blacksmiths, scholars, beastfolk hunters, and palace pages sit elbow-to-elbow, sharing trenchers of steaming wild boar stew, clinking pewter flagons of dark apple cider, and tearing into thick slices of Yui’s clover bread. 

Laughter rolls through the room like thunder. Children chase each other between the benches; the tavern dog naps happily beside the hearth; and Karyl barks orders from behind the bar with a fierce, protective joy that makes every patron feel like family.

I spend five hours at the great hearth, ladling broth, carving golden roast pork, and heaping platters with buttered turnips and roasted potatoes until my arms ache with wonderful, satisfying fatigue. 

This is the feast. 

Not an imperial banquet served on cold gold platters to whispering courtiers, but an open table where every hungry soul finds warmth, bread, and dignity.

When the sun dips below the western mountains, the crowd begins to thin.

The church bells of Landosol chime the sixth hour.

Six deep, resonant notes echo across the roofs, vibrating through the timber walls of the tavern.

The chatter in the room softens into quiet, unprompted reverence. The last patrons finish their cider, leave their coins on the counter, and step out into the twilight with gentle words of gratitude.

Kokkoro wipes down the long service bar. Karyl closes her heavy ledger and blows out the accounting candle.

I wash my hands in the copper basin, take off my flour-dusted apron, and pick up a wooden tray from the kitchen counter.

Upon the tray, I place a clean earthenware bowl of rich wild boar stew, fragrant with rosemary and sweet carrots. Beside it, I lay two thick slices of freshly baked clover bread spread with golden butter, a carved juniper spoon, and a steaming cup of sweet mountain mint tea steeped from leaves harvested on the cliffs of Mount Sol.

Kokkoro and Karyl walk beside me as I carry the tray across the empty common room.

We walk to the large bay window at the front of the tavern.

There, in the bright alcove overlooking the plum orchard where the twilight turns the sky to deep violet and rose, sits a round table of solid mountain oak.

Three rush-bottomed chairs surround the table. 

And at the head of the circle sits a heavy chair carved from dark walnut.

Draped over the back of that walnut chair is a dark wool cloak, its hem faded by sun and rain, its wool scorched by the fires of forgotten spells. Resting against the sturdy right leg of the chair stands a notched steel broadsword, its leather grip dark with the sweat of hands that saved the world, its blade polished until it shines like mirror glass in the fading light.

I set the wooden tray down before the walnut chair.

I place the bowl of stew in the center of the setting, lay the warm bread beside it, set the spoon in its place, and place the cup of steaming mint tea to the right.

Kokkoro sits in the chair to the left. 

Karyl sits across the table. 

I take the chair to the right.

Before each of us, a bowl of stew and a cup of tea are already waiting.

The four place settings are complete.

I reach out across the oak boards. I take Kokkoro’s small, warm hand in my right, and reach across the corner to take Karyl’s hand in my left. 

For a long time, the three of us sit in silence, listening to the quiet ticking of the hearth clock and the soft whisper of the evening wind through the ventilation grates. The aroma of roasted meat, wild clover, and sweet mint rises between us, filling the alcove with the scent of the home he gave us.

I look at the empty walnut chair. I look at the dark cloak, and at the notch in the steel blade.

My throat tightens, but my heart is full to bursting with an immortal, undying joy.

"Good evening, Yuuki," I whisper.

My voice is soft, carried only by the warm air of the room.

"We ate well today," I tell him, smiling through the quiet tears that trace down my cheeks. "We had eighty people in the tavern. We roasted an entire boar, and Yui brought clover bread from the plaza. Makoto brought melons from the southern trees, and Jun’s guards kept the mountain roads safe. The city is alive, Yuuki. Everyone is laughing. Everyone has a warm bed and a full stomach."

"The accounts are balanced," Karyl says softly, her fingers tightening around mine, her green eyes shining in the twilight. "No one is hungry, Yuuki. Not a single child in this kingdom had to go to sleep crying for bread. We're keeping the doors open. Just like you told us to."

Kokkoro bows her head, her voice trembling with tender, eternal devotion. "Your bed linens are fresh, my Lord. The flowers on your table are fresh mountain clover. Every morning when the sun rises, we speak your name. Every evening when the bells chime, we set your trencher. You are our knight, my Lord. You will always be our knight."

I squeeze their hands one last time, and then we release our grip.

We pick up our wooden spoons. 

We eat our dinner together, laughing softly over Karyl’s grumbling and Kokkoro’s gentle jokes, sharing the pot down to the very last bean.

When the bowls are empty, I stand up.

I walk out through the side door onto the tavern balcony, leaning my forearms against the heavy cedar railing.

The night has fallen over Landosol.

Below me, thousands of amber lanterns flicker along the streets and canals, mirroring the laughter drifting up from the plazas. Music from fiddles and flutes echoes off the ancient stone walls, sweet and joyful, celebrating the peace of a free people.

I lift my face to the heavens.

The sky is a deep, velvety indigo, dusted with millions of sparkling stars.

And there, perched at the very crown of the heavens, shining brighter than any constellation in the sky, is the **Silent Star**.

Its light is cold, silver, and brilliant. It pulses with a slow, gentle, rhythmic cadence—a heartbeat suspended in the void, watching over the world he loved with eternal, unyielding vigil.

I look up at that beautiful, solitary star, feeling the cool mountain breeze kiss my cheeks and stir the ribbon in my hair.

I raise my right hand toward the heavens, curling my fingers into a gentle wave, and I smile with all the love my heart can hold.

"We see you, Yuuki," I whisper into the night. "We're holding the table. We're eating our bread. And tomorrow... we'll wake up and smile again."

High above the earth, the Silent Star pulses once, bright and clear against the dark.

The hearth is warm. The city is sleeping. 

And the dawn is already on its way.
