# Ending 5: The Empty Table

The bells of the Royal Cathedral had been ringing since sunrise. 

From the high battlements of the palace to the crowded docks along the river basin, their brassy, rhythmic peals rolled across the terracotta roofs of Landosol. Banners of royal crimson and gold fluttered from every balcony, snapping in the brisk autumn breeze that carried the sweet scent of roasting barley and woodsmoke from the lower quarters.

A year had passed since the glass machinery of the Tower of Sol had been broken. 

The three-foot thick lead cables that had wrapped around the palace pillars had been cut away with cold chisels, melted down in the artisan foundries into water pipes and roof gutters for the rebuilding of Sector 4. The glass tanks of the cognitive abattoir had been removed from the Hall of Dynastic Memory, replaced by fresh coats of white lime plaster and garlands of dried mountain lavender. 

In the Great Throne Room, the King and Queen of Astraea sat upon their restored thrones, their eyes clear, their minds their own again. When the neural dampening field had collapsed with the shattering of Kaiser’s crown, the fog had lifted from their thoughts like morning mist before a southern gale. 

The Queen had spent three days in her chambers, weeping into the velvet folds of her gown, holding her daughter’s calloused, armor-scarred hands against her breast, kissing every faded burn and scraped knuckle as though she could wash away three years of forgotten love with her tears.

Yet Eustiana von Astraea did not wear the crown.

The golden circlet, mended by the royal goldsmiths with a thin seam of silver where Yuuki’s steel blade had split it, rested upon a purple velvet cushion on the high altar. Eustiana had stood before her parents, bowed with graceful reverence, and formally declined the daily scepter. She had named a council of regents—elder statesmen, master artisans from the districts, and Captain Jun of the Royal Guard—to govern the laws and manage the grain levies.

She had an older, more sacred promise to keep.

Down in the bustling heart of the artisan quarter, where the copper-smiths and barrel-makers had their shops, stood a two-story tavern of heavy timber and whitewashed river stone. 

Its front was dominated by a broad bay window with twenty small diamond panes that looked out over the municipal orchard, where ripe yellow plums hung heavy from the boughs. Above the stout oak door swung a painted sign, carved from mountain pine and varnished until it gleamed like amber:

**THE FOURTH HEARTH**
*None Who Hunger Shall Turn Away*

It was five in the afternoon, and the common room was already roaring with the warm, chaotic life of a free city.

Sixty wooden trestle tables filled the wide floor, packed shoulder-to-shoulder with carters, stone masons, weavers, and children from the orphanages. Long benches groaned under the weight of hungry patrons; pewter flagons of dark apple cider clattered against rough pine boards; and laughter rolled across the room like a summer wave.

Behind the long service bar, Kokkoro moved with steady, calm efficiency. 

Her green tunic was protected by a clean white linen apron, its front pocket holding a small leather notebook and a freshly trimmed goose quill. With one hand she ladled dark mountain honey into ceramic jugs; with the other she tallied the deliveries of salt beef and winter root vegetables brought in through the delivery arch. When a rowdy carter accidentally knocked over his horn cup, Kokkoro was already there with a damp rag, wiping the spilled ale with a serene, patient smile that made the burly teamster blush and apologize to his boots.

Beside her, perched on a high stool at the corner of the counter, Karyl was glaring at an open ledger.

She wore a dark wool skirt and a fitted cardigan of plum-colored yarn, her black cat ears twitching with irritation as she dipped her steel nib into black squid ink. A small brass abacus sat at her elbow, its wooden beads clicking with rapid-fire speed as her fingers flew across the frames.

"Hey! Carters' Guild!" Karyl barked across the counter, fixing a young teamster with a glare that crackled with faint, harmless sparks of violet static. "You billed us for twenty-four sacks of winter rye, but the scale in the back only weighed out twenty-two! If you think you can skim two bushels of flour off Eustiana's pantry to buy yourself extra ale down at the docks, I'll turn your whiskers into blue fireflies!"

The carter swallowed hard, pulling off his wool cap and stammering an apology. "A-clerical error, mistress! The boy left two sacks in the wagon by the horse trough! I'll bring 'em in right now, swear on the goddesses!"

"You'd better," Karyl muttered, making a sharp checkmark in her book as the man scrambled toward the door. 

Kokkoro set a cup of hot chamomile tea beside Karyl’s inkwell, her pink eyes crinkling with quiet amusement. "You are very fierce today, Lady Karyl. The merchants speak of you as the Iron Auditor of the Central Market."

"Someone has to keep this place from going bankrupt," Karyl grumbled, taking a sip of the tea and letting out a quiet sigh as the tension left her shoulders. "If I left the accounts to Pecorine, she'd give away the roof shingles to anyone who asked with a polite smile. We gave away two hundred loaves of sunflower bread this morning alone."

"And the granaries remain full," Kokkoro replied softly, gesturing toward the back door where two farm wagons loaded with fresh squash and leeks were pulling up to the unloading dock. "The farmers bring twice what we order. They say food cooked in this house carries a blessing that keeps their winter cellars from spoiling."

A loud, joyous clatter echoed from the swinging doors of the kitchen.

Pecorine kicked the doors open with her boot, carrying an enormous cast-iron roasting pan with both arms. 

She wore no armor today. Her golden hair was tied back in a cheerful, practical ponytail with a red ribbon, and a flour-dusted canvas apron was wrapped twice around her waist over a simple peasant dress of saffron linen. The pan held an entire roasted wild boar, its mahogany skin glazed with dark apple syrup, stuffed with wild chestnuts and rosemary, surrounded by heaps of crackling golden potatoes that sizzled in savory lard.

"Coming through! Hot pan! Extra crispy skin for anyone with an empty trencher!" Pecorine’s voice rang across the tavern with the joyous, earth-shaking enthusiasm that had once led armies.

The crowd erupted in cheers. Men banged their wooden trenchers against the tables, children stood on the benches clapping their hands, and the tavern dog—a scruffy terrier with one white ear—trotted eagerly behind Pecorine’s heels, sniffing the glorious steam.

Pecorine set the massive pan down upon the central carving table. 

With two flashing strokes of a heavy butcher’s cleaver, she carved thick, steaming slabs of juicy pork, heaping them onto wooden platters that were immediately whisked away by three young scullery girls from Sector 4. She wiped her forehead with the back of her floury wrist, her blue eyes shining with radiant, boundless happiness as she watched a grandfather help his little granddaughter spear a roasted potato with her spoon.

"Everyone eating well?" Pecorine called out, leaning against the carving block with her hands on her hips.

"Best pork in Astraea, Princess!" a mason shouted through a mouthful of meat.

"I'm not a princess in this room, Master Mason!" Pecorine laughed, pointing her carving fork at him. "In this room, I'm the cook! And if you don't eat those boiled turnips on your plate, there won't be any apple tart for you!"

The room roared with laughter, the mason sheepishly scooping up his vegetables as his companions poked his ribs.

Pecorine watched them for a moment, her chest rising and falling with deep, contented satisfaction. Then she turned her head toward the front of the tavern.

The grand clock in the corner began to chime the sixth hour.

Six deep, brassy notes echoed through the common room.

The laughter in the tavern did not die, but the chatter softened slightly, out of natural, unprompted respect. The regular patrons lowered their voices; the carters leaned back from their mugs; and the children sat quietly, watching the three girls move.

Pecorine picked up a wooden tray from the kitchen counter. 

Upon it, she placed a clean earthenware bowl filled with steaming wild boar stew, rich with dark broth, tender meat, and sweet winter carrots. Beside the stew, she laid two thick slices of freshly baked clover bread, golden-brown and steaming, spread with sweet cream butter. 

Kokkoro stepped out from behind the bar, carrying a steaming cup of mint and mountain pine tea, steeped from leaves harvested on the cliffs of Mount Sol.

Karyl closed her ledger with a soft snap, picked up an unlit wax candle in a brass holder, and walked out from behind the counter.

The three of them walked together toward the large bay window at the front of the room.

There, in the alcove where the diamond panes caught the warm amber light of the setting sun, sat a round table of solid mountain oak. 

No customer ever sat at that table. No mugs were slammed down upon its polished wood, and no coats were tossed across its chairs. It stood in the quietest, brightest corner of the tavern, surrounded by three rush-bottomed chairs and a fourth chair carved from heavy walnut that sat at the head of the circle.

Over the back of that walnut chair hung a dark wool cloak, faded by rain, its hem scorched by the fire of forgotten spells. Resting against the sturdy right leg of the chair stood a notched steel broadsword, its leather grip worn dark by calloused hands, its blade polished until it shone like mirror glass in the twilight.

Pecorine reached the table first.

She set down the wooden tray before the walnut chair. She placed the bowl of stew at the center of the place setting, laid the slices of warm clover bread beside it, and set down a carved juniper spoon.

Kokkoro stepped forward, setting the steaming cup of mint tea to the right of the bowl.

Karyl took a wooden match from her pocket, struck it against the sole of her shoe, and lit the wax candle, placing it in the small brass saucer beside the tea. The golden flame flickered once in the draft from the bay window, and then burned steady, bright and clear.

Pecorine sat down in the chair to the right of the walnut seat. 

Kokkoro sat to the left. 

Karyl sat across the circle.

Before them, three more bowls of stew and three cups of tea were already waiting.

The four place settings were complete.

Pecorine reached out with both her hands. She took Kokkoro’s small, warm hand in her right, and reached across the empty corner to take Karyl’s hand in her left. 

For a long moment, the three of them sat in silence, their eyes resting upon the empty walnut chair, upon the dark cloak draped over its back, and upon the notch in the steel blade resting against the wood.

The stew in the fourth bowl steamed gently in the cool evening air. The aroma of roasted meat, sweet clover, and wild mint rose between them, filling the small alcove with the scent of the home they had built with his blood.

Pecorine took a deep, shuddering breath. Her throat worked dryly, but when she lifted her face, there were no tears in her eyes. Only a fierce, beautiful, undying warmth that lit up her features like the morning sun over Astraea.

"Good evening, Yuuki," Pecorine whispered.

Her voice was soft, carried only by the quiet hum of the tavern.

"The pork turned out wonderfully tonight," she said, looking at the empty seat with a gentle, loving smile. "Just like you like it. I used the sweet apple glaze from the orchard trees by the south gate. We had two carters from Sector 4 who ate three full bowls each. You would have smiled watching them clean their plates."

"The accounts are balanced," Karyl said softly, her fingers tightening around Pecorine’s hand. Her green eyes were fixed upon the notched sword. "We have enough flour and salted beef in the cellar to feed the entire lower district through the winter. No one is going to starve, Yuuki. Not a single person in this city will ever have to wonder where their next crust of bread is coming from."

Kokkoro bowed her head, her voice trembling slightly with quiet devotion. "I trimmed the lantern wicks in your room this morning, my Lord. The bed linens are fresh, washed with sweet lavender water. Whenever the sun goes down... we look toward the window, and we feel your hand on our shoulders. You are with us, my Lord. At every sunrise, and at every meal."

A gentle breeze blew through the small ventilation slat above the bay window.

The flame of the candle danced, casting a warm golden glow across the dark wool of the cloak. The shadow of the notched sword stretched across the oak floor, steady, protective, and unbroken.

Pecorine squeezed their hands one last time, and then released them.

She picked up her wooden spoon, scooped up a mouthful of the rich stew, and took a bite. 

It was savory, hot, seasoned with the wild salt of the earth, and tasted of life. 

Beside her, Kokkoro lifted her spoon, and Karyl took a warm crust of the clover bread, dipping it into her broth.

They ate together. 

Around them, the tavern bustled with the noise of children laughing, carters arguing over horse prices, and flagons of cider clinking together in joyful toasts to the harvest. Outside the window, the sun dipped below the mountains, painting the sky in deep streaks of violet, amber, and rose.

The fourth chair remained empty. 

The bowl of stew before it grew cool in the evening draft, but the bread lay whole, the tea remained fragrant, and the candle burned bright beside the steel blade that had saved the world.

The Gourmet Guild was home. 

And as the stars came out over the roofs of Landosol, the table remained set, waiting forever for the knight who had taught them how to live.
