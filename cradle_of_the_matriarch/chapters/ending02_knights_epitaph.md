# Ending 2: The Knight's Epitaph

The ignition of thirty thousand pounds of military black powder did not sound like thunder. It was an absolute, blinding pressure that erased sound entirely, replacing the roar of battle with a searing, white-hot stillness.

For a single, suspended fraction of a second, the stone walls of the Citadel Bastion became translucent.

I watched the shockwave expand through the granite foundation. The load-bearing pillars, four feet thick and anchored into the mountain bedrock, shattered into dust under the thermodynamic fury of my Princess Knight core. The azure crystal that had consumed my left arm, my chest, and my throat did not shatter; it vaporized, releasing every scrap of soul data, every memory of three hundred forgotten cycles, and every joule of kinetic energy into the expanding fireball.

The pain was instantaneous and absolute, and then it was gone.

My physical avatar dissolved into pure, incandescent light.

Through the eyes of the fading singularity, I watched the destruction from above.

The Grand Causeway, an engineering marvel that had spanned the four-hundred-foot chasm of the river gorge for three centuries, bowed upward like a drawn bow. Then the central piers gave way. Fifty thousand tons of squared granite blocks sheared along their mortar lines, tumbling into the abyss in a colossal avalanche of stone and dust.

With the bridge went the imperial vanguard.

Over eight hundred Porcelain Paladins, sixty heavy centurions, and the thirty shock centaurs of the Emperor’s personal guard were caught on the collapsing span. Their algorithmic gyros spun uselessly in mid-air as the roadway dissolved beneath their iron hooves. They plummeted into the churning, mist-choked rapids four hundred feet below, crushed by the falling masonry, buried beneath millions of pounds of pulverized granite and river silt.

The mountain pass was sealed. 

A permanent, impassable scar of fractured cliff-face and jagged rockfall blocked the southern approaches to the mountains. No army, no mechanized siege walker, and no porcelain cavalcade from Landosol could ever cross that chasm again.

Across the canyon, four miles up the winding karst trail, the shockwave arrived as a rolling earthquake that rattled the gravel beneath the wagon wheels and sent flocks of wild cranes bursting from the pine canopy.

The ten thousand refugees stopped on the mountain road, turning in stunned silence to look back at the gorge.

A towering pillar of black smoke and white fire rose into the leaden sky where the Citadel had stood, piercing the cloud deck like an unyielding spear. And beneath that smoke, the mountain pass was closed forever.

Captain Jun stood at the edge of the overlook, her dented visor turned toward the pillar of fire. 

She did not speak. She slowly raised her right gauntlet to the brow of her helm, locking her posture in an unbroken, motionless salute while the wind whipped the soot from her breastplate. Beside her, Christina leaned against the pommel of her rapier, her golden hair blowing across her eyes, her shoulders shaking with silent, ragged sobs that she did not try to wipe away.

The Princess Knight was gone. The road to tomorrow was open.

***

That first night in the mountains was a crucible of frost and grief.

The rain turned to driving sleet as the refugee column climbed above the tree line, winding through the razor-sharp limestone gorges where the wind blew like knives. There was no shelter, no dry timber, and only the clothes on their backs. Women carried babies wrapped in torn wool shawls; old weavers from the industrial wards leaned heavily on the shoulders of bruised city watchmen. 

Jun and Christina did not sleep. 

Through the long, freezing hours, Jun walked the length of the five-mile line on foot, her heavy armor clanking in the dark, hauling stuck wagon wheels from the mud with her bare gauntlets, lifting exhausted children onto the supply caissons, and speaking in her calm, metallic voice to every trembling family she passed. Christina rode the outer ridges with Tomo and Matsuri, scouting the ravine flanks in the sleet, ensuring that no stray imperial drone had slipped past the collapse.

When dawn broke over the high peaks, the clouds parted for the first time in three hundred cycles.

A pale, golden sliver of honest sunlight touched the snow on the ridges. The wild, cold, clean sun of an open world warmed their cheeks. Ten thousand people stopped on the ridge, looking up at the blue sky above them with tears streaming down their soot-stained faces, breathing air that had never passed through a ventilation conduit or tasted of burnt sulfur.

They descended into the Great Karst Basin—a vast, hidden amphitheater of fertile mountain soil, ancient pine forests, and crystal-clear glacier lakes sheltered from the northern gales by an unbroken wall of granite peaks.

There, they built the Free Cantonment of the Bastion.

The first winter was brutal. The snow fell six feet deep, burying the cedar-log cabins to their window sills. Food was scarce; every grain of barley and every strip of salt-mutton was rationed by weight. But in those long winter evenings around the central communal hearth, nobody wore a silver wire in their neck. Nobody woke up screaming from a wiped memory. 

The watchmen who had mutinied with Nightmare laid aside their imperial insignia. They melted their gilded crests in the blacksmith’s forge, beating the metal into ploughshares, axe-heads, and simple iron brooches. They formed the Order of the Bastion—not a retinue of palace enforcers, but a brotherhood of protector-knights sworn by solemn oath to serve the people of the cantonment, guarding the mountain trails and harvesting timber beside the common laborers.

And at the edge of the severed gorge, where the bridge had fallen, they built the memorial.

***

Two years passed in the high valleys of the Karst Range.

Beyond the shattered gorge, the world of Landosol remained trapped in Kaiser’s leaden cradle, its cities growing colder and more silent with each passing month. But in the southern valleys where the refugees had settled, the sun broke through the clouds every morning.

The Cantonment had grown into a thriving commonwealth of three thousand homesteads. Terraced barley fields rippled green and gold across the lower slopes; stone irrigation channels carried sweet water to apple orchards; and the sound of iron hammers rang from thirty smithies along the river.

At the very edge of the severed mountain pass, where the southern abutment of the Grand Causeway terminated abruptly over the four-hundred-foot abyss, stood the Memorial Redoubt.

The stones of the old gatehouse had been cleared, but the jagged stump of the bridge pier remained untouched, pointing like an accusing finger across the vast canyon toward the distant, leaden haze of Landosol. 

In the center of the overlook, carved directly from a massive slab of white granite recovered from the riverbed, rose an unadorned stone cenotaph.

There were no long inscriptions of royal titles or victories. Upon the polished face of the stone, carved in deep, crisp Roman letters, were only eight words:

*HE STOOD ALONE. WE WALK IN THE SUN.*

At the foot of the cenotaph lay a pair of heavy iron-spurred cavalry boots, polished to a dull gleam, and an unnotched steel shortsword resting across an empty scabbard. Every morning, fresh sprigs of wild mountain lavender and blue gentian were placed between the boots by the children of the cantonment on their way to the valley orchards.

The morning sun had just cleared the eastern peaks, painting the limestone cliffs in brilliant shades of rose and pale gold, when the sound of heavy armor echoed up the winding stone stairs of the redoubt.

Captain Jun walked onto the overlook.

Her armor was newly forged mountain steel—plain, functional, and polished bright, bearing no insignia save for the black raven on her left pauldron. She carried her heavy halberd across her shoulder, its steel blade catching the morning light with diamond clarity. Her visored helm was tucked beneath her left arm, revealing her face to the mountain wind for the first time in twenty years.

Her dark hair was pulled back into a practical braid, shot through with strands of silver at the temples. Her skin was tanned by two summers of mountain sun, her grey eyes calm, steady, and clear.

Beside her, walking with loose, lazy grace, came Christina.

Christina wore a riding doublet of crimson leather and high deerskin boots, her rapier resting easy at her hip. She had grown her golden hair long again, tied back with a black silk ribbon that fluttered in the gorge breeze. In her hand, she carried an earthen jug of dark, spiced mountain ale and two wooden cups.

"You're late, Christina," Jun said quietly, her eyes fixed on the distant chasm.

"Tomo wanted me to inspect the new archery butts at the third terrace," Christina replied, stepping up to the parapet beside Jun. She set the jug down on the granite coping, pouring the dark foam into the two cups with steady fingers. "The recruits are getting cocky. Two of the farm boys thought they could parry a Seven Crown’s thrust. I had to knock them into the horse trough to remind them what steel tastes like."

She handed one cup to Jun, picking up the second.

Jun took the cup, but she didn't drink. She stepped forward to the base of the cenotaph, bent down, and poured half the ale onto the stone flags before the empty boots, watching the dark liquid soak into the white granite.

"To the Princess Knight," Jun said, her voice deep, solemn, and resonant with eternal gratitude.

"To the idiot who held the door," Christina added, raising her cup toward the empty sky before draining it in a single long draught. She wiped her lip with the back of her wrist, looking out across the immense void of the gorge where the river roared far below.

"Two years, Jun," Christina murmured softly, the usual mockery gone from her voice, leaving only an ache that had mellowed into quiet reverence. "Two whole years without a reset. The calendar actually turned. The winter came, the snow melted, and nobody woke up with their memories scrambled."

"He bought that calendar," Jun said, her gaze traveling toward the far side of the chasm where the smoking ruins of Landosol sat beneath its perpetual pall of lead. "Every sunrise we see from these peaks was paid for in his blood."

"I know," Christina said, her hand resting gently on the hilt of her rapier. "Sometimes I stand on this parapet at night, listening to the wind through the canyon, and I think I can hear the black powder going off again. That terrible, blinding light."

"It wasn't a terrible light, Christina," Jun replied, looking at the fresh blue gentian flowers resting between the boots. "It was the only honest light this world ever had. It burned away the lies."

Behind them on the stone steps, a pair of light, running footsteps broke the silence.

Matsuri ran onto the platform, her scout cloak flying, her blonde pigtails bouncing as she carried a wooden basket packed with fresh hot rolls wrapped in clean linen.

"Captain! Christina!" Matsuri called out, breathless and grinning. "The bakery in the lower ward just pulled the first batch of rye bread from the ovens! Master Kael said the first basket belongs to the redoubt!"

Jun turned, a rare, warm smile softening the severe lines of her mouth. She took a hot roll from the basket, breaking the crust with her fingers, the clean scent of yeast and caraway seeds rising into the mountain air.

"Thank you, Matsuri," Jun said. "Tell Master Kael the bread is good."

She took one half of the warm roll, walked back to the cenotaph, and laid the bread gently upon the white stone beside the lavender sprigs.

Tomo walked up the last flight of stone steps, her scout tunic unbuttoned at the collar, carrying a leather map case under her arm. She wiped the road dust from her forehead, her young face bright with pride.

"Captain, the southern patrol returned from the river ford," Tomo reported, snapping a crisp salute. "The herdsmen crossed thirty head of mountain cattle into the lower pastures. No sign of imperial reconnaissance within fifty leagues. The boundary is completely clean."

"Good work, Tomo," Jun said, nodding with quiet satisfaction. "Dismiss the patrol and see that the men get hot porridge."

The morning wind blew up from the gorge, carrying the sound of church bells from the valley village, the laughter of children playing in the orchard irrigation ditches, and the distant, rhythmic clink of blacksmiths hammering scythe-blades for the harvest. The scent of roasted malt and cedar smoke drifted across the battlements, mingling with the resinous perfume of pine needles warmed by the morning sun.

Jun rested her right hand on the cool granite of the memorial, her fingers tracing the carved letters of his epitaph. She could still recall the steady, resolute grip of his hand when the powder magazines were lit—the calm, unyielding pulse of a comrade who had gladly shouldered the dark so others could step into the light.

"The watch is secure, Yuuki," Jun whispered into the clear morning air. "We're holding the pass."

She turned, pulled her halberd onto her shoulder, and walked down the mountain stairs toward the bustling streets of the free city, her boots ringing steady and true upon the stones as the morning sun flooded the valley with light. Behind her, the white cenotaph gleamed bright against the green alpine slopes, watching over the freedom he had carved from stone.
