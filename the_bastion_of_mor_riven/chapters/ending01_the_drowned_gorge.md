# Ending 1: The Drowned Gorge

Logan did not hesitate. He swung his iron hammer against the copper wedges holding the inspection hatch, sealing the lower drift with three blows that echoed through his shoulder joints.

"Bring the three kegs from the gallery wall," Logan told Tobias, his voice cutting through the roar of the water. "We blow the north pier."

Tobias hauled the pitch-sealed barrels through the swirling current, his teeth chattering from the mountain runoff. They spiked the kegs directly into the expansion cleft at the base of the central masonry weir, where eighty years of hydraulic pressure had already worked the mortar loose. Logan jammed a three-minute sulfur cord into the touch-hole, struck his steel against the flint, and blew the spark into the tinder until red embers caught the dry braid.

"Up the stair!" Logan barked, shoving the lad toward the spiral treads. "Move!"

They scrambled up the slick granite steps on their hands and knees, boots slipping on wet moss as cold spray pelted their backs. They cleared the threshold of the mid-level battery just as the detonation struck.

The world dropped out from beneath their boots.

A dull, concussive shudder ripped through the foundation of Mor-Riven, throwing Logan hard against the limestone parapet. The scent of burnt sulfur and pulverized river stone blasted upward through the ventilation shafts in a hot, choking cloud.

Outside in the gray rain, the north weir gave way.

Ten thousand tons of swollen mountain flood, held back behind the stone barrier, slammed into the fracture. The granite piers cracked like pine firewood, tilting sideways into the boiling brown flume. An immense wall of foam and river gravel fifty feet high tore through the narrow Calder gorge with the acoustic fury of an iron avalanche.

Logan gripped the coping stone, blinking mud and rain from his eyes as he looked down into the gorge.

Baron Kestrel’s siege lines were annihilated in seconds. The timber mantlets, the heavy stone-throwers, the supply wagons, and two hundred yards of entrenched breastworks were swallowed whole by the roaring torrent. Men and horses were swept downriver like twigs, tossed into the boiling rapids toward the lower falls. The siege was broken before the enemy trumpets could sound an orderly retreat.

The cost lay raw and exposed beneath the clearing mist.

The lower bailey of Mor-Riven was gone. The water-wheels that powered the flour mills and trip-hammers had been ripped from their housings, reduced to splintered timber drifting on the brown froth. The storehouses of salt pork and winter grain lay submerged under twelve feet of roiling silt.

Mor-Riven still stood atop its jagged crag, an unassailable bastion of granite and iron. The garrison had survived, and the river belonged to them once more, but winter was coming on fast, and the valley below lay scrubbed clean down to the bedrock.

Logan turned his back on the gorge, wiped the soot from his jaw with the back of his sleeve, and walked down the dripping corridor toward the mess hall to count what bread remained.
