# Ending 3: The Mercenary's Charter

"Lower the basket," Logan said to Keller.

The sergeant blinked, his hand pausing on the oak winch handle. "Master Finch? You're treating with these cutthroats?"

"I am treating with their purse," Logan said, his voice flat as slate. "Aldous is dead. We have eighty mouths, sixty sacks of rotted grain, and four days of arrow-cord. Kestrel wants the river, not our skulls. Lower the basket."

The iron basket groaned down the parapet wall on its hemp hawser. The herald’s clerk stepped forward into the drizzle, placed the brass cylinder inside, and took two steps back with his fingers spread wide to show bare palms.

Logan hauled the cylinder up hand over hand. The brass was cold, slick with mountain drizzle. He popped the wax seal with his thumb and spread the calfskin vellum across the coping stone, weighting the corners with iron quarrel heads.

He read every clause with the cold eye of a builder reading a timber contract. Three clauses demanded excision: the unconditional surrender of the armor-stores, the quartering of mercenary lances within the private quarters of the masons, and the forfeiture of the river-gate master keys.

Logan took the ink-horn from his belt, chewed the tip of his quill until the split held, and struck through the offending lines with heavy strokes of black oak gall. In the margin, in the square, unhurried hand of a master engineer, he wrote his counter-covenant:

*The Garrison of Mor-Riven incorporates as the Chartered Guild of Calder Water-Works. Full custody of sluices, weirs, and foundation keys remains with the Master Sapper in perpetuity. Toll revenues shall be divided: three parts to the Iron League, two parts to the Guild maintenance chest. Violation of this article voids all obligations and authorizes the immediate firing of the foundation scuttles.*

He signed his mark, sealed it in hot pine resin stamped with the fortress surveyor's square, and lowered the basket back into the mist.

Down on the bridge, the herald unrolled the counter-articles. He read them to the clerk, conferring in muttered whispers while the rain beat upon their helmets. Ten minutes crawled past, measured only by the thrum of the river beneath the span.

At last, the herald produced an ivory signet ring and pressed Baron Kestrel’s green seal into the warm resin beside Logan’s mark.

Two hours later, the outer portcullis rose to the clink of oiled chains. There was no sack, no drunken rampage through the bailey, and no gallows erected in the square. A company of Kestrel’s heavy mercenary lances marched across the timbers in disciplined ranks, their green banners snapping in the river wind, followed by four pack-mules laden with barrels of salt pork, dried peas, and three iron-banded chests of silver thalers.

Logan stood in the archway of the water-gate, watching the mercenary captains inspect the masonry with wary, respectful eyes. They held the road, but Logan held the keys to the water below their boots, and every barge of iron ore that floated down the Calder would pay its toll into his guild ledger.

He walked over to the paymaster's mule, accepted the heavy canvas sack of silver thalers, and began dividing the garrison's back pay upon the nearest oak trestle.
