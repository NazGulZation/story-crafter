# Chapter 2A: The Drowned Sconces

The stairs to the lower vaults smelled of wet river rot, rotting tallow, and cold copper. Water ran down the dressed granite in greasy ribbons, pooling around Logan’s boots as he descended into the subterranean belly of Mor-Riven.

Tobias hauled the sledgehammer in one fist and an iron lantern in the other. The lantern flame sputtered, yellow and smoky against the damp stone. Below them, the Calder River sounded like a furnace draft, a deep concussive roar echoing through sixty feet of bedrock.

"Mind the third tread," Logan muttered, catching the lad’s shoulder as Tobias slipped on a mat of black river moss. "Break an ankle down here and you’ll drown sitting down."

They reached the lower gallery—a long, vaulted corridor of rough-hewn basalt that ran beneath the river-gate. The floor was already covered in six inches of brown water that tasted of iron filings and mountain clay when spray struck Logan’s lips. At the far end stood the sluice mechanism: an iron-strapped oak gate six inches thick, locked into the living stone with bronze pins the size of a man’s forearm.

Logan set his boots against the floor flags, grabbed the toggle bar, and knocked out the first safety collar. The iron clanged against the stone, ringing in the narrow vault like an anvil strike.

"Heave on the second pin," Logan ordered. "On three. One. Two. Three!"

Tobias struck the bronze wedge with the sledge. The pin yielded with a screech that set Logan’s teeth on edge. A jet of icy water shot through the seam, drenching Logan’s leather coat and stinging his face like needles.

With a grinding shriek of strained metal, the oak gate jumped upward in its tracks.

The Calder took the opening. A wall of brown water burst into the mill race, surging into the old gravel conduits beneath the north water-tower. Through the stone beneath their soles came a sudden, frantic succession of muffled thuds—shouts of terror, the cracking of green pine timbers, and the heavy, wet slumping of saturated river gravel caving into hollow space. Kestrel's sapping drift had collapsed, smothered under twenty feet of surging river mud.

"We got them," Tobias gasped, his knuckles white around the sledge handle as the backwash swirled to his knees. "We drowned the rat-hole!"

Logan did not answer. He pressed his ear against the damp basalt wall of the drainage gallery, listening past the rush of the water.

A dull, rhythmic clinking persisted. It came from deeper down, beneath the river weir itself.

"Grab the lantern," Logan said, splashing forward through the current.

They waded thirty yards down the lateral passage to the old inspection bore, a narrow drift cut into the soft limestone beneath the riverbed. Logan held the lantern into the opening.

The water from the flooded mill race had scoured away the mud, revealing what the enemy miners had left behind. Kestrel’s engineers had anticipated a breach. They had driven heavy iron caissons through the river gravel and sealed a dry chamber directly beneath the central weir masonry. Through the gap in the limestone, Logan saw four rows of pitch-sealed barrels banded in bright copper.

*Blasting powder.* Over forty barrels, stacked directly against the foundation struts of the weir. A hemp fuse, soaked in pitch and encased in sewn leather tubes to keep out the river water, trailed away into the dark toward the enemy lines on the north bank.

"They aren't mining the water-tower," Logan said, his voice dropping into the chill air. "They baited us with the gravel drift. They’ve packed enough powder under the weir to blow the riverbed into the clouds. When that goes, the Calder drops sixty feet into the foundation, and Mor-Riven slides into the gorge like a pile of gravel."

Tobias stared at the sealed powder vault, his jaw working silently in the lantern light. "Can we pull the charges?"

"The drift is too narrow, and the casing is spiked into the stone," Logan said. He ran his calloused thumb along the sweating rock. "We have two minutes before their trencher captain notices the water backing up and touches a port-fire to the fuse."

Two courses of action lay in Logan's hands.

The first was the main flood-weir release. By setting three kegs of their own fine-grain powder against the river weir's masonry piers above, Logan could rupture the Calder barrier entirely. The mountain flood would tear through the gorge in an unstoppable fifty-foot wave, sweeping away Kestrel’s siege lines, their timber mantlets, and their trenches in one apocalyptic deluge. The cost was total: the lower bailey, the water mills, and the garrison's grain stores would be obliterated, leaving Mor-Riven an isolated, starving fortress perched on bare granite.

The second was a desperate close-quarters counter-blast. Logan still carried three copper-cased sulfur charges in his haversack. If he waded through the submerged lateral drift, he could wedge the sulfur canisters directly into the rock seam above the enemy powder chamber and detonate it prematurely. The resulting cave-in would collapse the limestone roof into the enemy powder vault, snuffing the fuse and entombing the enemy sappers, but the blast would collapse the lower galleries and permanently trap Logan's team beneath the stone.

Logan took the iron tinder-box from his coat and looked at Tobias.
