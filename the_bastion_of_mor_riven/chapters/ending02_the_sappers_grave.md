# Ending 2: The Sapper's Grave

"Get up to the guardroom, Tobias," Logan said. His voice was steady, though the freezing water now lapped against his hip joints. "Take the hammer. Bolt the trapdoor from above and tell Keller to keep his crossbowmen trained on the river mouth."

"Master Sapper, you can't work the drift alone—"

"Go," Logan snapped, shoving the lantern into the boy's chest. "One man can crawl through that silt hole. Two will just get stuck and choke each other on powder smoke."

Tobias looked at him for one long, shivering second, then turned and splashed through the rising backwash toward the stair.

Logan pulled the three copper sulfur canisters from his haversack. He clamped the linen match between his teeth to keep it dry, dropped onto his hands and knees, and dragged himself into the narrow limestone bore.

The drift smelled of ancient mud, crushed worms, and the sharp, vinegar tang of mine damp. The freezing slurry rose to his collarbone, pressing against his ribs like a lead vise. Every yard he crawled demanded scraping his shoulders against jagged shale that shredded his wool jerkin and bit deep into his flesh. Ahead, a faint red glow flickered through the cracks in the rock—the enemy miners had already lit their port-fire.

Logan wedged his boots against an outcropping of hard basalt, shoved the three copper canisters into the natural fissure above their powder casks, and struck his striker against the rough stone. The linen match flared, spitting yellow sparks that reflected in the black water around his chin.

He gave the fuse twelve inches of cord—barely ninety seconds of burn.

He shoved himself backward through the mud with elbows and boots, scraping skin from his forearms as the slurry churned behind him. He reached the lateral gallery and threw himself behind the heavy oak retaining bulkhead just as the sulfur charges caught.

The shockwave hit like an iron drawbridge dropping onto his chest.

A blinding flash illuminated every fracture in the subterranean gallery, followed instantly by a deafening, concussive roar as eighty tons of saturated river limestone collapsed into the enemy chamber. The concussion blew Logan against the stone wall, driving the air from his lungs in a ragged gasp. Water, stone shards, and pulverized shale flooded the drift in a roaring avalanche, completely burying the enemy miners, their caissons, and their forty barrels of blasting powder under a solid plug of rock.

Then came the darkness.

Logan lay in the freezing mud, coughing up river grit and sulfur smoke. The air in the chamber had thinned to a sour, dust-choked whisper. Behind him, the way to the upper stair was choked with rubble—the concussion had brought down the vaulted ceiling of the lower gallery, sealing the drift off from the world above.

He sat with his back pressed against the living stone, his fingers checking his ribs. Sore and bruised, but unbroken. Through the sixty feet of solid rock above, he could feel the steady, rhythmic pulse of the Calder River continuing its course, and above that, the heavy, undisturbed weight of Mor-Riven standing firm upon its granite roots.

Sixteen hours later, the scrape of an iron pickaxe broke through the ceiling. Light from an oil lantern poured into the hollow space, illuminating Tobias and four soot-covered masons leaning through the breach.

Logan squinted against the glare, took the leather waterskin Tobias handed down to him, and drank the sour ale in three long, silent swallows.
