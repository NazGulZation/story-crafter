# Chapter 1: The Cracked Keystone

The Calder River had turned the color of steeped tobacco, thick with clay and dead mountain timber. It pounded against the lower sluice piers seventy feet below the bastion wall, shaking the mortar between the granite ashlar blocks until water seeped through the hair-cracks like grease.

Logan Finch wedged his iron pry-bar into the housing of the north winch and leaned his full weight against it. The oak beam groaned, spitting flakes of dry lichen against his cheek. Beneath his boots, the river-gate shuddered under the battering of a waterlogged spruce trunk caught in the flume.

"The bevel-gear is slipping, Master Sapper!" Tobias shouted over the roar of the water. The lad had mud caked into the stubble of his chin and grease smeared across his woolen jerkin from collar to hem. His hands trembled as he hauled on the guide rope. "If that spruce wedges beneath the oak apron, the gate won't seat. It'll rip the bronze guides straight out of the masonry!"

"Pass the iron wedge and get the mallet," Logan said. His voice was flat, dried out by cold wind and three days without hot broth. "And quit staring at the riverbank. What Kestrel's people are doing down in the mud won't matter if our own weir drowns us first."

Across the churning gorge, beyond the reach of the bastion's light ballistas, Baron Kestrel’s siege lines were settling into the rain-soaked alder bottoms. A line of timber mantlets covered their trenchers, who hacked into the river silt with picks and wicker baskets. Every four minutes, their stone-thrower loosed a three-hundred-pound block of gray river slate. One had struck the outer parapet an hour before sunrise, taking off twenty feet of battlements along with Commander Aldous and two crossbowmen. The commander's blood had already washed clean from the flags, carried down the gutters into the boiling river.

That left Logan with seventy garrison pikemen, fifteen stonemasons, four barrels of salt-cured herring, and forty kegs of fine-grain black blasting powder locked in the deep vaults behind the bedrock cistern.

A wet thump echoed through the stone beneath their soles.

Logan froze, his palms flat against the wet granite casing of the winch. It was not the battering of river drift against the weir, nor the distant thud of Kestrel's trebuchet. It came from deep within the gorge foundation—a hollow, rhythmic percussion.

*Tink. Tink. Shovel scrape.*

"They've breached the low water-gravel," Tobias whispered, turning pale. "They aren't trying to bridge the flume. They’re driving a drift under the north water-tower."

Logan spat into the sluice chute. The saliva vanished instantly into the churning foam. "They brought miners from the Calder iron pits. Hard-rock men who know how to drive timber galleries through saturated shale."

"If they plant a mine beneath the keystone of the water-gate, the whole river-wall drops into the gorge," Tobias said. "We lose the weir, the cistern, and the lower gatehouse."

Logan unhooked the brass-bound leather case from his belt and pulled out the fortress elevation chart. The vellum was limp with dampness, but the ink held true. His predecessor had laid out the lower works eighty years ago with the paranoid cunning of an engineer who expected betrayal from both land and water.

Two choices lay open before him, both carved in stone, both demanding immediate, irreversible action.

The first was the low flood-gate. By pulling the copper stop-pins in the lower gallery, Logan could vent the swollen Calder torrent straight through the disused mill race. The water would submerge the lower vaults, ruining fifty sacks of barley and the garrison's forge coals, but it would roar into the porous gravel strata below the north tower, drowning Kestrel’s sapping gallery and suffocating every miner in their trench before they could tamp a single charge.

The second lay above. On the outer water-gate parapet, a horn sounded three long, measured notes. Through the mist, a horseman in a silver-edged surcoat appeared upon the riverbank road, waving a green-and-gold pennant—the embassy of Baron Kestrel. The mercenary baron had not yet committed his heavy foot to a breach. He wanted the Calder gorge intact for his timber rafts and river tolls. The herald came to offer terms of capitulation, backed by the legal seal of the regional trade league. Parleying with the herald would buy time to assess Kestrel's numbers and exploit the mercenary commander's commercial greed, but it meant pausing the defensive works while the sappers continued their unseen tunnel below.

Logan wiped river spray from his eyes and looked from the iron winch to the parapet stair.
