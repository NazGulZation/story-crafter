# Chapter 1: An Acceptable Dinner

The roar of the blast vanished into the hiss of steam. 

A moment prior, the world had smelled of scorched wallpaper, atomized masonry, and the copper heat of his own shredded flesh in an Albuquerque nursing home. Gustavo Fring had stood in the ruined doorway of Room 6, pulled down the lapels of his suit coat, aligned his necktie with steady fingers, and surrendered to the floor. He had expected the dark void or the sulfurous judgment awaiting men of his trade.

Instead, his eyelids parted upon polished cherry wood and the warm glow of a low-hanging brass chandelier.

Gustavo sat upright in a high-backed upholstered dining chair. The air carried the aroma of roasted rosemary chicken, garlic butter, and an artificial vanilla candle flickering in a frosted glass tumbler. Directly opposite him, a woman with auburn hair resting in soft waves against a cream-colored cowl-neck sweater was tilting a green bottle over a crystal goblet. The pale yellow stream of Pinot Grigio splashed against the glass, releasing a faint, fermented sweetness.

"You look completely drained, honey," she said. Her voice possessed a light, musical register, practiced and smooth. "Another marathon day with the quarterly freight manifests?"

Gustavo did not speak immediately. He remained motionless, allowing his sensory faculties to catalog the physical vessel he now occupied. 

His vision was whole. Both eyes registered the room with crisp stereoscopic clarity, without the searing blindness that had torn away the right hemisphere of his skull seconds earlier. His hands rested upon the table linen—pale, unblemished, with clean-trimmed nails free of calluses or burn scars. When he flexed his fingers, the tendons moved with mild sluggishness. A dull ache throbbed in his lower lumbar spine, accompanied by the tightness of stiff hamstrings born of twelve hours spent sitting in an ergonomic office chair. 

Within the recesses of his conscious mind, memories surfaced in an orderly cascade, filing themselves into neat categorical compartments. 

His host was David Ward. Thirty-two years of age. Senior compliance and logistics auditor for Mid-Valley Freight Solutions. Married for four years to the woman seated across from him: Sarah Elizabeth Ward, twenty-nine, employed part-time at an upscale interior design showroom. The household carried an outstanding mortgage of one hundred and eighty-four thousand dollars against a two-story colonial in a quiet suburban cul-de-sac. David worked sixty-five to seventy hours each week, subsisting on black drip coffee and pre-packaged sandwiches, driven by a desperate, self-effacing urge to provide his wife with social stability and domestic comfort.

Gustavo absorbed the data without judgment. A man provides. That was an elemental truth. Yet the vessel he had inherited was brittle, depleted by anxiety, and starved of physical discipline.

"The audit was thorough," Gustavo replied. His voice emerged in a quiet, measured baritone. The slight Chilean cadence of his former life was softened by David’s Midwestern vocal cords, yet the enunciation remained crisp, deliberate, and devoid of the hesitant stammer David had carried for thirty years. "There were discrepancies in the regional fuel accounts. They required rectification."

Sarah blinked, pausing with the wine bottle hovering an inch above her glass. For a fraction of a second, her hazel eyes widened, searching his face for the usual apologetic smile or the exhausted slump of his shoulders. Finding only an erect posture and an unblinking, courteous gaze, she forced a bright smile and set the bottle down with a soft click.

"Well, you always fix everything, David. You're brilliant with numbers." She picked up her fork, nudging a roasted potato through a pool of herb butter. "I stopped by the grocery after my shift at the showroom. Organic chicken breasts were on sale. I know how much you like the rosemary rub."

"Thank you, Sarah," Gustavo said evenly. "Your consideration is appreciated."

He reached for the stainless-steel fork and steak knife beside his plate. He cut a single, precise rectangular piece of chicken breast, lifted it to his mouth, and chewed twenty times before swallowing. The meat was slightly dry, the rosemary excessive, the salt heavy on the tongue. 

As he ate, Gustavo’s eyes remained fixed upon Sarah Ward. 

To an untrained observer, she presented an image of suburban grace. Yet Gustavo had spent three decades navigating rooms filled with federal prosecutors, cartel assassins, and corrupt municipal commanders. He read human physiology the way an actuary read a balance sheet.

He noted three distinct anomalies within ninety seconds.

First, the acoustic rhythm of her breath. It was shallow, catching slightly at the end of every inhalation. Her pulse hammered visibly beneath the thin skin of her carotid artery, quickening whenever her left hand hovered near the edge of the cherry wood table.

Second, the smartphone. It lay facedown on the linen placemat, positioned precisely two inches to the left of her bread plate. During the brief silence while Gustavo chewed, the device vibrated twice against the wood—a dull, muffled buzz. Sarah’s left hand twitched instantly. Her fingers curled inward, the thumb brushing against the smooth band of her diamond wedding ring. Her gaze darted toward the dark glass screen before jerking back toward Gustavo with exaggerated innocence.

Third, the olfactory profile. 

Beneath the heavy cloud of peach-vanilla body lotion and the buttery steam of the chicken, there lingered another scent. It clung to the ribbed knit collar of her cream sweater and the loose strands of hair behind her left ear. It was the sharp, resinous tang of crushed cedarwood and bergamot, layered over the unmistakable musk of stale, dried perspiration. 

It was an aggressive, expensive cologne favoured by younger men who frequented commercial gymnasiums and nightclubs. David Ward did not own cologne; his cabinet held only unscented deodorant and generic witch hazel aftershave.

Furthermore, as Sarah tilted her head to tuck a stray curl behind her ear, the wide neck of the cowl sweater shifted. On the pale expanse of her left clavicle, partially masked beneath a heavy layer of pressed foundation powder, sat a faint, coin-sized contusion. The center was dull yellow, the perimeter fringed with fading violet. A grip mark, pressed into soft tissue by heavy, calloused fingers.

Gustavo swallowed his sip of tap water, setting the crystal tumbler down upon the coaster with silent precision. 

"Did you receive many visitors at the showroom today?" he asked.

Sarah’s fork scraped lightly against her china. "Oh, the usual. A few clients looking at marble swatches for kitchen islands. A couple from the country club arguing over velvet drapery. It was exhausting, honestly. My feet are killing me."

"And after your shift?" Gustavo asked, his tone level and polite. "Did you proceed directly to the market?"

"Of course," she said quickly. Her smile grew tight at the edges. "Where else would I go? Traffic on the parkway was backed up near the industrial bypass. Some stalled truck blocked the right lane. Why do you ask?"

"Curiosity," Gustavo said softly. "The drive from the showroom to the market is typically twelve minutes. The chicken requires forty-five minutes to roast at three hundred and seventy-five degrees. You were scheduled to leave work at four o'clock. It is now seven-fifteen."

Sarah froze. A sudden flush rose up her neck, mottling the pale skin beneath her jawline. Her hand tightened around the stem of her wineglass until her knuckles whitened.

"Are you... keeping a timetable on me now, David?" Her voice dipped into an offended, petulant cadence, weaponizing indignation to mask her panic. "I had to wait twenty minutes at the butcher counter. Then Mrs. Gable from the homeowner's association caught me in the produce aisle and talked my ear off about the storm drainage fees. I hurried home so you would have a hot dinner after working all day, and now you're interrogating me like a suspect?"

Gustavo observed her performance. He noted the slight tremor in her lower lip and the practiced way she leaned backward, crossing her arms to emphasize her vulnerability. In David’s fragmented memories, this tactic had succeeded every single time. Whenever David noticed an irregularity in their bank statements or an unexplained absence, Sarah deployed hurt feelings and moral outrage, prompting David to apologize profusely, retreat to his desk, and shoulder the blame.

Gustavo did not apologize. He picked up his white linen napkin, dabbed the corners of his mouth, and folded the cloth into a sharp, perfect triangle beside his plate.

"I apologize if my observation caused you distress," Gustavo said, his voice flat and smooth as polished marble. "The logistics of food preparation interest me. The chicken is acceptable. You have managed the household adequately."

Sarah stared at him, off-balance. She had braced for a confrontation, or at least the customary groveling retreat. The icy, dispassionate courtesy left her floating without purchase. 

Her phone vibrated again on the table. A double pulse. 

Gustavo stood up. He smoothed the front of David’s tailored blue dress shirt, pulling the hem taut against the waistband of his charcoal trousers. 

"Please excuse me, Sarah," Gustavo said, inclining his head with punctilious civility. "I will inspect the plumbing in the master bath. The pressure in the upstairs line was uneven yesterday morning."

"Oh," Sarah murmured, her hand darting down to slide the buzzing phone into the pocket of her linen trousers the instant his back turned. "Sure. Take your time, honey. I'll... I'll clear the table."

Gustavo walked with measured, unhurried strides through the archway and up the carpeted staircase. Each step was silent, his weight distributed through the balls of his feet. 

He entered the master bedroom. 

The room was spacious, dominated by a king-sized bed with an upholstered headboard and a slate-grey duvet. The scent of cedarwood cologne was significantly stronger here, hanging faintly in the conditioned air. 

Gustavo moved directly to the nightstands. On David’s side: an alarm clock, a paperback thriller with a creased spine, and a bottle of generic eye drops. On Sarah’s side: a ceramic dish holding pearl earrings, a hand cream dispenser, and two pillows stacked against the wall. 

Gustavo leaned down. On the left side of the mattress—David’s customary sleeping side—the memory foam topper exhibited a deep, irregular depression that had not fully rebounded. Near the headboard, on the walnut rail, two faint smudge marks broke the sheen of the wood wax. The width of the smudges matched the spread of large masculine thumbs gripping the wood from behind.

Gustavo stepped into the adjoining master bathroom. He switched on the vanity light. 

Six recessed halogen bulbs illuminated the mirror. Gustavo looked at the face staring back at him. 

The features were unremarkable. Dark brown hair cut in a generic side-part, pale skin showing faint dark circles beneath brown eyes, a soft jawline untempered by physical violence. A respectable, invisible civilian face. The kind of man who waited patiently in line at the post office and paid his property taxes three days early.

Gustavo reached up with both hands. He gripped the points of his shirt collar, pulling them straight. He adjusted the top button, ensuring the placket aligned with the buckle of his belt. His eyes in the glass were dark, flat, and immovable.

He opened the medicine cabinet. Among the prescription moisturizers and allergy tablets, he found a receipt tucked behind a box of cotton swabs: a purchase order from a boutique lingerie outlet in the neighboring county, dated Tuesday afternoon. Total: two hundred and forty dollars. Cash transaction. 

He closed the cabinet door without a sound.

Gustavo exited the bedroom and walked down the hall into the small corner study. The room smelled of warm electronics, paper, and stale carpet. A mahogany laminate desk supported a dual-monitor desktop computer, an inkjet printer, and three neat stacks of manila folders containing municipal utility bills and mortgage statements.

Gustavo sat down in the high-backed swivel chair. He pressed the power button on the central processing tower. The cooling fans spun to life with a low, metallic whir. 

He leaned back, resting his elbows on the armrests, bringing his fingertips together before his chin in a precise steeple. 

His predecessor had worked fourteen hours a day to preserve this house, blindly trusting that loyalty was a reciprocal currency. He had permitted a parasite to enter his perimeter, sleep in his bed, and consume his resources while laughing at his sacrifice.

Gustavo Fring did not tolerate parasites. He did not tolerate undisciplined subordinates, and he did not tolerate breaches of operational integrity.

The blue login screen of the operating system flickered to life, casting a cold, clean light across his face. Gustavo reached forward, rested David Ward’s uncalloused fingers upon the keyboard, and began typing.
