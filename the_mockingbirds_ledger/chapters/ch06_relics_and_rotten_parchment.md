# Chapter 6: Relics and Rotten Parchment

The gods are remarkably inexpensive to maintain, but their priests require an extraordinary amount of butter and silk.

The Cathedral of the Radiant Dawn was an architectural marvel of ribbed vaults and soaring glass, designed to convince the starving rabble that poverty was a divine virtue while the prelates dined on lampreys in wine. Down in the crypts, however, the sanctity wore thin.

The subterranean vaults smelled of burning frankincense that failed to smother the damp odor of tomb-mold, chilled limestone sweating in the autumn humidity, and the waxen, greasy aroma of unlit beeswax tapers stacked in crates like firewood.

I walked the flagstones of the North Chancel, my footsteps muffled by thick felt-soled slippers.

Before the altar of Saint Jude the Martyr stood High Chancellor Vivienne Claire.

She was draped in pleated robes of bleached samite, banded at the hem in silver wire, an ivory wimple framing a face as delicate and unblemished as curd. Her fingers, long and slender, clicked an ebony rosary as she knelt upon a velvet prie-dieu. To any pilgrim looking through the wrought-iron screen, she was a living saint—an angelic maiden who had risen from humble beginnings through divine grace and sacred purity.

I stood in the shadow of a basalt pillar, holding a small brass casket bound with lead wire.

"The devotion of the faithful is truly inspiring, Lady Vivienne," I said softly, allowing my voice to glide across the resonant stone.

The clicking of the rosary stopped abruptly.

Vivienne did not startle or gasp. A woman who runs a clandestine smuggling syndicate out of a cathedral crypt has excellent control over her breath. She rose smoothly from the prie-dieu, turning with an ethereal, practiced serenity, her pale blue eyes settling upon me.

"The crypts are closed to secular visitors after compline, brother," she said, her voice sweet, musical, and dripping with condescending piety. "If you seek absolution, the confessors sit in the outer nave."

"Absolution is a luxury for men with guilty consciences, my lady," I replied, stepping out from the column’s shadow and offering her a modest bow. "I am merely a man of business delivering a consignment."

She frowned, her gaze dipping to the brass casket in my hands. A tiny crease appeared between her pale, arched brows.

"The church does not receive deliveries at night," she said coldly.

"Not even from the port of San Remo?" I asked, tilting my head with polite curiosity. "Not even when the consignment contains two thousand gold florins in Genoese bills of exchange, remitted in payment for the... *sacred finger-bones* of Saint Jude?"

Vivienne’s porcelain mask fractured.

Her eyes darted instinctively toward the iron-grated stairs leading to the upper nave. She took three swift, silent steps toward me, her samite skirts rustling like dry reeds.

"Where is Brother Paul?" she whispered, her voice stripped entirely of its liturgical sweetness, replaced by the razor-sharp hiss of an cornered viper. "He was to bring the draft to the cloister gate at midnight."

"Brother Paul had an unfortunate encounter with the harbor watch two hours ago," I said smoothly, resting the brass casket upon the edge of a stone sarcophagus. "It seems he was carrying a crate marked *Sanctified Altar Linens* that, when dropped, made the most peculiar clanking sound. Forty-two chalices of pure church plate, freshly hammered flat to conceal the ducal coat of arms."

Vivienne went rigid. Her skin paled until it matched the starched linen of her wimple.

"You're a thief," she hissed. "The Grand Inquisitor will have your skin flayed from your flesh before sunrise."

"The Grand Inquisitor is an elderly gentleman with gout who owes *The Grey River Syndicate* eight hundred sovereigns for his private cellar of vintage Bordeaux," I said with an easy chuckle. "Furthermore, Brother Paul did not spend the evening with the watch. He spent it in the cellars of *The Brazen Quill*, drinking warm cider while explaining the charming little enterprise you have cultivated here."

I drew a folded sheepskin ledger from my inner pocket, turning the pages with deliberate slowness.

"A marvelous operation, really. You remove ancient church silver from the vault, claim it was consumed in the cathedral fire of three years ago, melt it down into ingots, and export it across the Bitter Sea to pay your private debts. To replace the inventory, you purchase pig-iron coated in silver leaf from the dwarf-smiths of the south. And to augment your income, you sell counterfeit saint relics to superstitious foreign dukes."

"Lies," she choked, her hand flying to the rosary at her throat. "Slander born of the Pit!"

"Item twelve," I read aloud, ignoring her protest. *"Fourteenth of August. The left femur of Saint Cecilia. Sold to the Count of Montfort for six hundred gold pieces. Provenance certified under the Great Privy Seal of High Chancellor Vivienne Claire."*

I looked up from the parchment, my grey eyes smiling into hers.

"Remarkable bone, that femur. Especially considering that Saint Ysolde died without legs, having been martyred by the Wheel. The bone in question belonged to a calf slaughtered behind the butcher's row in the Canal Ward."

Vivienne stared at me in horrified silence. Her chest rose and fell in rapid, shallow bursts. For the first time, she truly looked at my face—past the neat beard, past the fine wool coat.

Her blue eyes widened in sudden, staggering comprehension.

"Alden?" she breathed, the word tumbling from her lips like broken glass. "You... you were the one who..."

"Alden Croft," I supplied gently, dipping my head. "The very same novice you once counseled to accept his banishment with holy humility. *'The Light demands strength, brother,'* I believe were your exact words as you watched Lord Godfrey toss my sword into the horse trough."

Vivienne retreated half a step, her back pressing against the cold marble of Saint Maelor’s tomb. Her mind was working frantically, weighing threats, violence, and escape.

Then, true to her nature, she chose the weapon that had served her best for five years.

Her posture softened. The hard, calculating glare in her eyes melted away, replaced by a sudden, dewy sheen of moisture. She lowered her head, her lower lip trembling with exquisite vulnerability, allowing a single tear to trace the curve of her porcelain cheek.

"Alden," she whispered, stepping toward me, her hands reaching out to clasp mine. Her fingers were warm, soft, and perfumed with essence of myrrh. "Oh, Alden... if you only knew how my soul has wept for that day."

*Fascinating,* I thought, letting her take my hand. *The sheer, shameless artistry of it. Margaery Tyrell could have taken lessons from this girl.*

"Did it weep, Vivienne?" I asked, my voice pitched with gentle, sorrowful tenderness.

"Every night," she sobbed softly, pressing her cheek against the rough wool of my sleeve. "You don't understand the power Godfrey held over us! He threatened to brand me as a heretic, to cast me out to the wolves! I was a penniless novice, Alden! I had no choice but to smile, to agree... to submit to his bed! I did it to survive! I swear it by the holy light of the altar!"

She looked up through her wet eyelashes, her face luminous with theatrical repentance.

"Help me, Alden," she breathed, her lips inches from mine. "You always had the kindest heart. Take the ledgers. Burn them. Help me escape this gilded prison, and everything I have is yours. The gold, the church influence... my body, my devotion. We can leave Bell-Garde together."

I looked down at her.

I reached out with my left hand and gently, almost reverently, wiped the tear from her cheek with the pad of my thumb.

Then, I smiled.

"You have an extraordinary talent, my dear," I said softly. "Truly. In the theatre of King's Landing, you would have commanded fifty gold dragons a night for such a performance."

Vivienne froze. Her hands stiffened against my arm.

"The difficulty," I continued, withdrawing my hand and taking the folded ledger from my pocket, "is that I do not want your body. Nor do I have the slightest interest in running away to sleep in ditches with a woman whose word is as brittle as burnt bone."

She pulled back as if stung by an adder, her expression twisting into an ugly, snarling mask.

"What do you want, then?" she hissed. "If you take this to the Inquisitor, Godfrey will protect me! He needs my tithes to pay his retainers! He will have you hanged as a slanderer!"

"He might," I conceded cheerfully. "If I took it to the Inquisitor. But why on earth would I do something so thoroughly uncommercial?"

I took the brass casket from the tomb and placed it directly into her trembling hands.

"Two thousand gold florins, Lady Vivienne. The complete remittance from San Remo, less a modest ten percent handling fee for *The Grey River Syndicate*."

She stared at the casket, utterly uncomprehending. "You're... giving me the coin?"

"Of course," I said smoothly. "You have debts to pay, and a churchwoman in default is of no use to anyone. Henceforth, *The Grey River Syndicate* will handle all your overseas consignments. Our couriers are discreet, our vessels do not leak, and our ledgers are kept under double lock."

"And... and in exchange?" she whispered, her voice trembling.

"In exchange, you will continue to forge your saint bones and smuggle your silver plate," I said, leaning in until my lips brushed the edge of her wimple. "And for every transaction, you will provide my factor with a certified, notarized manifest bearing your personal seal."

Vivienne swallowed hard. "You are binding a noose around my neck."

"I am merely holding the rope, my lady," I whispered with infinite courtesy. "Whether you hang from it depends entirely upon how sweetly you sing when the winter audit arrives."

I offered her a final, sweeping bow and turned toward the crypt stairs.

Behind me, the ebony rosary clicked once more in the gloom—faster this time, like the teeth of a woman shivering in an unheated grave.
