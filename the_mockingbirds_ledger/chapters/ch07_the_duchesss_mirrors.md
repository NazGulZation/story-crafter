# Chapter 7: The Duchess's Mirrors

A woman who trades her honor for a gilded cage will spend her entire life measuring the thickness of the bars.

The salon of the Duchess of Bell-Garde was an overwhelming suffocation of lavender pomade, crushed rosewater powder, and the dry, rustling hiss of heavy damask curtains drawn against the afternoon gale. High above the sea cliffs, the wind shrieked through the leaded glass, but inside the chambers, three hearths roared with fragrant apple-wood, baking the air until it tasted of cloying syrup.

Madame Delphine, the premier modiste of the High Quarter—whose back debts of four hundred sovereigns were owned entirely by *The Grey River Syndicate*—was kneeling upon a velvet stool, pinning a hem of cloth-of-gold around the Duchess Lynette Morrow.

"The waist is too loose, Delphine," Lynette snapped, her voice sharp, high, and ragged with tension. She stood before a triptych of Venetian silvered glass, staring at her own reflection. "You are deliberately making me look thick. I told you, two inches tighter! Do you want the ladies of the court whispering that the Duchess is swelling like a broodmare?"

"A thousand pardons, Your Grace," Delphine murmured meekly, three brass pins held between her lips. "The velvet is stiff, but the whalebone stays can be drawn another notch."

"Then draw them!"

I stood by the lacquer screen near the door, disguised in the dark plum doublet of an assistant draper, holding a bolt of sea-green silk.

Through the silvered glass, I studied Lynette.

She was twenty-three years of age, but the delicate, fresh-faced milkmaid of Weirs-End had been thoroughly buried beneath an avalanche of courtly paint. Her skin was dusted with toxic lead-white that failed to completely conceal the dark, purplish hollows beneath her hazel eyes. Her lips were stained with cochineal red, cracked and dry from lack of water.

Whenever she turned her head, her hand flew instinctively to the pearl-choker at her throat, checking if the gems had slipped.

A woman who was born to mud and turnips, terrified that one day someone would notice she was not made of gold.

A heavy oak door slammed in the outer corridor.

Footsteps echoed—heavy, unhurried, accompanied by the bright, feminine laughter of a younger girl. A moment later, the silhouette of Lord Godfrey Sterling passed the frosted glass transom of the salon. He did not enter. He did not knock. He continued down the gallery, his boisterous baritone voice drifting through the cracks:

*"...the chestnut filly is yours, Lady Clara. A proper mount for the brightest jewel in the South March..."*

The young girl giggled, a breathless, musical sound that hung in the hallway like perfumed smoke.

Inside the salon, Lynette went dead still.

Her hazel eyes locked onto the closed door. The color fled from her painted lips, leaving the cochineal red stark against an ashen pallor. For three seconds, she stood frozen, then her right hand convulsed, her manicured nails digging into the velvet upholstery of the fitting stool until the fabric groaned.

"He was supposed to dine with me," she whispered, her voice cracking, all courtly affectation stripped away. "He swore on the altar he would attend the council dinner tonight."

Delphine did not look up from the pins. A seamstress who lives in royal courts learns to be deaf, dumb, and blind.

"Your Grace," Delphine said softly, "the factor from *The Grey River Trust* is here with the cosmetic accounts."

Lynette whirled around, her skirts flaring with a violent rustle of stiff silk.

"Leave us, Delphine! Take the girls and get out!"

The modiste and her two apprentices gathered their needles and scurried out the side door like mice before a broom.

The heavy door clicked shut. The salon fell silent, save for the crackle of the apple-wood fire and the distant roar of the surf against the cliffs.

Lynette turned her glare upon me. "Well? Did your masters procure the southern night-cream? The one made from pearl-dust and distilled moon-lily?"

I stepped forward, placing the bolt of green silk upon an ebony side table, and retrieved a small vellum portfolio from beneath my cloak.

"The elixirs have arrived from Port Aleron, Your Grace," I said, pitching my voice into that soft, melodic register that calms startled horses. "Six porcelain jars of the restorative balm, sealed with the apothecary's wax. Guaranteed to erase ten years of grief from the brow within a single fortnight."

Lynette let out a ragged, shuddering breath, pressing her palms against her temples.

"The price?"

"A trifling sum for the First Lady of Osterlund," I purred, opening the portfolio to reveal a series of neatly drawn bonds. "Fourteen hundred gold sovereigns."

Lynette dropped her hands, her eyes widening in fury. "Fourteen hundred? Are you insane? Last month the cost was eight hundred!"

"The winter storms have closed the southern shipping lanes, Your Grace," I explained with gentle, sorrowful patience. "Two cogs were lost off the Horn of Skulls. The apothecary had to convey the chests by armed mule-train through the mountain passes. Furthermore, the ingredients include twenty ounces of refined ambergris."

"I don't have fourteen hundred sovereigns in cash!" she cried, pacing the woven silk carpet, her silk train sweeping across the floor. "Godfrey cleared out the civil account yesterday to buy three southern blood-stallions for his personal guard! The exchequer is empty! Vivienne Claire laughed in my face when I asked for a cathedral advance!"

"A tragic state of affairs," I murmured, my eyes following her pacing figure with quiet amusement. "That a woman of your grace and station should be subjected to the indignities of a tight purse. Especially now... when younger noblewomen from the south are arriving with dowries of fifty thousand pieces."

Lynette stopped dead in her tracks.

She spun to face me, her hazel eyes wild with paranoia. "What do you know about the southern girls?"

"A factor hears things, Your Grace," I said with a deprecating smile. "The merchants talk. The couriers gossip over their ale. It is whispered that the Count of Val-Rhea has offered his eldest daughter, Lady Clara, to Lord Godfrey. A girl of sixteen, possessing the largest iron mine in the borderlands. They say the Duke is already drafting the petition to the High Orthodoxy for a... *matrimonial dispensation*."

Lynette’s jaw worked soundlessly. The word *dispensation* hung in the heated air like a headsman’s axe.

In Osterlund, a marriage could be dissolved by the High Orthodoxy if the union had produced no male heir within five years, or if the wife had entered the contract under "insufficient heraldic standing."

Lynette Morrow was the daughter of a pig-farmer from Weirs-End. Her heraldic standing was entirely fictitious, invented by a bribed herald on the day Godfrey took her to bed.

"He wouldn't dare," she whispered, though her voice shook so violently her teeth clicked. "I made him! I managed his households! I stood by him when we had nothing!"

"Of course you did, Your Grace," I said smoothly, stepping closer and offering her a clean goose quill tipped with gold. "And that is precisely why you must protect yourself."

"How?" she gasped, looking at me with the frantic, drowning eyes of a woman staring into the abyss. "How do I protect myself if I have no gold?"

"Land, Your Grace," I said softly, laying the parchment upon the writing desk. "The High Stewardship holds the reversionary deeds to the royal grain-manors of the Lower Sunder. Three agricultural estates currently yielding twenty thousand bushels a year."

Lynette shrank back. "Those manors belong to the Crown. I cannot sell Crown land without Godfrey's seal."

"You are not selling them, my lady," I said, my voice dropping to an intimate, soothing whisper. "Heaven forbid! You are merely *mortgaging* the future grain yields to *The Grey River Syndicate* for a term of three years. In exchange, the syndicate will immediately credit your personal account with twelve thousand gold sovereigns in hard, unminted bullion."

She stared at the parchment. Her breath came fast and shallow, rattling against the whalebone stays of her corset.

"Twelve thousand..." she breathed.

"Twelve thousand," I repeated, pressing the quill into her cold, trembling fingers. "Enough to pay the apothecary for a lifetime of beauty. Enough to hire your own private company of sworn lances to guard your person. Enough to ensure that whatever happens with Lady Clara, the Duchess of Bell-Garde will never return to Weirs-End with empty hands."

She looked down at the parchment. She did not read the small, densely scribbled High Imperial clauses at the bottom of the leaf.

She did not see the clause stating that in the event of default on the grain deliveries, the physical freehold of the manors, their tenant populations, and their stone manor houses would transfer unconditionally to the creditor.

She saw only the twelve thousand sovereigns. She saw only the illusion of security.

She dipped the quill into the inkpot and signed her name with a flourish: *Lynette, Duchess of Bell-Garde.*

"A wise decision, Your Grace," I said, taking the document, blowing gently upon the wet ink, and folding it neatly into my velvet portfolio.

"The gold," she said, looking up at me with feverish intensity. "When will it be deposited?"

"Tomorrow morning, at the Merchant's Gate," I promised, bowing low. "Rest easy tonight, my lady. The syndicate will watch over your fortunes."

I turned and walked toward the heavy oak door.

As my hand touched the brass latch, I paused and looked back over my shoulder.

She was standing before the triptych mirror once more, smoothing her pearl collar, smiling a pathetic, desperate smile at her own painted reflection, convinced she had outmaneuvered the court.

Five years ago, she had stood in a tavern and told a faithful boy that a girl grows up, that he was blind, that she preferred a real man who offered gold.

Now, she had just mortgaged the bread of ten thousand peasants to buy paint for her fading cheeks.

I opened the door and stepped out into the gallery, the signed deed burning pleasantly against my breast.

The bird was in the cage. All that remained was to shut the iron door.
