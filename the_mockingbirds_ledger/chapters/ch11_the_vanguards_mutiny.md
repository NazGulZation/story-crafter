# Chapter 11: The Vanguard's Mutiny

When a general loses her army, it rarely happens in blood; it happens when her men discover who signed their paychests.

Dawn broke over Bell-Garde not with sunlight, but with a suffocating, slate-grey blizzard that blanketed the city in eight inches of dense, frozen snow. The parade ground before the Iron Gate was a vast sheet of white, scarred only by the black ruts of iron-tired wagon wheels and the restless shifting of eight hundred heavy infantrymen standing in ranks of four.

The cold was vicious, biting through greaves and gambesons, frosting the iron nasal-guards of the soldiers until their breath came in thick, ragged steam.

On the raised wooden dais before the armory stood Lady Gisela Brandt.

She had abandoned her formal dress uniform. She was clad in full battle harness: blackened steel plate etched with silver wolves, an iron sallet pulled low over her russet hair, and a heavy bastard sword held bare in her right gauntlet, its point planted in the frozen mud.

Her face was purple with the cold and twisted with desperate, manic fury.

"Legionnaires of the Vanguard!" Gisela’s voice rang across the snowy square, harsh and cracked from hours of screaming orders into the blizzard. "The civil council has been corrupted by merchant traitors! A cabal of usurers and black-market factors has seized the city’s bread, starving our people to extort sovereign gold! By the emergency authority vested in me by the Grand Duke and Lord Godfrey Sterling, I declare martial law across Bell-Garde!"

She raised her broadsword toward the Lower Ward.

"We march on the Great Granary! We break the gates, hang the merchant factors from the wharf cranes, and distribute the flour to our barracks! Forward, the First Cohort!"

The wind howled across the parade ground, tossing the snow in blinding flurries.

Not a single boot moved.

Eight hundred soldiers stood motionless, their iron pikes held upright, their shields resting against their shins like rows of black gravestones. The only sound was the whistling gale through their ungreased scabbards and the hollow flap of the vanguard's pennant in the freezing air.

Gisela’s eyes widened in disbelief. She took two steps down the wooden stairs of the dais, the snow crunching violently beneath her sabatons.

"Captain Donald!" she roared, pointing her steel blade at the grizzled veteran standing at the head of the center column. "What is the meaning of this hesitation? Sound the advance! Move your men, or I'll have you broken on the wheel for treason!"

Donald did not sound the advance.

He stood with his iron-reinforced boots planted wide in the snow. He slowly turned his head to look at her, his leathery face weathered and impassive beneath his kettle helm.

"With respect, Grand Marshal," Donald said, his deep voice carrying through the ranks, "our men don't march on empty bellies, and they don't swing steel against their creditors."

"Creditors?" Gisela spat, stepping closer, foam speckling her lips. "He is an extortionist! A parasite! He is Alden Croft—a coward who deserted our party five years ago!"

"He paid our four months' back-wages at sunrise, my lady," Donald replied calmly.

From beneath his heavy woolen cloak, Donald drew a folded parchment slip stamped with blue wax.

"Every sergeant, every corporal, every pike-man in this yard received two silver crowns and a barrel of flour delivered to his family's hovel this morning. Signed by Master Croft. Underwritten by *The Grey River Syndicate*."

A murmur of agreement ran through the ranks—a low, rhythmic rumble of eight hundred men nodding in cold unison.

"I am your commander!" Gisela screamed, her voice cracking, wild panic breaking through her discipline. "You took an oath upon the altar of the Gilded Lion! You are sworn to the sword!"

"A sword don't feed four children through a blizzard, Marshal," another sergeant called out from the third rank. "And that pot-metal iron you bought from your uncle broke during morning drill."

The rhythmic clatter of iron-shod hooves echoed through the gatehouse.

A black lacquered carriage, its wheels fitted with ice-cleats, rolled smoothly onto the snow-covered parade ground, escorted by forty mounted cross-bowmen in grey woolen cloaks.

The carriage door opened.

I stepped down into the snow. I was dressed warmly in my charcoal coat, my boots lined with soft shearling, a dark wool scarf wrapped neatly around my throat. My gloved fingers lightly touched the silver mockingbird pin at my breast.

Behind me walked two clerks carrying a brass-bound strongbox.

I walked through the ranks of the Vanguard. The soldiers did not bar my path. They stepped aside with crisp, disciplined precision, lowering the tips of their pikes in silent, respectful deference as I passed.

I halted five paces from Gisela Brandt.

She stood alone in the center of the snowy yard, her bastard sword wavering in her grip, her chest heaving as if she had just run ten miles uphill.

"You," she choked out, her amber eyes burning with helpless, poisonous rage. "You bought them. You bought my soldiers like cattle."

"A soldier who is not paid is not an army, Gisela," I said softly, my voice quiet, reasonable, and conversational. "He is merely an armed laborer waiting for an honest employer. Godfrey gave them ballads and tourney ribbons; I gave them bread and silver. Who do you think they should serve?"

"I am the Grand Marshal of Osterlund!" she snarled, taking a step toward me, raising her broadsword with two hands. "I took the oath from the Grand Duke himself! I will cut your throat where you stand, you miserable quill-pusher!"

She lunged.

She did not take three paces.

Captain Donald’s broadsword cleared its scabbard with a sharp, ringing hiss. The veteran stepped between us, his heavy steel blade crashing against Gisela’s bastard sword with a deafening, bone-jarring *CLANG*. Sparks showered into the snow.

Before Gisela could recover her balance, two sergeants lunged from the flank, their iron halberd shafts locking across her chest and shoulders, pinning her arms to her sides and driving her down to her knees in the freezing slush.

Her broadsword slipped from her numbed fingers, tumbling into the snow.

"Let go of me, you dogs!" she shrieked, thrashing against the halberds like an enraged lynx. "Treason! This is high treason!"

"Not treason, my lady," Donald said coldly, looking down at her with stone-faced contempt. "Relief of command. By order of the Sovereign Regency Council."

I knelt before her in the snow.

The wind whipped a flurry of white flakes across her face, melting against her flushed, furious cheeks. I reached out with a gloved hand and picked up her fallen sword by the ivory hilt, turning it over to inspect the blade.

"The steel is cracked along the fuller, Gisela," I observed quietly. "The third smith who worked on this blade told my factor that your uncle skimmed forty percent of the cobalt from the smelting flues. If you had swung this against a real shield, it would have shattered in your hands."

"Kill me, then," she spat, her teeth bared, blood flecking her lips where she had bitten her tongue. "Do you think I fear dying? I’m a soldier! I fought in the mud while you ran away!"

"You didn't fight in the mud, Gisela," I whispered, leaning in until my lips were an inch from her frozen ear. "Alden Croft fought in the mud. Five years ago, on the southern road, three footpads took you in the brush. They had a knife to your belly and their hands down your tunic."

Gisela went completely rigid. The thrashing stopped. Her amber eyes dilated in sheer terror.

"Alden took two crossbow quarrels through his shoulder to drag them off you," I murmured, my voice as cold and merciless as the winter ice. "He carried you four leagues through the freezing drizzle to the nearest apothecary, with his blood dripping into your hair. And when you woke up, you looked at his ragged clothes and decided he wasn't glorious enough for a girl with ambitions."

A shudder ran through her armored frame—a deep, visceral revulsion of memory and shame.

"You sat in *The Boar’s Tusk*," I continued relentlessly, "and you laughed while Godfrey kicked him into the gutter. You told him he was dead weight. You told him you had been warming Godfrey’s blankets while Alden stood night-watch in the rain."

"Alden..." she whispered, her voice breaking into a ragged, pathetic sob. "Alden, please..."

"Alden died of shame, you treacherous little hypocrite," I said, rising smoothly to my feet, tossing the cracked sword onto the snow at Donald’s feet. "I am the Master of Coin. And I do not forgive bad debts."

I looked at Captain Donald.

"Remove the Grand Marshal's insignia. Escort her to the lower cells of the East Bastion. She will await trial for embezzlement, treason, and the purchase of fraudulent ordnance."

"Understood, Master Croft," Donald barked.

The two sergeants hauled Gisela to her feet. Her ivory-crested helmet was knocked loose, tumbling into the mud, exposing her cropped russet hair, plastered to her skull with melting snow and tears. She did not scream now. She did not roar. She was dragged away like a broken sack of iron, her boots trailing through the slush.

I turned back to the eight hundred men standing in the blizzard.

"Captain Donald," I called out, my voice ringing clear across the silent square.

"Sir!"

"Double the fuel rations for the barracks. Provide every man with a ration of rum and dried beef for the noon meal. The Vanguard is under the protection of *The Grey River Syndicate*."

Eight hundred men raised their pikes in unison.

"HAIL MASTER CROFT!" the roar shattered the freezing air, rolling across the snow-covered roofs of Bell-Garde like thunder.

I stepped back into my carriage, closing the lacquered door against the cold.

The army was mine. Now, to deal with the church.
