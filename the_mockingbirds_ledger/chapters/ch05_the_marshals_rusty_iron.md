# Chapter 5: The Marshal's Rusty Iron

A soldier with an empty belly is an unruly dog; a soldier with an empty purse is an auction waiting for a bidder.

The East Bastion smelled of things that had failed to harden: stale vinegar from the quench-tubs, damp charcoal that refused to take a flame, and the greasy, acidic sweat of six hundred armored men living too close together in the autumn cold.

I stood by the armorer’s open lean-to, dressed in the dark wool of an accredited merchant factor, listening to the rhythmic clang of a smith attempting to straighten a warped spear-point. Every fourth blow sheared a fleck of grey slag from the iron.

"Pot-metal," the smith spat, tossing the hammer onto the anvil with a dull, hollow ring. "Melted down scrap-shovels from the East Foundry. You hit a man in boiled leather with this, and the head folds like wet card."

"Quiet, you fool," muttered an armory sergeant, his eyes flicking nervously toward the courtyard gate. "The Grand Marshal is coming down the line."

The heavy oak doors of the inner keep groaned open, their ungreased iron hinges screeching like gulls over a carcass.

Lady Gisela Brandt strode into the courtyard.

She had changed in five years, yet the core was unmistakable. In the old days, when Alden Croft had carried her across the salt-fens with an arrow through her calf, she had been a lean, savage thing in boiled leather and greaves that didn't match. Now, she wore blackened steel fluted along the breast, with gold filigree tracing the gorget. Her russet hair was cropped close to her skull in the fashion of the imperial border guard, and an ivory-hilted broadsword hung at her hip.

She walked with the stiff, wide-legged swagger of a woman who believed that an army was simply a weapon you aimed and fired.

Behind her trailed three senior captains, their faces dark with suppressed fury.

"Where are the twelve-foot pikes for the Second Cohort?" Gisela demanded, her voice like gravel grinding between stones. She halted before the master smith, her iron-riveted gauntlet resting upon the pommel of her sword. "The border inspection is in eight days. If the Duke finds the vanguard drilling with willow staves, I'll have you flogged to the bone."

The smith did not flinch. A man who spends thirty years over an open fire rarely fears a woman in clean steel.

"You can flog my hide till it's boot leather, Grand Marshal," the smith rasped, wiping soot from his brow with a greasy forearm. "Won't change the fact that the forge flues are cold. Master Cole down at the Coal Wharf hasn't delivered a single cart since Michaelmas. Says the Vanguard owes him four hundred silver groats for the summer charcoal."

Gisela’s jaw clenched, the tendon in her neck corded like bowstring. "Tell Cole the crown warrants will clear after the harvest audit."

"Cole don't eat crown warrants, my lady," the smith retorted flatly. "Neither do my apprentices. Pay the ironmongers, or forge your own steel."

A low, dangerous silence descended over the yard. The three captains behind Gisela shifted their weight, their eyes avoiding hers.

One of them—Captain Donald, the thickset veteran whose back Nesta had scrubbed in my bathhouse two nights ago—cleared his throat.

"It isn't just the charcoal, Marshal," Donald muttered, his voice rumbling beneath his iron coif. "The men are four months without their copper allowances. The sutlers are refusing to sell ale on tick. Three companies from the West Gate walked off watch yesterday to hire out as ditch-diggers for the merchant guilds."

"Then hang the deserters!" Gisela snarled, turning on him with sudden, venomous fury. "Are you soldiers or washerwomen? When Lord Godfrey took the High Pass, we marched three weeks on dried peas and horse blood! If a man will not fight for his realm, he swings from the battlements!"

"A splendid sentiment for a ballad, Grand Marshal," I said, stepping smoothly out from the shadow of the armory lean-to.

The courtyard turned toward me.

Gisela’s hand dropped to her sword hilt with instinctive violence. "Who allowed this civilian into the garrison?"

I offered her a deep, elegant bow—the bow of a high courtier addressing an empress, executed with flawless grace.

"Alden Croft, factor for *The Grey River Syndicate*," I replied softly, my voice calm, melodic, and entirely devoid of martial swagger. "Forgive the intrusion, my lady. I was merely inspecting the consignment of Norrmark iron currently awaiting customs clearance on the High Wharf."

Gisela froze.

Her amber eyes locked onto my face. For three agonizing seconds, the courtyard held its breath. I watched the gears of memory grind behind her brow. She looked at my black hair, cut neatly and brushed back from a clean forehead; she looked at my trimmed beard, my tailored charcoal coat, my polished leather shoes.

She had known Alden Croft as a ragged boy with mud in his teeth, a clumsy longsword across his back, and tears in his eyes as she mocked him in the tavern five years ago.

Now, she saw a prosperous factor holding a velvet-bound ledger beneath his arm.

"Croft?" she said, her voice dropping, a flicker of bewildered recognition crossing her sharp features. "Alden... Croft?"

"At your service, Grand Marshal," I said, my smile warm, polite, and entirely empty. "The syndicate has recently acquired the paper for the municipal charcoal depots and the iron charters of the Lower Sunder."

"You..." She took half a step forward, her brow furrowing in genuine confusion. "You're with the merchant trust? How are you alive? Godfrey said you drowned yourself in the canal years ago."

"Rumors of my demise were somewhat overstated, my lady," I said, chuckling softly with disarming modesty. "It turns out that while I possessed remarkably little talent for the broadsword, numbers were somewhat kinder to my disposition. Water always finds its level."

Gisela’s lip curled. The confusion on her face vanished, replaced by the deep, instinctive contempt that martial brutes always feel for men who handle paper.

"A quill-pusher," she sneered, relaxing her stance. "A peddler of ledgers. I suppose that suits a coward better than steel."

"Infinitely so," I agreed, inclining my head in cheerful acquiescence. "Quills rarely sever arteries, and ledgers rarely demand that one sleep in wet ditches. Though, as it happens, this particular quill holds forty tons of high-grade Norrmark pig-iron currently sitting in warehouse seven."

Gisela’s smirk faltered. She glanced at the master smith, who had suddenly perked up like a hound catching the scent of roast mutton.

"Norrmark iron?" the smith breathed. "Cold-smelted? Low sulfur?"

"The finest quality," I purred, looking directly into Gisela’s amber eyes. "Sufficient to forge twelve hundred pike-heads and re-plate sixty suits of heavy cuirass. Furthermore, *The Grey River Syndicate* has recently purchased Master Cole’s charcoal debts."

Gisela crossed her iron-clad arms, her chest heaving slightly beneath the blackened steel. "What is your price, factor? The crown does not pay cash for raw materiel. We issue sovereign debentures."

"And the syndicate is delighted to accept crown debentures," I said, stepping closer, my voice dropping into that confidential, silky cadence that had once seduced the Small Council of Westeros. "At twelve percent interest, underwritten by a lien upon the gate tolls of the East Bastion."

Gisela frowned. "The gate tolls belong to the municipality."

"Duchess Lynette Morrow signed the authority to lease municipal tolls to private credit-holders three days ago," I said, producing a folded parchment with a crisp, emerald wax seal. "A mere administrative formality, I assure you. The iron will be delivered to your forge by dawn tomorrow. The charcoal carts will arrive by noon."

Gisela stared at the document, then back at me. A triumphant, arrogant sneer crept back onto her lips.

"So the beggar returns to feed from our scraps," she mocked, snatching the parchment from my fingers and signing it with a harsh scrape of her quill. "Still eager to be useful, aren't you, Alden? Just like the old days. Fetching water, sharpening blades, desperate for a pat on the head."

"It is my eternal privilege to serve the heroes of the realm, my lady," I murmured, bowing so low that she could not see the cold, dancing light in my grey eyes.

She tossed the parchment back to me, turning her back to bark orders at her captains. "Donald! Get sixty men down to warehouse seven at daybreak! Get those forges burning!"

As the captains dispersed, Captain Donald lingered by the gate.

I walked past him, pausing just close enough for our shoulders to brush.

"Your third boy is sick with the lung-rot, Donald," I said under my breath, my voice so faint only he could hear. "The apothecary in the Low Ward has the Peruvian bark, but he demands twelve silver shillings."

Donald stiffened, his hand trembling on his baldric. He looked at me with wide, terrified eyes. "How do you..."

I pressed a heavy purse of silver coins into his calloused palm, my fingers closing firmly over his.

"The Peruvian bark is already paid for, Captain," I whispered, smiling gently into his pale face. "Along with your gambling marker at the *Weeping Bell*. You owe Gisela your sword, but you owe me your son's breath. When the time comes to remember that... I know you will choose wisely."

I did not wait for his answer.

I walked out through the East Gate into the gathering sleet, the signed iron-lien tucked safely against my ribs.

Gisela Brandt thought she had bought iron to save her vanguard.

In truth, she had just handed me the keys to her armory and the loyalty of her senior captain.

Steel may break bones, but debt owns the hand that swings it.
