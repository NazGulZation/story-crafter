# Chapter 13: The Tears of the Milkmaid

A vow sworn in childhood is written on water, but a mortgage deed is carved into granite.

The old ruined chapel of Saint Ursula stood on the windswept bluff three miles outside the East Gate, overlooking the grey, frothing breakers of the Bitter Sea. It was the highest point on the road to Weirs-End. Ten years ago, two peasant children with mud between their toes had climbed to this very ridge, staring toward the gleaming spires of Bell-Garde, swearing that one day they would walk those streets together as lord and lady.

Now, the chapel roof was caved in, its altar overgrown with blackened nettles, the air tasting sharply of salt-spray, rotting sea-kelp tossed upon the rocks below, and the damp, sour wool of a drenched travelling cloak.

The sea gale screamed through the broken lancet arches, driving needles of freezing drizzle into the ruined nave.

Lynette Morrow stood shivering beside the collapsed granite font.

She had stripped off the heavy court velvets that were being seized by the bailiffs at the Citadel. She wore a simple gown of faded plum wool, torn at the hem where it had caught on the gorse-bushes, wrapped in an old, threadbare cloak pinned with a cheap brass brooch.

Her hair was soaked by the rain, plastered in dark, dripping ribbons across her pale, ruined face. The lead-white paint had washed away completely, revealing the blotched, hollow skin of a terrified twenty-three-year-old country girl whose world had dissolved beneath her feet.

She heard my footsteps against the wet shale.

She turned with a sharp, ragged gasp, her hands flying to her breast, clutching the wet wool as if to protect her heart.

"Alden," she whispered, her voice trembling, drowned out by the roar of the surf until she stepped forward into the lee of the stone wall.

I did not hurry. I walked into the chapel, my charcoal-grey cloak shedding the rain, my leather gloves dry and supple. I paused three paces from her, leaning my back casually against the broken stone pillar, my hands tucked into my sleeves.

"A bleak afternoon for a pilgrimage, Your Grace," I remarked pleasantly. "Or should I say... Mistress Morrow? Given that the High Council of Regency dissolved your ducal patent at noon today."

Lynette flinched, a low, keening sob catching in her throat. She took another unsteady step toward me, her velvet shoes squelching in the mud of the nave floor.

"Don't call me that," she begged, her hazel eyes red-rimmed and swollen from hours of weeping. "Don't look at me with those cold eyes, Alden! It's me! It's Lynette! Your Lynette from Weirs-End!"

"I remember Lynette from Weirs-End," I said calmly. "A sensible girl who could milk five goats before dawn and mend a linen smock with neat, invisible stitches. But that girl died five years ago in an inn called *The Boar’s Tusk*, when she announced to a room full of drunken mercenaries that she had spent two years warming the bed of an electrum-plated peacock."

Lynette collapsed to her knees before me, her soaked skirts spreading across the wet gravel and nettles. She reached out, her trembling, bare hands grasping for my boots.

"He lied to me, Alden!" she wailed, tears streaming down her hollow cheeks, mingling with the salt drizzle. "Godfrey promised me everything! He swore he would make me Queen when the old Duke died! He swore our children would wear crowns! I was sixteen years old, Alden! I was a foolish, ignorant village girl dazzled by his golden armor and his fine horses!"

She leaned her forehead against my leather boot, sobbing so violently that her breath rattled in her chest.

"I never loved him!" she cried into the leather. "Every time he touched me, I closed my eyes and thought of you! I thought of the willows by the river! I thought of the wooden acorn you carved for me when we were twelve! I did it all for us, Alden! To give us a future! To lift us out of the cabbage mud!"

I looked down at the crown of her wet, matted head.

*Fascinating,* I thought, a quiet, sardonic sneer curling within my mind. *How easily the human mind rewrites its own treachery. When she was swimming in pearls and velvet, Alden Croft was a clumsy dog beneath her contempt. Now that the bailiffs have taken her mirrors and Godfrey has kicked her into the rain, she was merely a martyr who sacrificed her virtue for love.*

In King's Landing, Lysa Tully had wept the same tears, swearing that she had poisoned her husband and betrayed her house solely for my sake. Women will commit every crime under the sun, and then expect a man to call it devotion.

"Stand up, Lynette," I said quietly.

She looked up through her tangled, wet hair, her hazel eyes burning with a desperate, pathetic spark of hope.

"You... you forgive me?" she breathed, her hands sliding up my calves. "You remember the willows?"

"I said, stand up," I repeated, my tone dropping to a cold, level command that brooked no disobedience.

She scrambled to her feet, swaying on her ankles, wiping her nose with the wet sleeve of her cloak.

I drew a rolled parchment from my coat, bound with a black ribbon and sealed with the golden sun-crest of House Sterling.

"Do you recognize this seal, Lynette?"

She stared at the gold wax, her breath hitching. "Godfrey's private signet..."

"Delivered to my townhouse this morning by the Lord Chancellor," I said, untying the ribbon and snapping the seal. "A petition of marital nullity, drafted and signed under Godfrey Sterling’s own hand."

I held the parchment before her eyes, pointing to the Latin clauses inscribed in dark, flourishing ink.

"Read it, my dear. Item three: *'The marriage between Lord Godfrey Sterling and the peasant woman Lynette Morrow is declared null, void, and of no legal effect, by reason of fraudulent personation of nobility, base rural birth, and total failure of dowry.'*"

Lynette gasped, her hands flying to her mouth, her eyes darting across the signatures.

"He... he signed it?" she whispered, horrified. "He cast me out like a stray dog?"

"Of course he signed it," I said with a thin, mocking chuckle. "Why wouldn't he? Yesterday afternoon, Godfrey entered into a provisional betrothal contract with the twelve-year-old daughter of the Count of Val-Rhea. Her dowry is forty thousand sovereigns in unencumbered silver, which Godfrey desperately requires to pay off the interest on his tourney horses. To Godfrey, you were never a wife, Lynette. You were merely an inexpensive mistress who was foolish enough to manage his household accounts without asking for a wage."

A terrible, suffocating silence descended over the ruined chapel, broken only by the mournful wailing of the wind through the stone arches.

Lynette’s jaw hung slack. Her eyes were empty, the last shreds of her vanity, her pride, and her delusions stripped away, leaving only the raw, shivering carcass of a betrayed peasant girl.

"He discarded me," she whispered, looking at her empty hands. "Just like... just like we discarded you."

"Precisely like you discarded Alden," I corrected her gently. "Except when you discarded Alden, he had given you five years of loyal devotion, defended your family from taxes, and bled for your safety. When Godfrey discarded you, you were simply an unpaid debt he could no longer afford to service."

"What am I to do?" she wept, looking around the desolate, rain-swept ruins of the bluff. "Where am I to go? My father's cottage in Weirs-End was seized by the tax-men! The palace guards threw my clothes into the gutter! I have no food, Alden! I have no coin! I have nowhere to sleep!"

She took half a step toward me, her eyes pleading, her voice dropping into a desperate, groveling whine.

"Take me back, Alden. Please. I'll be your servant. I'll scrub the floors of *The Brazen Quill*. I'll wash your linen. I'll sleep on the straw at the foot of your bed. Only don't abandon me to the winter."

I looked into her face.

There was no hatred in my chest. Hatred is an emotional luxury reserved for angry young men with swords who care about honor. I felt only the clinical, satisfied balance of an account that had been settled to the last penny.

I reached into the pocket of my charcoal waistcoat.

My fingers found a small, circular piece of metal—cold, notched at the edges, and green with verdigris.

I held it out between my thumb and forefinger.

A single copper farthing.

Lynette blinked at the tiny, worthless coin. "What... what is this?"

"The price of a bowl of coarse turnip broth at the public soup kitchens in the Low Ward," I said softly. "The exact amount Alden Croft had in his pocket when you threw him out of *The Boar’s Tusk* five years ago."

I dropped the coin.

It tumbled through the freezing air and landed with a soft *chink* in the muddy puddle between her soaked velvet shoes.

"There is an opening for a fleece-washer at the public wool-stalls near the canal, Mistress Morrow," I said, pulling my leather gloves tight across my fingers. "The wage is three copper pennies a day. If you work diligently and refrain from stealing the lard, you will not starve before April."

"Alden!" she shrieked, falling to her knees in the mud once more, clawing at the gravel to find the copper coin. "Alden, don't leave me here! In the name of our childhood! In the name of the promise!"

I turned my back on her and walked through the broken arch of the chapel.

"A promise, my dear Lynette," I called back over my shoulder, my voice carrying clean and sharp against the roaring sea wind, "is an unsecured debt. And unsecured debts are the first to be canceled when the market crashes."

I climbed into my waiting carriage on the high road, closing the door against the rain.

Behind me, on the lonely bluff above the sea, the frantic, hysterical weeping of the milkmaid of Weirs-End was swallowed entirely by the cold northern gale.
