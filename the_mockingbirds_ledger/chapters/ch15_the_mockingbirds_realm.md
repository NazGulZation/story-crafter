# Chapter 15: The Mockingbird's Realm

Six months can alter the face of a kingdom, provided one understands the arithmetic of power.

The autumn dawn broke crisp and diamond-clear over the High Citadel of Bell-Garde, sweeping away the sea mists with a brisk, invigorating wind that carried the scent of woodsmoke, roasted chestnuts, and the rich, yeasty aroma of fresh wheat bread rising from three hundred municipal bakeries. Below the white limestone battlements, the River Sunder gleamed like polished slate, its docks bustling with forty merchant cogs unloading salt, timber, and northern wool without a single delayed manifest.

I stood upon the Grand Balcony of the Council Spire, resting my elbows upon the carved balustrade.

I wore a tunic of dark charcoal damask lined with white ermine, over which hung the heavy gold collar of the Lord Protector and Supreme High Chancellor of Osterlund. Upon my chest rested my silver mockingbird brooch, its uncut black diamond catching the first golden rays of the morning sun.

Between the knuckles of my right hand, a freshly minted gold sovereign danced with effortless, rhythmic dexterity—rolling over the bone, slipping between the fingers, flashing in the dawn light.

The face stamped upon the gold was no longer Lord Godfrey Sterling slaying a mythical beast.

It was an open ledger, flanked by a quill and an ear of wheat.

"The morning reports, Your Grace," a voice murmured behind me.

It was Master Mallow, now Chief Comptroller of Customs, bowed so low his nose nearly brushed his polished calfskin shoes. His hands, once stained with cheap elderberry wash, were clean and adorned with two modest signet rings.

"Speak, Mallow," I said, without turning around, watching a barge unfurl its red sails on the river below.

"The municipal grain reserves are filled to capacity—eighty thousand bushels of winter rye secured in the new brick silos," Mallow reported with evident pride. "The foreign merchant loans have been amortized to twelve percent, and the port tariffs for the third quarter yielded forty-four thousand gold sovereigns in net surplus."

"A respectable beginning," I said softly. "And the prisoners?"

Mallow consulted a small vellum scroll.

"The former champion, Godfrey Sterling, completed his sentence in the municipal stocks three months ago. He was assigned to the sanitary corvée of the East Bastion under Captain Donald. He cleans the latrines and carries charcoal for the armory hearths. The guards report he has grown remarkably quiet. When the commoners pass him on the road, they no longer spit; they simply laugh and toss him crusts of moldy rye."

"A fitting retirement for a slayer of beasts," I said, a thin smile touching my lips. "A man who lived for the roar of the crowd should never be deprived of an audience. And the women?"

"Gisela Brandt accepted an indenture of penal exile upon the eastern marches," Mallow continued. "She commands forty conscripted ditch-diggers building the border palisade. Her captains report she sleeps on straw, eats salt pork, and has not touched a broadsword since the winter."

"And Vivienne Claire?"

"The Grand Inquisitor stripped her of her vows and consecrated names. She resides in the lay dormitory of Saint Eluned’s cloister, scrubbing the flagstones of the chapter house in coarse burlap. She is forbidden to speak above a whisper on pain of the scourge."

"And Lynette Morrow?"

Mallow hesitated for a fraction of a second.

"She remains in the Canal Ward, Your Grace. Working at the municipal textile mill in the Low Sump. She washes raw sheep fleeces in the lye vats for three copper farthings a day. Her hands are cracked with the soap, and her hair has gone grey at the temples. A merchant factor offered to take her as a scullery maid last week, but she refused, muttering something about waiting for the willows to bloom."

I paused, the gold sovereign coming to a sudden, silent halt against my palm.

The willows of Weirs-End.

The dead boy's heart within this chest did not stir. Not even a flutter. The phantom of Alden Croft had finally evaporated, dissolved by the cold, cleansing solvent of reality.

"Ensure the textile mill pays its workers their full wages on Friday, Mallow," I said, waving a hand toward the door. "And provide the fleece-washers with an extra half-loaf of bread on holy days. I have no desire to see my laborers freeze."

"You are a paragon of divine mercy, Your Grace," Mallow said, bowing once more before retreating through the arched doorway.

"Mercy," I chuckled softly to the morning wind. "What an astonishing word to describe good management."

I leaned against the balustrade, looking out over the sprawling roofs of the city I now owned.

In Westeros, they played the game with poison, daggers, incest, and shadow-babies. They schemed behind every curtain, murdered their own kin for an acre of rock, and fought endless, bloody wars that left the land scorched and starving. Brandon Stark had cut me open; Sansa Stark had cut my throat.

Yet here, in this naive world of shining knights and weeping maidens, they thought that a hero was a man who possessed golden curls and a square jaw. They thought that glory was won by swinging an iron bar at a northern animal, and that a realm could be ruled with tourney ribbons and songs of chivalry.

They never understood that the world does not belong to the men who swing the swords.

It belongs to the men who pay the smiths.

It belongs to the men who own the grain.

It belongs to the quiet man in the dark corner of the tavern, watching the heroes spend their coin, smiling as he adds another column of figures to his ledger.

"Chaos isn't a pit," I whispered, rolling the gold sovereign one last time before slipping it into my velvet pocket. "Chaos is a ladder. And when the heroes fall... only the accountant reaches the top."

I turned my back on the balcony and walked into the Great Council Chamber, humming a quiet tune from the Fingers, ready to balance the books of an empire.
