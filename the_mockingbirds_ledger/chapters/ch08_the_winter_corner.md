# Chapter 8: The Winter Corner

Winter is the only auditor whose judgment cannot be bribed.

The frost arrived three weeks before the feast of Saint Martin, carried down from the jagged northern crags on the back of a biting, howling gale. By the third morning, the surface of the River Sunder was choked with jagged plates of black river-ice that ground against the wooden pilings of the wharves with the sickening groan of snapping timber.

The air smelled of salt-sleet, freezing oak pitch, and the dry, musty starch of frosted grain sacks being hauled across frozen mud.

I stood upon the roof platform of the Great Granary of the Lower Basin, wrapped in a heavy charcoal cloak lined with beaver fur. My breath plumed white in the freezing air, dispersing into the grey, leaden sky.

Below me, sixty flat-bottomed river barges sat locked in the frozen slips, their holds packed to the gunwales with winter rye, hard northern wheat, and dried field peas.

Every single bushel of it belonged to *The Grey River Syndicate*.

"All eight northern convoys accounted for, Master Croft," reported Master Hemlock, the former court clerk who now served as my chief harbor factor. The old man’s nose was purple with the cold, but his eyes gleamed with the ravenous delight of an accountant whose ledgers have swallowed a river. "The frost locked the upper cataracts yesterday. Not another barge will descend the Sunder until the spring thaw in April."

"And the municipal reserves?" I asked, watching a solitary gull circle low over the grey water.

"The High Keep’s granaries hold twelve days' flour for the royal household and the garrison," Hemlock wheezed, rubbing his gloved hands together. "The civil bakers in the Low Ward have enough meal for forty-eight hours. By Thursday, there won't be an uncracked loaf of bread between the Iron Gate and the sea."

I drew a deep, satisfying breath of the freezing air. It tasted clean, cold, and entirely of power.

A monopoly on bread is far more absolute than a monopoly on steel. When a king raises an army, men may choose whether to fight or run; when a city has no bread, men must either eat or butcher the man who holds the keys.

By midday, the panic had begun.

From the parapet of my granary, the roar of the lower wards drifted upward—a dull, rhythmic hum that grew louder as the hours passed, punctuated by the sharp crack of bakeries' shutters being kicked in by desperate mobs. A standard loaf of coarse rye, which had sold for two copper farthings three days ago, was trading for half a silver groat at the West Market.

Bakers were beaten with their own peel-irons. Women with starving infants clawed at the gates of the High Citadel.

At the second hour of the afternoon, the clatter of armored hooves echoed across the frozen mud of the wharf.

A detachment of twenty heavy horsemen from the Sovereign Vanguard galloped down the quay, their black steel cloaks whipping in the wind. At their head rode Lady Gisela Brandt, her ivory-hilted sword drawn, her face taut and pale beneath her bascinet.

Beside her rattled a closed carriage bearing the ducal arms of Bell-Garde.

The horsemen halted before the iron gates of the Great Granary. The guards at my gates—stout, well-fed stevedores armed with heavy billhooks and crossbows, paid four silver shillings a week from my private purse—did not budge.

Gisela spurred her mount forward, her blade pointing directly at the gatehouse.

"By order of the Sovereign Council!" she roared, her voice carrying over the whistling wind. "Open these gates in the name of Lord Godfrey Sterling! The grain in these stores is requisitioned for the defense of the realm!"

The wicket door of the gatehouse clicked open.

I stepped out onto the raised wooden walkway above the gate, leaning my forearms casually against the timber rail.

"Requisition is such an ugly word, Grand Marshal," I called down, my voice light, pleasant, and carrying clearly in the frosty air. "It sounds so dreadfully like theft."

Gisela’s horse danced sideways on the frozen ruts, its iron shoes striking sparks against the stones. She reined the beast in with a violent jerk, her amber eyes narrowing as she looked up.

"Croft?" she yelled, disbelief warring with fury in her voice. "You own these stores?"

"The Grey River Syndicate owns them, my lady," I replied, offering her an amicable wave of my gloved hand. "Every bushel was purchased under valid commercial charter, stamped by the Customs Assessor and paid for in hard silver coin."

The door of the ducal carriage burst open.

Duchess Lynette Morrow stepped down into the freezing mud, her velvet boots sinking into the slush. She was wrapped in an ermine mantle that cost three hundred sovereigns, but beneath her hood, her face was white with unadulterated terror.

"Alden!" she cried out, stumbling toward the gate, her hands reaching through the iron bars. "Alden, thank the heavens! Tell your men to open the doors! The mobs have broken through the lower palisade! They are burning the tax offices! If we do not bring grain to the Citadel by sunset, the Duke says the city will fall to anarchy!"

I looked down at her.

The wind tore at her ermine hood, exposing her disheveled honey-brown hair and the streaks of running lead-powder upon her cheeks. She was not an imperial duchess now; she was the terrified girl from Weirs-End, begging the boy she had discarded to pull her out of the fire.

"Anarchy is a terribly inconvenient state of affairs, Your Grace," I said with profound sympathy. "Though I confess, I am somewhat confused. When I spoke to your factors last week regarding the renewal of the grain contracts, I was informed that the Crown preferred to rely upon the royal manors of the Lower Sunder."

Lynette’s breath caught in her throat. Her knuckles turned white against the iron bars.

"The manors..." she choked out, her voice dropping into a desperate, frantic whisper. "The frost... the northern sleet ruined the crops before the harvest was threshed! Alden, please! For the love of our home, for the memories of Weirs-End!"

"Weirs-End?" I mused aloud, resting my chin in my hand as if searching my memory. "Ah, yes. The muddy little ditch where children swear eternal vows beneath willows, only to discover that vows are remarkably fragile things when tested by a handsome horse and a gilded scabbard."

Lynette flinched as if I had struck her across the mouth.

"You bastard," Gisela snarled from the saddle, urging her charger closer to the gate. "Open the gates or I'll have the pioneers bring up the battering ram! We'll take the flour over your corpse!"

"You may certainly try, Grand Marshal," I said, my voice dropping its playful cadence, becoming as cold and level as a sheet of river-ice.

I raised my right hand.

Along the roofline of the granary, forty crossbowmen stepped forward, their heavy steel arbalests cocked and loaded, their iron-tipped bolts aimed directly into the tight knot of horsemen below.

Beside each crossbowman stood a stevedore holding a sealed ceramic vessel of distilled pine pitch, fitted with a burning sulfur wick.

"The timber of this granary is seasoned pine, Lady Brandt," I said softly. "One spark, and forty thousand bushels of grain will be reduced to black ash in twenty minutes. If you strike my gates, the fire will consume the flour. The mob behind you will see the smoke. And when twenty thousand starving men realize that their Grand Marshal has burned their bread... I wonder how long your twenty horsemen will survive the ride back to the Citadel?"

Gisela’s face went rigid. She looked at the cocked crossbows. She looked at the pitch-pots. She looked back at the city gates, where the dark, roiling roar of the bread riots was swelling like a tidal wave.

Her sword arm trembled. Then, slowly, with agonizing humiliation, she lowered the point of her blade.

"What do you want?" she spat, the words tasting like poison in her mouth.

"A civilized negotiation, of course," I purred, leaning over the rail with a radiant, benevolent smile. "I am a merchant, not a barbarian. I have no wish to see the citizens starve."

"Name your price," Lynette begged, her voice sobbing against the iron bars.

"The price of grain has risen somewhat due to the unfortunate inclement weather," I said. "Six silver shillings per bushel for the common rye. Ten for the fine wheat."

"That is eight times the autumn rate!" Gisela roared.

"Supply and demand, my dear Marshal. A law far older and far more absolute than any decree of the Sovereign Council."

I reached into my cloak and drew forth a heavy vellum document, already stamped with the syndicate's seal.

"Furthermore, the syndicate will not accept promissory notes signed solely by the High Stewardship. We require a formal sovereign requisition, signed by the Grand Duke and Lord Godfrey Sterling himself, ratifying *The Grey River Syndicate* as the Chief Creditor and Supreme Logistical Factor of the Realm."

I dropped the parchment through the iron gate. It fluttered down, landing in the slush at Lynette’s velvet-clad feet.

"Bring me that parchment with the Hero's signature by nightfall, and twenty barge-loads of flour will be delivered to the city bakeries under armed escort."

I smiled down at the two women, my grey eyes sparkling with cold, razor-sharp malice.

"Until then, ladies... I suggest you counsel your citizens to chew their boots."

I turned my back on the gate and walked back into the warmth of the granary loft, leaving the Grand Marshal and the Duchess shivering together in the mud of the frozen wharf.
