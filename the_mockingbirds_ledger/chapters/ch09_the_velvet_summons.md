# Chapter 9: The Velvet Summons

The climb to the High Citadel consists of precisely two hundred and twelve marble steps.

Five years ago, Alden Croft had been dragged down those steps by four city watchmen, his boots scuffing the wet stone, his broken sword bouncing behind him like a tin pot tied to a dog’s tail. The retainers of Lord Godfrey had spat upon his head as he passed, laughing at the beggar who had dared to dream of walking among champions.

Tonight, the same steps were swept clean of snow and lined with sixty honor guards from the Sovereign Vanguard, their halberds held at present-arms.

I did not walk quickly. A man who rushes reveals his anxiety; a man who strolls commands the architecture.

I wore a coat of deep charcoal-grey wool, woven from fine West-Reach merino, edged at the cuffs and hem with midnight-black velvet. It was unadorned by gold braid, gems, or heraldic posturing. Upon my left lapel rested my only ornament: a delicate, wrought-silver brooch shaped like a singing mockingbird, its eye a tiny, uncut black diamond.

Beside me walked two clerks carrying iron-bound oak caskets filled with the realm's promissory notes, bills of exchange, and foreign mortgages.

We paused in the moonlit Colonnade of Lions just outside the Grand Council ante-chambers. The night air was razor-sharp, carrying the scent of pine smoke from the city below and the briny, freezing mist rising from the roaring surf against the sea-cliffs.

From the private gallery ahead, the rustle of stiff damask skirts approached.

Duchess Lynette Morrow stepped out into the colonnade, intending to take the air before the council convened. She was adorned in an emerald silk kirtle and a choker of river pearls that pinched her pale throat. When her hazel eyes found my silhouette in the moonlight, she froze mid-stride, her breath hitching in her chest.

"Master Croft," she said, her voice wavering between haughty court formality and rural unease. "You... you walk late in the high corridors."

I stepped from the shadows of the marble archway, offering her a slow, leisurely bow.

"A creditor never sleeps while his gold wanders, Your Grace," I murmured, my voice soft, rhythmic, and intimate.

Lynette’s fingers flew instinctively to her pearl choker, checking if the gems sat straight. Her skin was painted in lead-white, but beneath the cosmetic crust, a sickly grey pallor showed through.

"You delivered the grain," she said, stepping back until her velvet train caught upon the stone plinth of a marble lion. "The bakeries have flour. You have your sovereign requisitions. Why are you here, Alden?"

"I am here to ensure all obligations are properly settled," I replied, taking a single, measured pace closer. The faint, sweet aroma of her lavender pomade mingled with the cold stone air. "You wear your pearls with such poise, Lynette. Did Lord Godfrey purchase them with the twelve thousand sovereigns you raised against the royal grain-manors of the Lower Sunder?"

Lynette went rigid. The color vanished completely from her painted lips.

"You..." she stammered, her hazel eyes widening in sheer, mounting dread. "The factor from the draper's shop... Madame Delphine's agent... that was *your* man?"

"A prudent merchant keeps his ledger close to his chest," I said, smiling that thin, mocking smile that had once terrified high lords in King's Landing. "You signed the parchment with such a graceful hand, my lady. It would be a terrible tragedy if the winter sleet prevented the manors from fulfilling their yield. In which case... the freehold reverts unconditionally to the syndicate."

"What are you doing?" she gasped, her hands shaking so violently that the pearl drops in her ears rattled against her cheeks. She looked at my face, searching the dark grey eyes of the boy who had once promised her the world under the willows of Weirs-End, and found only an icy, impenetrable abyss. "What are you weaving around us, Alden? This isn't just about grain. You're... you're doing something to this city. Something monstrous."

She could not discern the mechanics—she had no mind for syndicated debt, defaulted crown notes, or purchased soldier arrears—but her primal peasant instinct screamed that an iron net had been drawn tight around their necks.

"Monstrous?" I echoed, tilting my head with genuine, quiet amusement. "I am merely a humble clerk balancing an open book. A debt is a curious thing, Lynette. You imagined you could trade one man for another, sell an oath for a handful of gilded tourney ribbons, and the world would simply forget. But the ledger always balances. And tonight... every copper farthing will be tallied."

I leaned in, my voice dropping into a lethal, velvet whisper:

"Tell me, my dear... does Godfrey's bed feel as warm now that the winter has set in?"

Lynette let out a strangled cry, pulled her skirts violently aside, and fled down the gallery toward the high ante-solar.

I did not pursue her. I stood in the colonnade, resting my fingertips upon the cool silver of my mockingbird pin, listening with quiet delight.

The carved oak doors of the ante-solar were half-ajar, permitting every word to carry through the marble hall.

Inside the solar, a massive cedar fire crackled in the hearth. Lord Godfrey Sterling sat sprawled upon a gilded settle, nursing a chased silver goblet of spiced vintage wine, flanked by Grand Marshal Gisela Brandt in her polished black armor and High Chancellor Vivienne Claire in her starched white samite wimple.

Lynette burst into the room, her slippers skidding across the woven rugs, throwing herself at Godfrey's knees.

"Godfrey! You must listen to me!" she shrieked, clutching the hem of his crimson velvet tunic. Her voice cracked with hysterical, suffocating terror. "It's him! It's Alden! He's not here for a simple grain settlement! He has laid a trap for us—he's going to destroy us all!"

Godfrey frowned, looking down at her with lazy, aristocratic irritation, swirling the wine in his goblet.

"Control yourself, Lynette," Godfrey said, his resonant baritone dripping with contempt. "You're screeching like a washerwoman in front of the Grand Marshal. What nonsense is this?"

"I saw his eyes!" she wept, tears cutting dark streaks through the lead-powder on her face. "He's weaving something around the realm! He bought my manors, he bought the harbor grain, he's here for our lives! We have to execute him right now! Sound the alarm, summon the Vanguard, throw him from the sea-cliffs before he steps into the council chamber!"

Godfrey threw back his head and laughed—a booming, arrogant, golden sound that bounced off the high stone ceiling.

"Have you lost your rustic little mind, woman?" Godfrey mocked, shaking her hands off his tunic as if brushing away dirt. "Trembling in terror over Alden Croft? That spineless, limp-wristed country dog who couldn't even swing a wooden practice sword without spraining his wrist?"

"Godfrey, please—"

"Quiet!" Godfrey barked, his chiseled face hardening into cold vanity. "He is a miserable little peddler who got lucky scraping together a few sacks of frozen rye from northern river barges. I am the Champion of the Light, the Slayer of the Great Behemoth! I have broken warlords and crushed armies. If that ink-stained wretch dares speak above a whisper in council, I will take his head off with a single blow of my broadsword. Go wash your face; you are humiliating me."

Grand Marshal Gisela Brandt snorted into her wine, resting her gauntleted hand casually against the hilt of her sidearm.

"The boy who blubbered like a child when a roadside cutpurse grazed his shoulder?" Gisela sneered, shaking her head with disgust. "He is a worm, Lynette. My Vanguard holds every gate, every tower, every battlement in Bell-Garde. If that scribbling clerk twitches the wrong way, my captains will skewer him like a pig on a spit."

High Chancellor Vivienne Claire clicked her ebony rosary beads between her manicured fingers, her lips curling in pious, saccharine derision.

"The Light favors the righteous and the strong, Lynette, not superstitious fools who jump at their own shadows," Vivienne purred, her pale blue eyes cold as winter frost. "Alden Croft was a faithless, pathetic wretch who should have died in a ditch five years ago where we threw him. Stop your vulgar country shrieking."

Beyond the carved oak screen, I stood in the corridor, listening to their laughter.

*He should have died in a ditch five years ago.*

A slow, glorious smile spread across my face—the same smile I wore when I watched Eddard Stark step into the throne room of the Red Keep.

There is no creature on earth quite so delightfully blind as an arrogant fool who believes his own songs.

I turned to my two clerks.

"Pick up the caskets, gentlemen," I whispered. "It is time to present the bill."

The two-leaved mahogany doors of the Grand Council Chamber groaned inward on oiled brass pivots.

The warmth of the chamber struck me like a wall of heated spice: seasoned cedar logs crackling in the massive granite hearth carved with rampant lions, the heavy aroma of beeswax tapers burning in brass candelabras, and the heady bouquet of warmed Malmsey wine laced with cloves and cinnamon.

Around the horseshoe-shaped council table sat the masters of Osterlund.

At the center sat the Grand Duke, an elderly, gout-ridden nobleman in ermine robes, his rheumy eyes staring blankly at the polished mahogany.

To his right sat Lord Godfrey Sterling, his crimson tunic smoothed, his flaxen hair gleaming in the candlelight, radiating the supreme confidence of a predator who believes himself untouchable.

To Godfrey’s left sat Duchess Lynette Morrow, shivering uncontrollably, her eyes darting toward the floor, utterly defeated by the mockery of her peers.

Further down the curve sat Grand Marshal Gisela Brandt, her chin held high in defiant contempt, and beside her sat High Chancellor Vivienne Claire, clicking her beads with tranquil, pious arrogance.

"Presenting Master Alden Croft," the chamberlain’s voice announced, his staff striking the marble floor with three sharp raps. "Chief Factor of *The Grey River Syndicate*, Senior Assessor to the League of Creditors, and Holder of the Sovereign Bread Warrants."

I stepped forward into the circle of candlelight, stopping five paces from the mahogany table.

I offered the Grand Duke a slow, courtly bow—the precise thirty-degree inclination of the torso used by the Master of Coin when greeting royalty.

"Your Grace," I said softly, my voice rich, low, and soothing. "My lords and ladies of the Council. I have answered your gracious summons."

Godfrey Sterling leaned back in his tall, gilded chair, resting his chin in his hand. He looked at me with that lazy, white-toothed grin that had graced a thousand tapestries.

"You're a hard bargainer, Master Croft," Godfrey said, his resonant baritone filling the vaulted chamber with effortless ease. "You squeezed our treasury dry for that northern flour. Half the lords in this hall wanted you arrested for extortion."

"Commerce is often mistaken for malice by those who do not keep the books, my lord," I replied, my tone radiating modest deference. "The ice on the Sunder was unforgiving. My syndicate expended fifty horses and twelve river lighters to bring the grain through the locks. We took the risk; we merely ask for the agreed return."

"And the return was paid," Godfrey said, waving a negligent, diamond-ringed hand toward the table. "Twenty-two thousand sovereigns in sovereign debentures, underwritten by the future wine harvest of the southern valleys. You have your paper, factor. Why have you demanded this midnight audience?"

Beside him, Lynette’s knuckles turned white. She did not look up.

"I requested this audience, Lord Godfrey," I said, motioning to my two clerks, "because the term of the sovereign debt has expired."

The Grand Duke stirred in his chair, blinking. "Expired? What debt?"

My clerks stepped forward, setting the two iron-bound caskets upon the polished mahogany. The locks clicked open with the heavy, definitive snap of a sprung bear trap.

I stepped to the table and drew forth the master ledger—a thick, vellum-bound register bound in black calfskin, its edges gilded with lead.

"The kingdom's debt, Your Grace," I said, my voice dropping into that terrifying, gentle whisper that had once made the lords of the Seven Kingdoms sweat through their velvets.

I opened the ledger. The vellum pages rustled in the silent room.

"Item one: The municipal charcoal debentures, issued to Grand Marshal Brandt's garrison, four months in arrears. Total principal: fourteen thousand silver groats."

I turned a page.

"Item two: The foreign exchange bonds issued by High Chancellor Vivienne Claire to the merchant houses of Val-Rhea and Khors, secured against the reliquaries of the First Archon. Total defaulted balance: twenty-two thousand gold florins."

I turned another page.

"Item three: The sovereign mortgages upon the royal grain-manors of the Lower Sunder, executed under the private hand and seal of Duchess Lynette Morrow. Defaulted: twelve thousand gold sovereigns."

I turned the final leaf, laying my palm flat across the crimson totals at the foot of the parchment.

"Item four: The primary sovereign loans issued to Lord Godfrey Sterling for the Anniversary of the Great Slaying, the construction of the Gilded Sun Fountain, and the maintenance of sixty tourney courser steeds. Defaulted: two hundred and sixty-four thousand gold sovereigns."

The council chamber fell into a silence so profound that the sputtering of a dying candle sounded like a whip crack.

Godfrey’s smile vanished. The color drained slowly from his chiseled face, leaving his skin the shade of chalk.

"What is the meaning of this insolence?" Godfrey growled, leaning forward, his hands gripping the mahogany table until the wood groaned. "Those notes were distributed among twenty different merchant houses across the continent!"

"They were, my lord," I said smoothly, looking directly into his cold, blue eyes. "Until three weeks ago. When *The Grey River Syndicate* purchased eighty-four percent of those obligations at a modest discount from your terrified creditors."

I closed the ledger with a soft, hollow *THUD*.

"Every promissory warrant. Every reliquary lien. Every grain mortgage. Every unpaid soldier's wage. They now reside within these two caskets."

I smiled—a thin, immaculate, mocking smile that made the hairs on Godfrey's neck stand up.

"The realm of Osterlund is in unconditional default, my lords. And as of midnight tonight... the creditor has arrived to collect."

Godfrey stared at me.

And in that fraction of a second, the fog of vanity finally cleared from his mind. He saw the black hair. He saw the dark, calculating grey eyes. He heard the quiet, rhythmic cadence that he had laughed at in *The Boar's Tusk* five years ago.

The Hero of Bell-Garde went pale as death.

Arc One had ended. The ladder was built.

Now, the descent would begin.
