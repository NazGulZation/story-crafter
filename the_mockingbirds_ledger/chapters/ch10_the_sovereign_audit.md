# Chapter 10: The Sovereign Audit

### I. The Marshal’s Iron (Gisela Brandt)

A sword was the only truth that ever held up on a battlefield. 

Numbers were for clerks who lacked the guts to bleed. That was what I told myself as I sat at the mahogany council table, thumbing the cold iron rivets of my gauntlets beneath the rim of my blackened dress cuffs. Across the room, the cedar fire crackled in the granite hearth, but the air in the Grand Council Chamber felt thin, sour, and suffocating. 

Beside me, High Chancellor Vivienne was clicking her ebony beads with a feverish, rhythmic rasp that scraped on my nerves like dull iron on a grindstone. To my right, Lynette was shivering in her emerald silks, pale as a fresh shroud, staring at the table as if she expected the polished wood to swallow her whole.

I thought Lynette had lost her country wits in the solar when she threw herself at Godfrey’s boots, babbling that Alden Croft had come back as a phantom to butcher us all. 

*Alden Croft.* 

I almost laughed aloud into my wine cup. I remembered the boy. I remembered the hedge-fighter who used to mend my split leather greaves by the campfire while his hands shook from exhaustion. He was dead weight—a sentimental novice who lacked the iron stomach to rise in Godfrey’s court. We threw him into the mud behind *The Boar’s Tusk* five years ago, broke his practice blade, and left him to rot. 

Yet the man standing five paces from the Grand Duke’s chair didn't look like a wretch from the gutter. 

He wore charcoal West-Reach wool tailored to perfection, with a wrought-silver mockingbird pinned over his chest. His posture was leisurely, his dark grey eyes calm and unblinking. When his clerks dropped the two iron-bound caskets onto the mahogany, the locks popped open with a heavy, metallic snap that sounded entirely too much like an arbalest being cocked.

Then he opened the black calfskin ledger. 

He spoke in a low, velvet cadence, and with every sentence, he hammered an iron wedge into our foundation. 

Fourteen thousand silver groats in garrison fuel arrears. Twenty-two thousand gold florins in defaulted foreign bonds. Twelve thousand sovereigns on the Lower Sunder grain-manors. Two hundred and sixty-four thousand sovereigns in Godfrey’s private tourney debts. 

Three hundred and twelve thousand gold sovereigns in total. The entire kingdom was mortgaged to *The Grey River Syndicate*.

"You dare?" Godfrey hissed. 

The Champion of the Light rose from his gilded chair. Electrum wire bit into the knuckles of his right hand as six inches of his legendary broadsword cleared the scabbard with a shrill, scraping shriek. "You insolent dog! You sit there in your peddler’s wool, spewing numbers like a market fishwife, and tell me my realm belongs to a syndicate of cutpurses?"

I reached for the hilt of my sidearm, expecting Croft to flinch, to drop to his knees, to beg the Hero for mercy like he had five years ago.

He didn't flinch. 

He didn't even reach for a knife. 

Croft merely leaned back against his crimson velvet chair, picked up his silver wine goblet, and took a slow, leisurely sip of vintage red. 

"Draw the steel, Godfrey," Croft murmured. His voice was quiet, melodic, and colder than northern river-ice. "Draw it completely from the sheath. Let the Grand Duke see how the Champion of the Light resolves a debt."

Godfrey froze, the gilded blade caught half in the candlelight.

"You bluff!" I barked, slamming my palm down onto the mahogany. "The city watch is under my command! One signal, and the Sovereign Vanguard will seize your warehouses, hang your factors from the wharf cranes, and reclaim the grain!"

Croft turned his head. 

Those grey eyes locked onto mine. For the first time in five years, I saw what was behind his face—not the broken boy who had dragged me through the southern mud, but an ancient, smiling predator looking down the length of a butcher’s blade. 

"Will they, Lady Brandt?" he whispered, a thin, pitying smile touching his lips. "Have you checked the garrison accounts this evening?"

A sudden, freezing knot seized my gut. "What did you do?"

"Item fourteen of the Sovereign Audit," Croft said, tapping the black vellum page. "Three days ago, *The Grey River Syndicate* purchased the complete arrears of the Sovereign Vanguard—forty-eight thousand silver shillings owed to eight hundred legionnaires. As of sunset, every company captain holds a certified draft upon my banking house in the Low Ward, cashable tomorrow morning."

He leaned forward, resting his forearms on the table.

"I pay their wages, Gisela. I pay for the bread their children eat. I pay for the charcoal that warms their barracks. If you order them to draw steel against me, do you truly believe they will march for a commander who gave them pot-metal pikes and four months of empty promises?"

The blood drained from my face until my skin felt numb. 

I looked toward the chamber doors. 

Stationed at the threshold stood two heavy halberdiers of my own Vanguard—veteran guardsmen who had sworn their oaths to my banner. 

"Captain Donald!" I snapped, my voice cracking in the dead silence. "Guard the door! Arrest this factor!"

Neither man moved. 

Their iron sallets stayed locked forward. Their halberd shafts remained rooted to the marble. Not a single muscle twitched beneath their mail, and not one of them would look me in the eye. 

The truth hit me with the crushing weight of a falling portcullis. 

My army was gone. My command was gone. The boy we left to die had bought my soldiers from right under my boots.

### II. The Creditor’s Harvest (Lord Petyr Baelish)

There is an exquisite poetry in watching a warrior realize that his sword has no edge.

Grand Marshal Brandt sat frozen in her seat, her hand hovering over a useless pommel, her amber eyes wide with the hollow, sickened dread of a commander who has given an order and heard only silence in return.

Beside her, Godfrey Sterling looked as though someone had drained the blood from his veins and replaced it with ditch-water. His broadsword hung suspended in the air, neither drawn nor sheathed, his knuckles twitching against the electrum wire.

I set my wine goblet down upon the mahogany table with a soft, definitive *click*.

"And you, Lady Vivienne," I said, turning my gaze to the trembling churchwoman.

Vivienne collapsed from her chair, dropping to her knees upon the cold marble flags. Her white samite robes pooled around her like spilled cream, her slender fingers clutching her ebony rosary in a frantic, rattling prayer.

"Alden... please," she wept, her voice breaking into a ragged sob. "In the name of the sacred Light—"

"Do not invoke the Light to a man who holds the notarized shipping manifests for your forged relics and stolen sacramental silver," I said, cutting her off with icy disdain. "The Grand Inquisitor was delivered certified transcripts of your southern smuggling ring two hours ago. Even now, thirty episcopal bailiffs are waiting outside the cathedral cloister to seal your reliquaries."

Vivienne let out a wailing cry, burying her face against the stones.

Godfrey looked slowly around the circle.

His Grand Marshal was paralyzed with horror. His High Chancellor was weeping in the dirt. His Duchess was huddled in her chair like a broken porcelain doll, sobbing silently into her sleeves. And the Grand Duke was covering his rheumy eyes, groaning into his ermine mantle.

The gilded illusion was stripped away. The rot beneath was exposed for all the court to see.

"What do you want?" Godfrey rasped, his broadsword slowly sliding back into its gilded scabbard with a dull, defeated click. His chest heaved, his handsome, curated curls disheveled, looking suddenly gaunt, bewildered, and broken. "Land? Titles? You want your old cottage back? You want Weirs-End?"

I laughed.

It was a melodic, genuine laugh that echoed off the vaulted stone arches of the high chamber.

"Weirs-End? A muddy ditch filled with pigs and cabbage patches? My dear Godfrey... how remarkably small your imagination remains."

I rose smoothly to my feet, brushing an invisible speck of lint from my charcoal wool, and adjusted the silver mockingbird pin at my throat.

"I do not want Weirs-End. I want everything you possess."

From the second casket, I drew forth four parchment warrants, inscribed in High Imperial script, bearing the blank counter-seals of the Sovereign Regency.

"The terms of liquidation are remarkably simple," I said, laying the pages before the trembling Grand Duke.

"First: Lord Godfrey Sterling is stripped of all sovereign emergency authority, tourney stipends, and military precedence. His private estates, coursers, and golden armor are attached to satisfy the creditor claims of *The Grey River Syndicate*."

Godfrey gasped, clutching his chest as if an invisible quarrel had struck him through the ribs.

"Second: Lady Gisela Brandt is relieved of command of the Sovereign Vanguard, pending an official trial for garrison embezzlement and the purchase of fraudulent ordnance."

Gisela’s teeth clicked shut in silent, suffocating fury, her gauntlets trembling against her knees.

"Third: Lady Vivienne Claire is remanded to the custody of the High Inquisitor for the high crimes of simony and church theft."

"And fourth: Duchess Lynette Morrow’s marriage to Lord Godfrey is referred to the Grand Council for immediate annulment, upon the grounds of insolvency and fraudulent nobility."

Lynette screamed—a raw, shrill screech that bounced violently off the stone walls—and fainted dead away, her head slipping from the arm of her chair to strike the floor with a hollow thud.

Godfrey did not even glance down at her. He stood there shivering in his crimson velvet tunic, staring at me as though I were an apparition drawn from the depths of the void.

"You're a demon," he whispered, his lips pale in the dying candlelight. "No man does this with ink."

"A demon?" I murmured, walking around the mahogany table until I stood three inches from his broad chest. I looked up into his handsome, empty eyes, my smile razor-sharp. "No, Godfrey. A demon takes your soul in exchange for gold. I merely allowed you to spend coin you did not have, until you hung yourself with your own vain appetite."

I reached out with my gloved hand and patted his armored shoulder with gentle, mocking condescension.

"Tomorrow morning, the audit goes public. The heralds will read these ledgers in every square in Bell-Garde. I suggest you pack your fine tunics tonight, my lord. The bailiffs arrive at dawn."

I turned my back on the council and walked toward the high double doors.

As I passed the threshold, Captain Donald and his sentry snapped their boots together, their halberd shafts striking the marble floor with a thunderous, loyal salute.

"Good evening, Captains," I said smoothly.

The Sovereign Audit was finished. Now, the liquidation would begin.
