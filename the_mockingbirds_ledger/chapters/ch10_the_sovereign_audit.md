# Chapter 10: The Sovereign Audit

A sword can cleave a man’s skull, but a ledger cuts through his legitimacy.

The Grand Council Chamber was suffocating in the sour, cold sweat of five people who had just realized their kingdom was made of glass. Outside the tall lancet windows, the winter gale rattled the leaded panes, but within the room, the only sound was the frantic ticking of the bronze water-clock in the corner.

Lord Godfrey Sterling’s hand had found the pommel of his gilded broadsword.

The knuckles of his right hand were white, the electrum wire wrapping biting into his flesh. His jaw worked, the chiseled contours of his face contorted into an ugly, violent snarl.

"You dare?" Godfrey hissed, his voice trembling with an aristocratic rage that bordered on hysteria. "You insolent dog! You sit there in your peddler’s wool, spewing numbers like a market fishwife, and tell me my realm belongs to a syndicate of cutpurses?"

"Not cutpurses, my lord," I said, remaining seated, leaning back into the crimson velvet upholstery of my chair. I picked up my goblet of vintage wine, inhaling the delicate scent of crushed blackberries and oak tannins. "Lawful creditors. Men of commerce who advanced your grace the means to play at king while you bled your realm white."

"I am the Hero of Osterlund!" Godfrey roared, half-rising from his gilded throne, six inches of gleaming steel scraping from his scabbard with a shrill, metallic screech. "I slew the Great Northern Behemoth! I broke the wild clans at the high passes! A word from my lips, and forty guards will drag you to the highest battlement and throw you into the sea!"

Beside him, the Grand Duke covered his face with his trembling, spotted hands, groaning in terror.

I did not flinch. I did not reach for a dagger. I merely took a slow, measured sip of wine, swirled the liquid across my tongue, and swallowed.

"Draw the steel, Godfrey," I said softly, stripping every vestige of courtly honorific from my tone. "Draw it completely from the sheath. Let the Grand Duke see how the Champion of the Light resolves a debt."

Godfrey froze, his sword caught between the scabbard and the candlelight.

"You think this is a game of tourney ribbons?" I continued, setting the silver goblet down on the mahogany table with a quiet, deliberate *click*. "You think you are facing Alden Croft, the clumsy village boy who wept when you stole his milkmaid and threw his sword into the mud?"

Lynette let out a sharp, choked gasp, her face buried in her sleeves, her shoulders shaking violently.

"Alden Croft died five years ago in the gutter behind *The Boar’s Tusk*," I said, my grey eyes locking onto Godfrey’s furious blue glare with an icy, merciless intensity. "The man who sits before you is Master of Coin. And if so much as a drop of my blood touches this Persian carpet, your world ends within the hour."

"You bluff!" Gisela Brandt barked, her hand slamming onto her sword belt. "The city watch is under my command! One signal, and the garrison will seize your warehouses, hang your factors, and reclaim the grain!"

I turned my head slowly to regard the Grand Marshal.

"Will they, Lady Brandt?" I asked, allowing a thin, pitying smile to touch my lips. "Have you checked the garrison accounts this evening?"

Gisela’s brow furrowed in sudden unease. "What did you do?"

"Item fourteen of the Sovereign Audit," I said, tapping the black ledger. "Three days ago, *The Grey River Syndicate* purchased the complete arrears of the Sovereign Vanguard—forty-eight thousand silver shillings owed to eight hundred legionnaires. As of sunset, every company captain holds a certified draft upon my banking house in the Low Ward, cashable tomorrow morning."

I leaned forward, resting my forearms on the table.

"I pay their wages, Gisela. I pay for the bread their children eat. I pay for the charcoal that warms their barracks. If you order them to draw steel against me, do you truly believe they will march for a commander who gave them pot-metal pikes and four months of empty promises?"

Gisela’s face went dead white. She looked from me to the doors of the council chamber, where the two armored sentries of her own vanguard stood rigid, their eyes fixed straight ahead, utterly refusing to meet her gaze.

"And you, Lady Vivienne," I said, turning my gaze to the trembling churchwoman.

Vivienne dropped to her knees, her white samite robes pooling around her like melted wax, her hands clutching her rosary in a frenzy of terror.

"Alden... please," she whispered, her voice cracking. "In the name of the Light—"

"Do not invoke the Light to a man who holds the invoices for your calf-bones and stolen chalices," I said with cutting disdain. "The Grand Inquisitor was delivered certified transcripts of your southern smuggling ring two hours ago. Even now, thirty episcopal bailiffs are waiting outside the cathedral cloister to seal your reliquaries."

Vivienne let out a wailing sob, collapsing forward until her forehead struck the cold marble flags.

Godfrey looked around the room.

His Grand Marshal was paralyzed with terror. His High Chancellor was weeping on the floor. His Duchess was huddled in her chair like a broken doll. And his Grand Duke was sobbing into his sleeves.

The golden facade was cracking, revealing the hollow, rotten timber beneath.

"What do you want?" Godfrey rasped, his broadsword slowly slipping back into its gilded scabbard with a dull, defeated click. His chest heaved, his handsome face drained of its haughty chivalry, looking suddenly old, gaunt, and bewildered. "Land? Titles? You want your village back? You want Weirs-End?"

I laughed.

It was a quiet, melodic sound, full of genuine amusement, echoing off the high stone vaults of the council hall.

"Weirs-End? A muddy ditch filled with pigs and cabbage patches? My dear Godfrey... how terribly small your mind remains."

I stood up, adjusting the silver mockingbird pin at my lapel, smoothing the charcoal wool of my doublet.

"I do not want Weirs-End. I want everything you possess."

I drew forth a series of parchment warrants, already inscribed in legal Latin, bearing the blank seals of the Sovereign Regency.

"The terms of liquidation are remarkably straightforward," I said, laying the pages before the trembling Grand Duke.

"First: Lord Godfrey Sterling is stripped of all sovereign emergency powers, tourney stipends, and military command. His personal estates, coursers, and armor are attached to satisfy the private creditor claims of *The Grey River Syndicate*."

Godfrey gasped, his hands twitching as if reaching for an invisible weapon.

"Second: Lady Gisela Brandt is relieved of command of the Sovereign Vanguard, pending an official court of inquiry into garrison embezzlement and supply fraud."

"Third: Lady Vivienne Claire is remanded to the custody of the High Inquisitor for the trial of simony and sacrilege."

"And fourth: Duchess Lynette Morrow’s marriage to Lord Godfrey is referred to the Grand Council for immediate annulment, upon the grounds of fraudulent nobility and insolvency."

Lynette screamed—a raw, animal shriek that tore through the chamber—and fainted dead away, her head slipping from the arm of her chair to hit the floor with a hollow thud.

Godfrey did not look at her. He stood there, shivering in his gilded crimson tunic, staring at me as if I were an apparition from the depths of the void.

"You're a demon," he whispered, his lips blue in the dying candlelight. "No man does this with paper."

"A demon?" I murmured, walking around the table until I stood three inches from his chest. I looked up into his handsome, hollow eyes, my smile razor-sharp. "No, Godfrey. A demon takes your soul in exchange for gold. I merely allowed you to spend gold you did not have, until you hung yourself with your own vain appetite."

I patted his armored shoulder with gentle, mocking condescension.

"Tomorrow morning, the audit goes public. The heralds will read the ledgers in every square in Bell-Garde. I suggest you pack your fine clothes tonight, my lord. The bailiffs arrive at dawn."

I turned my back on the council and walked toward the high doors.

As I passed the two Vanguard sentries at the threshold, both men snapped to attention, their halberds striking the marble with a thunderous, loyal salute.

"Good evening, Captains," I said smoothly.

The Sovereign Audit was complete. Now, the execution would begin.
