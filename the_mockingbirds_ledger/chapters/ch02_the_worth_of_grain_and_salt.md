# Chapter 2: The Worth of Grain and Salt

A man who wishes to steal an empire must first learn the price of a bushel of rye.

By midday, the sleet had surrendered to a thick, briny fog that rolled off the Bitter Sea, clinging to the rigging of the merchant cogs like grease upon wool. The Lower Wharf was an acoustic tumult of bawling carters, the rhythmic *thud-clack* of wooden cranes swinging crates of dried ling, and the wet crunch of coarse salt beneath heavy boots.

I spent one silver shilling.

Fifty copper pennies’ worth of change bought me a steaming bowl of mutton broth thick with barley, three hard rolls, a shave at a barber’s stall behind the fishmongers, and a clean, albeit patched, doublet of coarse grey wool from an old clothesman. When I stared into the cracked glass of the barber’s basin, the face that looked back was no longer a corpse.

Alden Croft had possessed an unmemorable countenance: dark, unruly hair, high cheekbones, and grey eyes that had once been liquid with earnest devotion. Now, the gaze was mine. Cold, calm, and slightly hooded. A man who looks at other men not to see their virtue, but to measure the circumference of their necks for an appropriate collar.

I walked the quays, my thumbs tucked lightly into my belt.

Every three hundred yards stood a monument to Lord Godfrey Sterling.

There was a bronze statue of the Hero spearing the Great Behemoth of the Frostmarches, its pedestal inscribed with verses praising his divine valor. There was a fountain carved with nymphs offering golden wheatsheaves to his chiseled likeness. The gilding on the hero’s bronze locks was pristine, catching the wan northern sun.

Directly beneath the fountain, four dockhands in rags were shivering as they unloaded leaking barrels of pickled herring from a battered cog.

"Third week without full coin," one docker muttered, spitting a dark wad of dried leaf onto the stones. "The Assessor says the crown took a surcharge for the Winter Tourney."

"The Hero needs his gold leaf," another sneered, heaving a sixty-pound cask onto his shoulder with a grunt. "Can't have his lance rustin' while we break our backs."

*Lovely,* I thought. *A city ruled by a chivalric peacock who spends gold before it enters the harbor, while the men who move the iron and grain go hungry.*

In King's Landing, Robert Baratheon had played the same tune. Robert would feast on roast swans and tourney champions while I borrowed six million dragons from the Iron Bank and the Lannisters, juggling promissory notes until the kingdom was essentially my private property. A warrior king is the greatest gift God ever gave to an ambitious accountant.

I turned away from the fountain and entered the sovereign customs house.

The hall was drafty, smelling of damp cedar shavings, fish glue, and cheap ink. Dozens of merchants in fur-lined mantles stood in angry clusters, waving bills of lading at harried clerks perched behind long pine desks.

At the center of the dais sat Senior Assessor Mallow.

He was a thick-necked man in late middle age, his jowls resting upon a stained linen ruff, his small piggish eyes squinting through brass spectacles at a stack of sheepskin manifests. A pewter jug of watered wine sat at his elbow, and his fingers were perpetually stained with purple oak-gall ink.

"Next!" Mallow barked, slamming an iron seal into a block of hot wax. "Cog *The Sea-Wife*, out of Wester-Reach. Four tons of salt cod, three casks of pitch. Duty is forty-two silver groats, payable to the High Stewardship."

A merchant with red-rimmed eyes leaned forward. "Forty-two? By the charter, coastal fish is taxed at eight percent, not twelve!"

"The High Duchess Lynette Morrow signed the revised tariff on Tuesday," Mallow said without looking up, his tone a bored droning whine. "Pay the coin or let the cod rot on the jetty. Next!"

The merchant cursed under his breath, counted out the silver, and snatched his stamped parchment.

I did not join the line. I leaned against a fluted cedar pillar twenty paces away, my eyes locked on Mallow's hands.

A novice watches a man's face. A professional watches his ledgers.

Mallow kept two books.

The first was the Great Crown Register—a massive, iron-bound volume bound in pigskin, resting squarely in the center of the desk. When a merchant paid his duty, Mallow dipped his quill into the dark black ink and recorded the sum.

The second was a small, oblong notebook bound in worn brown leather, resting half-hidden beneath a stack of salt-charters to his left.

Whenever a consignment of high-value goods passed his inspection—fine Spanish wool, barrels of fortified Malmsey wine, or crates of refined tallow—Mallow’s quill dipped into a secondary inkpot. A paler ink, slightly watered. He scribbled a note into the brown leather ledger, then stamped the clearance with a second, slightly smaller brass signet kept inside his right sleeve.

It took me precisely forty-five minutes to decipher the arithmetic.

He was recording forty percent of the luxury cargoes as "damaged in transit" or "spoiled by bilge-seepage," claiming the statutory remission under Chapter Four of the Maritime Code, and pocketing the difference in unminted silver ingots delivered to his private carriage behind the warehouse.

A clumsy scam. In Braavos, they would have cut off his ears and nailed them to the harbor gate. In King's Landing, I would have made him an assistant deputy within three days.

I waited until the bells tolled the third hour of the afternoon.

The merchants dispersed as the tide turned, leaving the customs house cold and dim. The clerks blew out their tallow dips and shuffled into the rain, eager for their evening ale.

Only Mallow remained, counting stacks of silver groats into canvas bags, muttering curses as he locked the Great Register with an iron padlock.

I walked silently across the stone flags. My shadow fell over his desk.

"We're closed, beggar," Mallow snarled without raising his chin, tying the neck of a silver bag with hemp twine. "Come back at dawn with your harbor pass, or I'll have the watch toss you into the dock."

"A magnificent trade, salt cod," I said softly, my voice drifting through the empty hall like silk drawn over stone. "Though forty-two groats on four tons seems remarkably harsh, given that Chapter Nine of the Maritime Code allows a three-groat rebate for vessels carrying northern ballast."

Mallow froze. His pen halted mid-air, a fat drop of black ink trembling at the nib before splashing onto his blotter.

He looked up, pushing his brass spectacles up the bridge of his bulbous nose. His eyes flicked from my patched grey doublet to my clean, lean face.

"Who the devil are you?"

"A humble student of numbers, Master Assessor," I said, offering him a gentle, deferential bow. "Alden Croft, formerly of the Weirs-End mariners. I have spent the last hour admiring the extraordinary dexterity of your pen."

"Get out of my office," Mallow said, his voice dropping into a harsh, defensive rasp. He slid his right hand toward an iron bell on the desk.

"If you ring that bell," I said mildly, stepping closer and resting my fingertips on the edge of the pine desk, "the sergeant of the gate will enter. And when he enters, I shall be compelled to ask him why the cargo of the *Merry-Dancer*—two hundred casks of vintage cider cleared at noon—was entered into the Crown Ledger as sixty barrels of sour vinegar."

Mallow’s skin drained of color, turning the shade of curded goat's milk. His hand hovered over the bell, trembling.

"You're mad," he choked out. "The manifests are sealed by the High Stewardship."

"They are sealed by your secondary signet," I said, tapping the edge of the small brown notebook peeking out from beneath the salt-charters. "The brass one with the notched rim that you keep in your right sleeve. A clever device, though using elderberry wash to fade the second carbon leaf is terribly dated. A wet sponge and a pinch of chalk dust will bring the original figures back in thirty seconds."

He stared at me as if I were a ghost risen from the harbor mud.

"Who sent you?" he whispered, sweat breaking out along his receding hairline, gleaming under the tallow lamp. "Was it the Guild of Vintners? The Grand Marshal's bailiffs? I swear by the Light, I have paid my dues to the commander!"

I smiled. It was the smile that had disarmed kings and queens across the Narrow Sea—modest, patient, and infinitely dangerous.

"No one sent me, Master Mallow. The gods, in their infinite whimsy, merely guided my footsteps to your table."

"What do you want? Silver? I have forty groats in this sack—"

"Keep your groats," I said, waving a hand with airy dismissiveness. "Silver in a sack is spent in a week and remembered by no one. What I require is an inkpot, a desk by the south window, and thirty silver shillings advanced against my first month's wage as your chief tally-assistant."

Mallow blinked, his mouth opening and closing like a landed carp.

"My... my assistant?"

"You are drowning, Master Assessor," I said softly, pulling a tall oak stool forward and seating myself with elegant composure. "The Crown’s sovereign deficit is three hundred thousand gold pieces. Duchess Lynette is demanding a twenty percent increase in port revenues by the winter solstice to pay for Lord Godfrey’s new gilded stables. If you continue with this clumsy elderberry wash, her auditors will have your neck in an iron collar before the first snow."

I leaned in, my grey eyes holding his until he averted his gaze.

"I, on the other hand, know how to make three coins appear where two were recorded, and ensure the missing one looks like an unavoidable loss to seasonal damp. You will retain your head, Master Mallow. Your purse will grow heavier. And I shall have a quiet perch from which to observe the trade of Bell-Garde."

He swallowed hard, his Adam's apple bobbing violently. He looked at the little brown notebook, then looked back at my face.

"Thirty shillings," he croaked, reaching into his iron strongbox with a key that shook in the lock. "And... and you balance the timber ledgers for the Vanguard tonight."

"A delightful task," I said, accepting the stack of heavy silver coins with a graceful nod. "Numbers, my dear Mallow, are the only honest creatures in this wretched city. They never pretend to be heroes."
