# Chapter 2: The Worth of Grain and Salt

A man who wishes to steal an empire must first learn the price of a bushel of rye.

By midday, the morning fog had burned away into a pale, watery sunlight that caught the white limestone ramparts of Bell-Garde like salt upon slate. The Lower Wharf was an acoustic tumult of bawling carters, the rhythmic *thud-clack* of wooden cranes swinging crates of dried ling, and the wet crunch of coarse rock salt beneath heavy boots.

I spent four silver shillings of the fee Master Klaus had paid me.

Twenty copper pennies bought me a steaming bowl of mutton broth thick with barley, three crusty rolls, and a shave at a barber’s stall behind the fishmongers. Two silver shillings purchased a clean, tailored doublet of heavy charcoal-grey wool from a deceased merchant’s estate sale, along with a crisp linen collar and a dark woolen cloak that smelled faintly of cedar chips.

When I stared into the cracked bronze mirror at the tailor’s booth, the apparition of the starving beggar had vanished.

Alden Croft had possessed an unmemorable countenance: dark, unruly hair, high cheekbones, and grey eyes that had once been liquid with earnest, dog-like devotion. Now, the gaze was mine. Cold, calm, and slightly hooded. A man who looks at other men not to evaluate their chivalric virtue, but to measure the circumference of their necks for an appropriate financial collar.

I walked the quays, my hands clasped loosely behind my back.

Every three hundred yards stood a monument to Lord Godfrey Sterling.

There was a colossal bronze statue of the Hero spearing the Great Behemoth of the Frostmarches, its pedestal inscribed with heroic hexameters praising his divine purity and unyielding lance. There was an octagonal marble fountain carved with nymphs offering golden wheatsheaves to his sculpted likeness. The gilding on the hero’s bronze curls was immaculate, catching the wan sun.

Directly beneath the fountain, four dockhands in rags were shivering as they unloaded leaking casks of pickled herring from a battered cog.

"Third week without full coin," one docker muttered, spitting a dark wad of dried leaf onto the wet stone flags. "The Assessor says the crown took a surcharge for the Winter Tourney."

"The Hero needs his gold leaf," another sneered, heaving a sixty-pound cask onto his shoulder with a grunt that rattled his ribs. "Can't have his gilded armor dullin' while we break our backs on his bread."

*Delightful,* I thought, a thin, familiar amusement warming my blood. *A realm ruled by a chivalric peacock who spends treasury gold six months before it enters the harbor, while the men who move the iron and grain starve in the mud.*

In King's Landing, Robert Baratheon had played the exact same tune. Robert would feast on roast swans and crown tournament champions while I borrowed six million dragons from the Iron Bank and House Lannister, juggling promissory notes until the Iron Throne was essentially my private freehold. A warrior king is the greatest gift God ever bestowed upon an ambitious financier.

I turned away from the fountain and entered the sovereign customs house.

The hall was drafty, smelling of damp cedar shavings, fish glue, and cheap ink. Dozens of factors in fur-lined mantles stood in angry clusters, waving bills of lading at harried clerks perched behind long pine desks.

At the center of the raised dais sat Senior Assessor Mallow.

He was a thick-necked man in late middle age, his triple jowls resting upon a stained linen ruff, his small piggish eyes squinting through brass spectacles at a stack of sheepskin manifests. A pewter jug of watered cider sat at his elbow, and his fingers were perpetually stained with purple oak-gall ink.

"Next!" Mallow barked, slamming an iron seal into a block of hot wax. "Cog *The Sea-Wife*, out of Wester-Reach. Four tons of salt cod, three casks of pitch. Duty is forty-two silver groats, payable to the High Stewardship."

A merchant with red-rimmed eyes leaned forward over the barrier. "Forty-two? By the municipal charter, coastal fish is taxed at eight percent, not twelve!"

"The High Duchess Lynette Morrow signed the revised luxury tariff on Tuesday," Mallow said without looking up, his tone a bored, nasal drone. "Pay the coin or let the cod rot on the jetty. Next!"

The merchant cursed beneath his breath, counted out the silver, and snatched his stamped parchment.

I did not join the line. I leaned against a fluted cedar pillar twenty paces away, my eyes locked upon Mallow's hands.

A novice watches a man's face. A professional watches his ledgers.

Mallow kept two books.

The first was the Great Crown Register—a massive, iron-bound volume bound in pigskin, resting squarely in the center of the desk. When a common merchant paid his duty on grain or timber, Mallow dipped his quill into the dark black ink and recorded the sum with ponderous strokes.

The second was a small, oblong notebook bound in worn brown calfskin, resting half-hidden beneath a stack of salt-charters to his left.

Whenever a consignment of high-value luxury cargo passed his inspection—fine West-Reach merino wool, casks of fortified Malmsey wine, or crates of refined candle-tallow—Mallow’s quill dipped into a secondary inkpot. A paler ink, slightly watered. He scribbled a note into the brown calfskin ledger, then stamped the clearance with a second, slightly smaller brass signet kept concealed inside his wide wool sleeve.

It took me precisely forty-five minutes to decipher the arithmetic.

He was recording forty percent of the luxury cargoes as "damaged in transit" or "spoiled by bilge-seepage," claiming the statutory remission under Chapter Four of the Maritime Code, and pocketing the difference in unminted silver delivered to his private carriage behind the warehouse.

A clumsy, parochial scam. In Braavos, the Sealord’s bailiffs would have cut off his ears and nailed them to the harbor gate. In King's Landing, I would have used him to buy three city councillors before the month was out.

I waited until the bells tolled the third hour of the afternoon.

The tide turned, and the customs hall cleared. The clerks blew out their tallow candles, locked their cash drawers, and shuffled into the chilly dusk, eager for the warm hearths of the waterfront taverns.

Only Mallow remained, counting stacks of silver groats into canvas bags, muttering curses as he locked the Great Register with an iron padlock.

I walked across the stone flags. My shadow fell long across his desk.

"We're closed, beggar," Mallow snarled without raising his chin, knotting the neck of a silver bag with hemp twine. "Come back at dawn with your harbor pass, or I'll have the gate-watch toss you into the canal."

"A magnificent trade, salt cod," I said softly, my voice drifting through the empty hall like silk drawn over polished marble. "Though forty-two groats on four tons seems remarkably harsh, given that Chapter Nine of the Maritime Code allows a three-groat rebate for vessels carrying northern ballast."

Mallow froze. His pen halted mid-air, a fat drop of black ink trembling at the nib before splashing onto his blotter.

He looked up, pushing his brass spectacles up the bridge of his bulbous nose. His eyes flicked from my tailored charcoal wool coat to my lean, clean-shaven face.

"Who the devil are you?"

"Alden Croft, factor and commercial syndicator," I said, offering him a gentle, deferential incline of my head. "I represent *The Grey River Mercantile Trust*. I have spent the afternoon admiring the extraordinary dexterity of your penmanship, Master Mallow."

"Get out of my hall," Mallow said, his voice dropping into a harsh, defensive rasp. He slid his thick right hand toward an iron bell on the corner of the desk.

"If you ring that bell," I said mildly, resting two fingers upon the edge of the pine desk, "the sergeant of the watch will enter. And when he enters, I shall be compelled to ask him why the cargo of the *Merry-Dancer*—two hundred casks of vintage cider cleared at noon—was entered into the Crown Ledger as sixty barrels of sour vinegar."

Mallow’s skin drained of color, turning the shade of curded goat's milk. His hand hovered over the bell, trembling.

"You're mad," he choked out, his jowls quivering. "The manifests are sealed by the High Stewardship."

"They are sealed by your secondary signet," I said, tapping the edge of the small brown notebook peeking out from beneath the salt-charters. "The brass one with the notched rim that you keep inside your right sleeve. A clever device, though using elderberry wash to fade the second carbon leaf is terribly dated. A wet sponge and a pinch of chalk dust will bring the original figures back in thirty seconds."

He stared at me as if I were a demon conjured from the salt marshes.

"Who sent you?" he whispered, sweat breaking out along his receding hairline, gleaming beneath the tallow lamp. "Was it the Guild of Vintners? The Grand Duke's auditors? I swear by the Light, I have paid my dues to the court!"

"No one sent me, Master Mallow," I replied with a patient, tranquil smile. "The gods, in their infinite whimsy, merely guided my footsteps to your table."

"What do you want?" Mallow croaked, his hands clutching a heavy bag of coin against his chest. "Silver? I have forty groats in this sack—take it and leave Bell-Garde!"

"Keep your groats," I said, waving a hand with airy dismissiveness. "A sack of silver is spent in a week and forgotten by Monday. I have no interest in your petty pocket-money, Master Mallow. Nor do I have any desire to sit behind a pine desk dipping a quill for a clerk’s pittance."

Mallow blinked, his mouth opening and closing like a landed carp. "Then... what in the Light's name do you want from me?"

"I want what your department considers worthless," I said softly, drawing a folded sheet of parchment from my coat.

"Three months ago, the municipal bailiffs seized the old imperial basalt cistern near the Coal Sump—*The Drowned Cellar*—for non-payment of fifty sovereigns in crown arrears. The lease reverted to the Sovereign Customs Department as delinquent salvage. It sits in your strongbox, gathering dust and mildew, while the city levies an annual maintenance penalty against your ledger."

Mallow stared at me, his piggish eyes narrowing with dawning bewilderment. "The cistern? It's flooded knee-deep in canal sludge! The boiler trusses are rotted through! No sane man would pay three copper bits for that waterlogged tomb!"

"Then you will be delighted to dispose of it," I said, leaning forward, my grey eyes holding his with hypnotic stillness.

"You will draw up a deed of absolute freehold assignment under Chapter Six of the Solvency Statutes, transferring the lease of the cistern and the adjoining cellar to *The Grey River Mercantile Trust*. You will record the acquisition as an emergency drainage redemption, discharging all prior crown tax liens."

I placed ten silver shillings—cold, unclipped, and heavy—upon his blotting paper.

"Ten shillings for the municipal registry fee. And in return, Master Mallow, your little brown notebook remains safely hidden beneath your salt-charters. Better still, I shall teach you how to disguise your ledger skims using amortized shrinkage formulas from the Free Cities, so Duchess Lynette's auditors never suspect a single coin is missing."

Mallow swallowed hard, his Adam's apple bobbing violently. He looked down at the ten shillings, then looked at the cold, unyielding iron in my gaze.

"And... that is all?" he asked, his voice shaking.

"Not quite," I smiled. "From this day forth, whenever a shipment of foreign bullion, sovereign debt warrants, or luxury imports clears this dock, a duplicate manifest will be delivered to my door before it reaches the High Citadel. You will remain Senior Assessor, Master Mallow. You will grow richer. You will retain your neck. But you belong to me."

He sat frozen for three long heartbeats, listening to the wind rattle the heavy oak shutters of the customs house.

Then, with fingers that trembled like aspen leaves, he reached into his drawer, drew out a blank parchment with the municipal seal, and uncorked his iron pot of ink.

"The deed will be registered by sunset," he whispered.

"A pleasure doing business with a man of figures," I said, taking the signed and sealed parchment from his desk and sliding it into my breast pocket. "Numbers, my dear Mallow, are the only honest creatures in this wretched city. They never pretend to be heroes."

I bowed gracefully, turned upon my heel, and stepped out into the crisp evening air, the deed to my new kingdom resting against my heart.
