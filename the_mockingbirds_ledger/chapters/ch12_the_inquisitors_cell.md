# Chapter 12: The Inquisitor's Cell

A church is a fortress of righteousness until someone opens the cellar doors and finds the silver.

The sub-dungeon beneath the Cathedral of the Radiant Dawn was not an ordinary gaol. It was the *Well of Penitence*—a series of damp limestone cisterns carved twenty cubits into the bedrock beneath the crypts, where the air was permanently stale, reeking of wet lime, unwashed wool, and the bitter, metallic tang of rusted iron chains. Water seeped perpetually through the vaulted mortar, dripping with a rhythmic, hollow *plink... plink... plink* into stagnant puddles upon the floor.

A single tallow lantern swung from an iron bracket outside cell seven, casting wavering yellow light across the iron-barred wicket door.

Behind the bars sat Vivienne Claire.

The starched wimple of the High Chancellor had been stripped from her brow. Her silver-blonde hair hung in greasy, tangled strands across her hollow cheeks, matted with bits of rotten straw. She wore a coarse, undyed tunic of burlap that scratched her delicate skin until it bled, her wrists bound by heavy iron cuffs linked to a ring-bolt set into the bedrock.

On the damp stone beside her pallet sat an earthenware bowl containing grey water and three floating scraps of turnip skin—the prison swill of a condemned heretic.

I paused at the iron grate, holding a small brass hand-lamp.

"The accommodations seem somewhat lacking for the Keeper of the Holy Relics, Lady Vivienne," I said softly, my voice slicing through the gloom.

Vivienne gasped. She scrambled across the damp straw on her knees, the iron chains rattling violently against the stone floor. She pressed her face against the cold, rusted bars, her pale blue eyes wide with feverish, manic hope.

"Master Croft!" she breathed, her voice a desperate, rasping croak. "Oh, thank the Light! I knew you would come! You received my message?"

I reached into my charcoal coat and drew forth a scrap of stained linen—a note smuggled through a bribed gaoler, scribbled in charcoal dust: *Save me. I will sign anything. Everything.*

"I received it," I said mildly, setting the brass hand-lamp upon a stone ledge outside the cell.

"The Inquisitor is a madman!" she sobbed, tears cutting pale channels through the grime on her cheeks. "He tore through my private apartments! He seized the caskets from Val-Rhea! He says I am to be degraded before the High Altar on Sunday, stripped naked, and branded with the iron of the blasphemer! They're going to burn me, Alden!"

She thrust her trembling, bruised hands through the iron bars, reaching desperately for the hem of my cloak.

"You have the gold," she wept, her fingers clawing at the fine West-Reach wool. "The Inquisitor owes your syndicate eight hundred sovereigns! Bribe him! Buy the gaolers! Arrange a boat to take me across the Bitter Sea! I know all of Godfrey’s secrets! I know where he hid the southern tribute! I can give you the names of every bishop who took bribes from the crown!"

I looked down at her hands. I did not pull away, nor did I step closer. I remained perfectly still, watching her unravel with the clinical detachment of a physician observing a rotting limb.

"You are very generous with other people’s property, Vivienne," I remarked quietly.

"I will be generous with myself!" she cried, pressing her body against the iron bars, her voice sinking into an urgent, frantic, seductive whisper. "You loved me once, Alden! Don't look away from me! When we were novices in the south, before Godfrey... you used to look at me by the campfire. I saw the way your hands shook whenever you handed me bread! You thought I was holy! You thought I was an angel!"

A hollow, mocking smile touched my lips.

"Alden Croft was a twenty-year-old virgin who had never seen an unclad woman outside of a village bathhouse," I said. "He thought any girl who prayed with her eyes closed was an angel. A remarkably common affliction among hedge-swordsmen."

"I can be whatever you want, Alden!" she pleaded, her voice rising in pitch, her fingers gripping my lapel so hard the cloth strained against the seams. "I am young! My skin is soft! Take me to a villa in the south! I will lie beneath you every night! I will worship you like a god! Only don't leave me in this dark hole!"

I reached up and gently, finger by finger, peeled her dirty hands from my cloak.

I drew a clean linen handkerchief from my pocket and wiped the dust from my velvet collar, folding the cloth neatly before returning it to my pocket.

"You speak of love, Vivienne," I said, leaning in until my face was framed by the iron bars, the candlelight illuminating the cold, dark amusement in my grey eyes. "Yet my memory of you is somewhat different."

Vivienne froze, her breath hitching in her throat.

"I remember a night in a peat mine near the Weirs-End border," I murmured, my voice as soft and lethal as silk dragged over an open wound. "The roof-timbers gave way with a sound like thunder. Two tons of wet peat and slate crushed your chest. The water was rising to your chin. The other men fled, screaming that the tunnel was cursed."

A shudder tore through her frame. Her eyes widened, the feverish hope draining away, replaced by an agonizing, paralyzing dread.

"Alden Croft dug you out with his bare hands," I said. "He broke four fingernails to the quick. He tore the ligaments in his left shoulder lifting the oak beam off your ribs. When he pulled you into the moonlight, you were coughing black mud, and he gave you his only woolen cloak to keep you from freezing."

Vivienne dropped her forehead against the iron bars, weeping silently.

"And then," I continued, my voice dipping into a lower, harsher register, "two years later, in *The Boar’s Tusk*, you sat beside Godfrey Sterling in your fine white wimple, drinking wine paid for with my bounty coin. And when Godfrey kicked me into the dirt, you smiled. You told the room that the Light demands strength. You told the room that I was dead weight, holding back your glorious destiny."

"Godfrey forced me..." she choked out, her voice muffled against the cold iron.

"Godfrey did not force you to laugh, Vivienne," I said mercilessly. "Godfrey did not force you to whisper into Lynette’s ear that Alden was a pathetic dog who deserved to starve. You did that because you were vain, greedy, and utterly devoid of a soul. You thought you were climbing a ladder of gold."

I reached for the brass hand-lamp on the ledge.

"Alden Croft died in the alley because he believed your tears were holy," I said, looking into her terrified, upturned face. "I, on the other hand, am Lord Petyr Baelish. And in my world, women who trade their virtue for silver are kept in brothels, not cathedrals."

Vivienne’s jaw dropped. "Petyr... who is Petyr? What are you saying?"

"It matters remarkably little who I was, my dear," I whispered, holding the lamp before my lips. "What matters is who you are. A convicted thief. A sacrilegious fraud. A creature whose holy relics were nothing more than boiled calf-bones and stolen chalices."

"No!" she shrieked, clawing at the bars once more as I took a step backward into the corridor. "Alden! Don't leave me! Don't blow it out! Please, by the gods, don't leave me in the dark!"

I smiled.

"The Light demands strength, sister," I purred, repeating her own sacred words with exquisite, venomous precision. "Holding back my destiny is a sin of pride."

I drew a sharp breath and blew upon the wick.

The flame died with a tiny hiss of smoke.

The sub-dungeon plunged into absolute, suffocating blackness.

The only sounds remaining were the steady, indifferent *plink... plink... plink* of the dripping ceiling water, and the shrieking, hysterical sobs of High Chancellor Vivienne Claire, clawing at the stone floor in the dark.

I turned on my heel and walked up the stairs toward the fresh air of the street, guided by memory and the quiet song of the mockingbird.
