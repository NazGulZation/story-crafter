# Chapter 3: The Brazen Quill

A brothel is an academy of human frailty, but a bathhouse is a church of unvarnished truth.

In King’s Landing, my establishments were legendary for their velvet alcoves, their perfumed girls, and the boys with hair like beaten copper. Men entered thinking they were seeking pleasure; they left having laid bare the secrets of their houses, their treasuries, and their king. When a man’s boots are off and his skin is slick with lavender oil, his tongue loosens with remarkable alacrity.

In Bell-Garde, I did not possess the gold for velvet and myrrh. Not yet. But the Canal Quarter was damp, filthy, and populated by thousands of men whose bones ached with the northern chill.

Two streets down from the Lower Wharves stood the remains of the Old Imperial basalt cisterns—a squat, vaulted stone structure built of dark basalt, known to the locals as *The Drowned Cellar*. It had been abandoned three years prior when an overflow from the canal flooded the boiler room. The municipal bailiffs had nailed an iron notice to the rotting oak door: *Forfeited for Unpaid Crown Levies — Forty Gold Sovereigns or Best Offer.*

Forty sovereigns was what the Crown demanded on parchment. In reality, the building was a damp tomb collecting rat carcasses and stagnant sludge.

I did not offer forty sovereigns.

I went to the Municipal Court of Solvency with Master Mallow’s sealed deed of assignment in my coat and the gold sovereigns from Master Klaus safely secured in my belt. The court clerk was an elderly, wheezing wreck named Hemlock, whose wool sleeve was worn through at the elbows.

"The customs department has formally assigned the delinquent tax lien to *The Grey River Mercantile Trust*," I told Hemlock, placing four silver shillings upon his greasy desk. "The basement is knee-deep in canal sewage, and the cedar trusses are black with dry rot. No sane merchant will touch it. Yet, if the court ratifies the transfer and certifies the five-year tax exemption for drainage restoration under the statutory decree, I shall relieve you of the nuisance and pay seven silver groats into the municipal relief chest."

Hemlock stared at the four shillings. His tongue wet his dry, cracked lower lip.

"Seven groats into the city chest," he rasped, his hand sliding across the oak desk to swallow the silver. "And a modest fee of three shillings for the court seal registering the freehold to... what name did you say?"

"The Grey River Mercantile Trust," I replied with a warm, self-effacing bow. "Represented by their humble factor, Alden Croft."

By nightfall, the iron padlock had been shattered with a cold chisel.

Within three weeks, *The Brazen Quill* was born.

It took labor, and in Bell-Garde, labor was cheaper than turnips. The streets were swarming with the discarded debris of Godfrey Sterling’s glorious wars: widows of pikemen who had frozen on the northern borders, girls who had washed soldiers' cloaks for crusts of moldy bread, and orphaned boys who lived like ferrets in the coal barges.

I did not treat them with chivalric pity. Pity is a currency that buys nothing and breeds resentment.

I treated them with coin and discipline.

"Two copper pennies a day for scrubbing timber and shoveling out the canal silt," I told the twenty ragged women gathered before the cistern steps on my second morning. The wind was howling down the alley, reeking of low-tide mud and horse offal. They shivered in their thin woolen shawls, looking at my clean grey coat with sullen, suspicious eyes.

"Two pennies is what the carters get," an older woman with a scarred cheek muttered. "We want bread."

"You will receive two pennies and a bowl of hot barley porridge at dawn," I said, my voice cutting cleanly through the gale. "And for every piece of parchment, every whispered conversation, and every unsealed dispatch you recover from the clothes you wash or the rooms you clean, you shall receive an additional three silver pennies."

Silence settled over the steps.

"Scraps of parchment?" a young girl with hollow cheeks asked, clutching a broom made of tied heather twigs.

"Parchment," I repeated, smiling gently. "Lords and merchants are careless creatures. When they drink, they leave notes in their doublets. When they quarrel, they throw letters into hearths that fail to catch fire. Bring me those scraps. Tell me which captain visits which widow, which guildmaster is selling his silver plate, and which clerk in the High Citadel is weeping into his ale over an unbalanced audit."

I reached into my pocket and tossed a silver shilling into the girl’s apron.

"A deposit on your future diligence, my dear. What is your name?"

"Nesta, sir," she whispered, clutching the silver to her breast as if it were a holy icon.

"Work diligently, Nesta. In this house, silence is prized, but curiosity is rewarded."

The repairs proceeded with astonishing speed.

I hired two drunken masons to rebuild the fire flues beneath the stone floor, piping hot water from the city aqueduct into three sunken basalt cisterns lined with planed pine. Above the bathhouse, on the ground floor, I established the tavern: twelve sturdy oak tables, a stone hearth burning dried peat, and barrels of dark, unadulterated spruce beer procured on credit from an indebted brewer in the East Ward.

There was no music. No boisterous bards singing grand ballads of Lord Godfrey’s heroic charge against the Frost Behemoth. Men who wish to shout go to *The Boar’s Tusk*. Men who wish to whisper come to *The Brazen Quill*.

The private alcoves were curtained with thick, oiled wool that muffled voices from the main hall. Beneath the floorboards, I had the masons leave narrow acoustic flues—hollow terra-cotta drainage pipes that connected the ceiling of the private booths directly to the small, windowless counting-room behind my desk.

By the second month, *The Brazen Quill* was yielding sixteen silver shillings a night in ale and steam-bath fees.

More importantly, the pipes were singing.

Every evening, I sat in my high-backed chair beneath a single tallow candle, dipping my goose quill into iron-gall ink, recording the whispers that floated up from the terra-cotta flues.

*Flue Three: Second Captain Donald of the Sovereign Vanguard, speaking between groans as Nesta scrubbed the salt from his muscular back.*

"...the iron is brittle rubbish," Donald’s voice echoed, hollow and distinct through the clay pipe. "The new pike-heads from the East Foundry cracked during drill yesterday. Grand Marshal Gisela knows it, but she can't cancel the contract. The foundry belongs to her uncle, Lord Brandt. If the Duke finds out the Vanguard is armed with pot-metal, Gisela's head will be on a pike before midwinter."

I smiled, my quill scratching across the parchment. *Gisela’s iron. A charming vulnerability.*

*Flue One: Master Orlo, a clerk from the High Court of Chancellor Vivienne Claire, drinking spiced wine with a courier from the southern ports.*

"...three crates of liturgical silver cleared the chapel gate at midnight," Orlo whispered nervously, his cup clattering against the pine table. "Banded in lead, marked as *Holy Relics of Saint Jude*. But they weren't bones, I swear it. They sounded like church plate. Lady Vivienne signed the transit pass herself. She has a debt of four thousand sovereigns with the Genoese bankers that comes due on the feast of Saint Michael."

I paused, blowing softly upon the wet ink. *Vivienne's church plate. An arch-chancellor who steals from her own altars to pay her debts.*

And from the city above, the church bells of the High Citadel began to chime the eighth hour of the evening.

Seven grand chimes, rolling across the roofs of Bell-Garde, announcing the curfew of the poor and the beginning of the feast for the golden heroes on the cliffs.

I stood up, walked to the narrow slit of a window overlooking the canal, and looked up at the glittering lights of the High Keep.

Godfrey Sterling was up there, probably laughing with a woman’s hand in his lap, secure in the belief that the world belonged to men who swung heavy swords and possessed golden curls.

"A beautiful castle," I whispered, lifting my cup of dark spruce beer in a silent, solitary toast. "Such a pity about the foundations."
