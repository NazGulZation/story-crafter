# Chapter 4: The Golden Hero's Appetite

No beast on earth devours wealth with greater ferocity than a chivalric hero in search of an audience.

The "Festival of the Sun-Gilded Lance" was held upon the high chalk meadows north of the Citadel. It was an offensive display of luxury, orchestrated with the delicate subtlety of a war hammer.

The air smelled richly of charred fatback from twenty spit-roasted oxen, almond paste crushed into sugar subtleties shaped like the radiant sun, horse manure, and the dry, powdery stench of crushed chalk dust kicked up by three hundred courser steeds. Brass clarions blared every twenty minutes, deafening the common folk who pressed against the rope barriers, their eyes wide with bovine adoration as the knights in gilded armor paraded before them.

I stood in the pavilion of the foreign factors, wearing a dark wool cloak trimmed with sheared otter. Not fine enough to draw the eye of a royal bailiff, but sufficiently respectable to warrant a seat among the creditors who watched the spectacle with grim, tightening jaws.

Below us, in the grand lists, Lord Godfrey Sterling was preparing for his tilt.

He was magnificent. There was no denying it. The man was constructed entirely for the eye. His armor was not common plate; it was steel washed in bright electrum, chasing the sunlight with a blinding, dazzling glare. A crest of golden eagle feathers streamed from his bascinet, and across his broad chest blazed the heraldry of the Gilded Lion.

Beside him rode his squire, bearing four lances painted in carmine and gold.

"Behold the Savior!" the herald’s voice bellowed across the grounds, magnified by brass horns. "Lord Godfrey Sterling, Champion of the High Keep, Vanquisher of the Northern Terror, Protector of the Faithful!"

The rabble cheered until their throats tore. They threw dried flowers and woolen caps into the dust.

Godfrey raised his electrum-plated visor, revealing his square, chiseled face. He smiled—a brilliant, practiced flash of white teeth—and tossed a handful of silver groats into the crowd. The peasants scrambled on their hands and knees, clawing at each other in the chalk mud for the coins, while Godfrey laughed, reining his great white stallion in a theatrical pirouette.

"Look at him," a voice murmured to my left.

It was Master Corban, an elderly Ost-Reach banking factor with yellowed parchment skin and ink-stained knuckles.

"Ten thousand gold sovereigns on pavilions alone," Corban wheezed, shaking his head until his black velvet skullcap slipped. "The pavilions are hung with Ost-Reach tapestry. The horses are shod in silver. And who pays the smiths? The Crown Treasury! He hasn't won a war in five years, yet he spends as if the River Sunder flowed with molten bullion."

"A hero must maintain his luster, Master Corban," I said mildly, sipping spiced wine from a pewter cup. The wine was acidic, masking its cheap vintage with cinnamon and nutmeg. "A rusty savior inspires very little credit."

"Credit?" Corban scoffed, spitting into the straw at his feet. "His credit is pig manure! I hold twenty-two thousand sovereigns in crown promissory warrants signed by the Grand Duke and Duchess Lynette. They were due at Michaelmas. When I presented them to the Exchequer on Tuesday, that silver-haired witch Lady Vivienne smiled through her wimple and told me the money had been diverted to 'sacred festival contingencies.' She offered me sixty percent in tax-credit vouchers redeemable three years from now! I have three merchant vessels waiting in the bay that cannot pay their crews!"

"A sixty percent reduction is remarkably steep," I observed, keeping my voice gentle, sympathetic, almost sorrowful. "The markets must be severely alarmed."

"Alarmed? The merchant banking houses are fleeing! Half the factors on the High Wharf are selling their crown bonds at fifty cents on the sovereign just to cover their shipping margins!"

My fingers curled around my cup. *Fifty cents on the sovereign.*

In King's Landing, when King Robert's tourney debts overwhelmed the treasury, I had done the same. The Lannisters lent gold; the Iron Bank lent gold; the Faith lent gold. The crown lived on promises, and as long as the music played, nobody looked beneath the floorboards. But when a creditor smells blood, paper becomes as cheap as birch bark.

"What a tragedy for commerce," I murmured, leaning closer to Corban. "It breaks the heart to see an honorable factor ruined by courtly vanity."

Corban glared at me through his rheumy eyes. "You're with that new syndicate, aren't you? The Grey River Trust?"

"I am merely their factor, Master Corban. A humble man of numbers."

"Your trust has been buying up timber contracts in the East Ward. Do they buy debt?"

"Debt is a dangerous enterprise, my good sir," I said, offering him a cautious, hesitating smile. "The Crown is powerful. A private factor cannot seize the High Citadel if Lord Godfrey chooses to default."

"They won't default!" Corban hissed, grabbing my sleeve with trembling, bony fingers. "The Duke cannot afford an embargo! If the northern shipping halts, Bell-Garde starves by winter! Listen to me, Croft. I have fourteen thousand sovereigns in short-term debentures signed by Duchess Lynette. Pledged against the royal salt-pans of Weirs-End. I will give them to you for seven thousand in hard silver coin. Tomorrow morning. At the customs wharf."

I allowed myself to pause, looking out over the lists.

Down below, Godfrey was taking his lance. On the royal viewing dais sat Duchess Lynette Morrow.

She was dressed in a gown of heavy emerald damask, her throat encircled by a collar of irregular teardrop freshwater pearls that rose and fell with her breath. She looked radiant, distant, and intensely proud—the very image of an imperial duchess.

Yet I had seen the reports from Nesta that morning.

Lynette had spent two hours screaming at her tirewoman because the crow's feet around her hazel eyes were beginning to show through the pearl-powder. Godfrey had spent the previous evening in the tent of a seventeen-year-old countess from the southern marches, and Lynette had drunk half a flagon of poppy wine to stop her hands from shaking.

A milkmaid playing at queen, desperate to believe the lie that bought her soul.

"Seven thousand sovereigns in silver is a great deal of liquid bullion, Master Corban," I said, my gaze returning to the old merchant. "My masters would assume tremendous risk. If the salt-pans fail..."

"They won't fail! The brine wells are perennial!"

"Five thousand," I said softly.

Corban blinked, his face twitching. "Five? That is... that is less than forty percent of the face value! It is robbery!"

"It is hard silver, delivered to your warehouse within two hours of signing," I replied with an icy, tranquil smile. "Or you may take your parchment to Lady Vivienne's cathedral and see if her holy relics will buy grain for your sailors."

The brass clarions sounded again.

Down in the lists, Godfrey charged. The thunder of hooves shook the wooden grandstand. The champion’s electrum lance struck his opponent squarely in the breastplate with a deafening *CRACK* of splintering ash. The opposing knight flew from the saddle, tumbling through the chalk dust like a sack of broken armor.

The crowd roared in ecstatic delirium. Godfrey raised his lance, basking in the sea of adulation, his golden curls catching the sunlight as he took off his helm.

Master Corban looked from the roaring crowd down to the dirt, his shoulders slumping in defeat.

"Five thousand," the old man croaked, the fight draining from his eyes. "At noon tomorrow. Behind the customs house."

"A pleasure doing business with an honest man," I said smoothly, inclining my head.

I rose from the bench and walked toward the exit of the pavilion, the cheer of the tourney fading into the background.

Godfrey Sterling had won a ribbon of silk and the screams of three thousand starving peasants.

I had just purchased the salt rights to his wife's ancestral home for forty coppers on the gold piece.

The ladder was growing taller, one ledger at a time.
